# Prologue: The Agni Kai

The tea on the council table had gone cold while the maps were still being argued over, and Jake, who had poured it an hour earlier, stood against the back wall with the empty pot and tried to be furniture.

It was the only trick he had ever had. If he did not move and did not speak and kept his hands folded on the pot, the room stopped counting him, and men said things in front of him that they would not have said to a boy listening. He had heard a great deal that way over thirteen years. He had never once been asked about it, which was the whole design.

Below the war gallery the courtyard had been swept for the duel. Servants had done it that afternoon: sanded the circle, banked the braziers along the ring, filled the shallow stone basin in the alcove by the west door. That basin Jake had filled himself at first light, because that was his hour and his basin. Now the sand was white as bone again under torchlight, and the men on the maps upstairs had begun to raise their voices.

Prince Zuko's voice carried best of all.

Jake heard it break upward through the floor: a boy's pitch in a room of grown men, arguing with men who outranked him.

"The new division goes in first," a general said from somewhere above the joists. "Untried men. The Earth Kingdom line will spend itself on them, and the veterans walk through the gap."

"They are not a gap," Prince Zuko said. "They are boys from the colonies who have never held a spear. You would spend them like coals and call it a plan."

There was laughter after it, brief and respectful, the kind that closed a door. Then the Fire Lord spoke, and the laughter went out of the room entirely.

A runner came to the doorway. He looked past Jake, as runners always did, and spoke to the steward instead.

"The prince has insulted the war council," the runner said. "The Fire Lord has called an Agni Kai, and the gallery is to attend."

An Agni Kai was a fire duel. Every child in the palace knew the shape of it: two benders in a swept circle, no weapons, no armor, the court at the rail to witness. It settled an insult the way a debt was settled, in front of everyone, and it ended when one of the two could not get up. Jake had seen two, both between officers, both over before he had understood what he was watching. He had never seen one called on a prince.

Jake filled the pot. He did not remember deciding to.

---

They lined the rail above the courtyard: generals, wives, clerks, servants with trays. A mask of hammered brass lay on the stand at the ring's edge, and Prince Zuko came out of the doorway into the torchlight and stopped in front of it. His own mask hung on his arm. He was thirteen and had always passed for older, tall for his year and already built through the shoulders, and he stood looking at the brass mask for longer than the moment wanted.

Then he set his own mask down and turned to the doorway without it.

Jake, at the back of the rail with the pot, saw his face go smooth and bare. He saw Zuko look up at the gallery, across it really, to the tall doors on the far side where the Fire Lord would come through.

"You will not fight me," said a voice from those doors. It did not rise. It did not need to. "You will fight me, Prince Zuko, or you will learn what a prince is who refuses his father."

"I won't fight you." Zuko's voice had cracked on it in the war room once already; he held it whole now. "I said what I said at the council table, and I meant it. Punish me for the words if you mean to punish me, but I won't brawl for you in the sand like a—" He stopped, and finished smaller. "I won't fight you."

"Then you will learn respect," said the Fire Lord, "and suffering will be your teacher."

The men came through the doorway robed in gold, and his father walked out onto the sand with the torchlight running up the sleeves of him. Zuko did not take up the guard his master had taught him, not properly. He stood with his arms loose at his sides, mouth working once, and whatever he saw on his father's face made him stop working it.

Jake watched the fire gather down the long line of the Fire Lord's arm.

There was no order given. There was no bell. The blast took Zuko across the shoulder and the side of the face and drove him down in the sand with his arms up, still holding them up, and the gallery made a sound like a cloth being torn. Jake heard himself make no sound at all. His hands were cold on the pot. The cold ran up his arms into his chest and kept going, and he was thinking, quite calmly, that Zuko's hands were open, they were open and empty in the sand, and that he was not going to do anything else.

Jake set the pot on the rail. He went over it.

He did not count the guards or the drop or the fire. His boots hit the sand and he was across the ring in four strides. Behind him the basin in the alcove stood up out of its stone and came with him in a single flat sheet, all the morning's water. It hung between him and the torchlight, green with basin-stain, like a lid being raised.

For half a breath he was holding a roof of water over the prince's body, and his arms were up and his hands were open and flat beneath it, palm to the sky, and it was the easiest thing he had ever done.

Then the fire came down on it anyway.

The water took the force and the force went through the water, because there was too much fire and not enough of it, and what survived boiled. The steam came down onto his uplifted palms in one scalding sheet. It took the whole underside of both his hands at once, palm and thumb and out every fingertip, as if the fire had gripped him through and shaken. Jake screamed at the ceiling of sky he had built and the basin's water broke apart and fell on him and the sand and the prince in a warm dirty rain.

He was still standing when the guards came off the rail, because he had gone stupid on his feet the way a struck animal does, with his hands curled up against his chest and the skin of them sliding. A man beside the ring shouted it before the others took him down.

"Waterbender!"

It did not belong to him.

He was a boy who had carried water to a basin at first light, and somebody had mistaken the water for him. While he was still trying to find the error, the guards had his arms and were hauling him backwards out of the torchlight.

A servant with a tray of plates came out of the west door and stopped dead on the threshold and never moved again. The last thing Jake saw before they took him around the wall was the braziers, the white circle, and Zuko on his side in the sand with his good arm working under him, turning his burned face away from the gallery.

At the rail's end his sister stood with her arms folded, watching the wet stone rather than the boy being dragged out of it. Jake could not tell what she had made of anything at all.

---

They put him in a room off the laundry with a grate for a window and did not come back for two days.

Jake spent them being useful about not understanding. He made the bed they had not given him. He counted the floor stones. He worked his hands over the wash bucket, palms up, because the linen had gone stiff with salt and the skin under it was hot. The water below them rose an inch and hung there, holding to the shape of his palms. He did not lower them into it. When the sting had eased he set his arms at his sides, and the water lay back into the bucket, and that was the whole of it, and he did not try to make anything else happen.

The burns were the plainest part. The skin had gone tight and shining across both palms and wept through the linen it had been wrapped in by some orderly who had not looked at him while doing it. Blistered and cooling, they looked, to Jake, exactly like what they were: he had held fire above his head and his hands were open at the time. He turned them over in the lamplight and memorized them and made no connection he could say out loud. A man's face came into his head instead: Zuko's father's arm going down the length of the ring. He made himself stop thinking it.

Through the grate on the second morning he watched guards cross the yard to the servants' wing in twos. Two of them came back out with a woman and a man in unmarked kitchen gray, hands not bound but very still, going where they were pointed. The guards had gone in through the door Jake had left by every day of his life.

A steward came at noon with a folded list and stood in the corridor reading it rather than looking at him. Names on the list. Hours. A question about which rooms to keep, which to seal. The names were asked in a tone that assumed they were guilty of the price of a boy, which was a sentence Jake turned over eleven times in one afternoon until it stopped meaning anything.

Jake did the only thing left in his kit. When the steward came back past the grate, he asked it small and early, already half-apologizing.

"Will the rooms need the winter blankets aired? September damp gets into wool, and no one will think of it now."

The steward looked at him for the first time. The look lasted long enough to frighten him, and then the man wrote something on his list and went away.

The third morning the lock came off and the door opened onto a hall Jake had been in four times in his life when he was delivering something.

Uncle Iroh stood in the doorway.

He said Jake's name the way he said everyone's name, as though it were a cup he was setting down carefully.

"The Fire Lord has settled the matter of the boy who interrupted his Agni Kai," Iroh said. "You will consider it settled the same way: say nothing of it to anyone. Not to me. Not to the prince."

"Where am I to go?" Jake asked.

"You are assigned to the prince's household," Iroh said. "Going north. You will want your coat."

---

"By the Fire Lord's command," the herald read from the terrace steps, "the prince's topknot is cut, and the prince goes north, and does not come back."

They cut the prince's topknot in the fountain court at noon, in front of the staff that had not already been dismissed. Zuko knelt with his chin up while the fire-knife took it, and his face was swathed to the hairline in linen, and the hair fell on the stones between his knees. Nobody spoke. Somewhere behind Jake a woman was crying with her mouth shut, which is a sound you feel in your own teeth. He spent the ceremony hunting for her in the crowd instead of watching, because hunting was bearable and watching was not.

"The two from the kitchen were taken before dawn," a woman on the dismissed line said, without turning her head. "Nobody is to speak of it."

He did not see Haru. He did not see Emi. He stood at the back with a bundle of his own in both bandaged hands and listened for their two voices with his whole attention. The ceremony ended, the servants were turned out through the side gate, and they were not in it.

He had one thing in the bundle that was his and nothing else besides: a braided cord charm Haru had pressed into his hand the week before, when everything had still been ordinary, meant as a fairing for nothing at all. Jake had been carrying it in his sleeve for eight days. It was the stupidest object he owned and he never once considered leaving it with the winter blankets.

At the docks the ship stood taller than anything in his life had ever been. Crew moved past him in pairs with crates and did not look at the burned-handed boy at the prince's shoulder, and a bosun who did look had his wrist taken by Iroh without any shift of expression at all, and did not look again.

"I'm not on the manifest," Jake said, half way up the gangway, because it had only just occurred to him.

"You are on it now," Iroh said, and did not slow down.

On the deck, out of the crowd's road, Iroh sat him down on a coil of rope and put his hands in his lap and worked the wraps off them with fingers that were quicker than Jake had ever seen them be. The palms shone and tightened in the sea wind and Jake's whole body arched at the cold of it and he did not make a sound, which cost him more than the sound would have.

"These are plain, and they are ugly," Iroh said, and drew a pair of plain ugly gloves out of his sleeve. He put them on Jake's hands one at a time, over new linen, taking his time about it.

"Leather keeps the linen where linen wants to go, and the sun does not touch what the leather is over. You will wear them, and they will fit, and that is the end of the subject."

Where the prince was, Jake could see from the rope: Zuko at the forward rail with his bandaged head between his shoulders, alone in a lane the crew opened and closed around him like water round a stone. Nobody approached him. Nobody met his eyes. The topknot's hair had been swept off the court stones and there would be no box of it anywhere on this ship. And Jake understood with a lurch: he had been given something to do with his hands at exactly the moment the last thing he knew how to do had been cut off the prince in public.

The lines came off the bollards. The ship's bell rang twice and the shore began to move.

Jake stood at the aft rail in his new gloves with his bundle against his chest and watched the harbor lights go out one catch at a time. The customs lamps, then the forge row, then the twin fires on the palace mole that had not gone out in his lifetime. Between him and the stones the wake closed over itself in a long slow swallow, black into black.

The island put its shoulder between them and the last of the fires went.

Behind him the deck was being holystoned over the place where a prince's hair had never fallen, and ahead of him there was open water, and the water did not once stand up.
