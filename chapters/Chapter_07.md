# Chapter 7: The Sandal Trail

The forest smelled like something that had never been burned.

Jake noticed it first as a lack. Three days of coal smoke had settled into his coat, his hair, the linen under his gloves, and he had stopped smelling it somewhere off the rig. Now the trees stood between the camp and the sea wind, and the air that came through them was pine resin and wet bark and the cold, mineral smell of earth that had not been shaken by an engine in a long time. It made the ship's smell on him louder.

The ship sat at anchor a quarter mile out, black and low in the gray water, with a thin thread of smoke from the one boiler they were still willing to feed. The engines had been given a rest to cool. Nobody had said what it would cost to ask them to start again.

The camp was a flat of trampled fern just above the tideline. Two casks stood open by the stream. A third lay on its side while Kaito rolled it upright with his boot, because his left palm still wore the knot Jake had tied for him and he was careful with it.

There were two men missing from the shore party. They were the two who had been missing from the watch since the night of the sail, and Jee had left them aboard without being asked, and had said nothing about it to anyone. Jake had noticed, and had not asked either. It sat in the camp like a stone in a boot.

Beyond him, six or seven of the crew were scattered through the trees with hatchets and canvas sacks, hunting dead branches for the stove and for anything else that would burn. Somebody had found a seam of brown coal showing in the stream bank, and two men were prying at it with a boat hook. It was crumbling to wet powder under the iron. Jake watched them for a moment and then looked away, because he had counted the days left in the ledger before they landed, and the seam did not change the number.

The date was written at the head of the ledger in Jake's own hand, because Jake was the one who wrote dates. Three days to the winter solstice, the shortest day of the year, and after that the northern weather shut down for the season. Storms came down off the ice in the first week of the new year and sat on the coast until spring, and a ship that lost a day in the north lost a week, and a week in the north was the season gone.

Kaito came to stand at his elbow, waiting to be told where to put the number.

"How long past the solstice can a ship work the north coast, sir?"

"Depends on the weather. The storms come down off the ice."

"Then the solstice is the last day we can count on."

"It is."

"We want to be north of the sound before it."

"We want to be north of the sound a week ago." Jake turned the board over. "Take the casks to the stream. And when you come back, I want the north chart out of the box. We are going to be in a hurry, and I do not want to be doing it in the dark."

The Avatar was a boy with a bison. He could sit out a storm as easily as a day. Every hour the ship lost in a harbour was one he did not have to be found, and Jake had done that sum in six ports now, and it had never once come out in the Fire Nation's favour.

He had made himself the one who stood still. That had been his decision on the beach: tally the casks, mark the wood, keep the work moving and keep his hands out of it. When a cask needed lifting, he found a reason to be looking at something else, and someone stronger stepped in. Nobody questioned it. He doubted anyone had noticed there was a pattern.

His hands had been quiet for the length of the walk from the boat. Standing in the cold, they started again.

It began under the gloves, a slow pulse in the palms that kept time with his heart. He had wrapped them himself before first light, two turns each, snug across the heel and the splits, and the linen had gone damp and stiff under the leather by the time the casks were counted. The leather on top had dried into the shape of a closed fist. Every time he straightened his fingers to point at a cask, the wrap pulled where it had stuck, and he stopped short of forcing it. He kept his face pleasant. He pointed anyway.

"Fresh water first," he told Kaito, "then wood. The stream runs clear above the bend. Don't fill from here, the coal's fouling it."

"Yes, sir." Kaito said it too quickly and went off through the ferns, and Jake realized he had been called sir by a nineteen-year-old and had not once thought about it.

Across the clearing, Zuko was walking.

He had been walking since they landed, a long strip of ground between the fire pit and the first trees, ten paces out and ten back. He wore no coat. His armor was buckled on over the same dark shirt he had slept in, and the cold had put color into his face that looked less like cold than fever. His right hand was in his pocket. It had been in his pocket since the rig. Through the cloth Jake could see the shape of the knuckles, closed around something small.

He knew what it was. He had not told anyone he knew more than that, and he did not intend to start.

Zuko reached the end of his strip, turned, and looked at the ship, then at the sky, then at the gray line where the coast bent away north. He did not look at the water or the trees. He looked at where something might be. Jake had watched the same look for three years, and it had never once been about the thing in front of him.

"Who has the count on the casks?" Zuko called. He did not turn.

"I do. Two of five full. We'll have all five by midday."

"We leave within the hour."

"The wood—"

"Within the hour, Jake."

"Yes, Prince Zuko."

It came out even. Jake was pleased with that. He let his hands hang loose at his sides and felt the blood move in them, warm and wrong.

"Oh, I'd hardly call this a delay."

Iroh had come up the stream path with his hands folded behind him, his robe belted loose and a folded towel over one shoulder that Jake had not seen him bring from the ship. He moved as he always moved on land after too long at sea, a little rolling, taking his time. He stopped beside Jake and breathed in through his nose, slow, like a man tasting wine.

"The forest," he said, "is very good for the lungs."

"Uncle." Zuko had turned. "We are not staying."

"Of course not." Iroh smiled at the trees. "But I remember a spring near here. Past the ridge, in a hollow. Hot enough to boil an egg at the source, though the pool itself is perfect. I passed it years ago, marching the other way, and I have thought about it ever since." He rolled one shoulder, and it made a small, dry sound. "These old joints have been a very long time on iron decks."

"There's no time."

"There is always time for the joints."

"The Avatar is not going to wait for your joints." Zuko's hand came halfway out of his pocket and went back in. "We have a lead. We have a *real* lead, and the engines are the only reason we're not already on it—"

"Then the engines will thank me for giving them an hour." Iroh patted Zuko's arm on his way past, as though Zuko had said something agreeable. "And I will be back before the casks are full. Nephew. Jake."

He gave Jake a look as he passed. It lasted about as long as it took to step over a root. Iroh's eyes went to the stiff leather of the gloves, then to Jake's face, then to Zuko's back, where the prince stood with his shoulders drawn up around his ears. Then he was gone down the path with his towel and his easy walk, whistling something that did not have a tune.

Jake understood. Iroh had picked an hour out of the day and carried it away where Zuko could not spend it on anyone. It was a generous theft. It was not likely to work.

"An hour," Zuko said to the empty path. "He knows it's an hour."

"He knows," Jake said.

Zuko resumed his walking. Jake let him.

He stood a while with the tally board and made marks he did not need to make. Then he sat on the lid of the nearest cask, carefully, and set his hands on his knees palm-up, a little apart from the wool of his coat, so that nothing touched them. The cold felt good on the leather. He did not take the gloves off. He could not have put them on again.

The fire popped. Somewhere deep in the trees a hatchet struck dead wood, and the sound came back flat and late. Jake watched the line of Zuko's back go out and come back, out and back, and thought that something in his walking had stopped being restless and started being something that would eventually give.

He did not know which of them it would be.

By his own count, it took twenty minutes. Zuko stopped between one stride and the next, with his hand halfway to his pocket. He looked at the path where Iroh had gone, and Jake saw the decision cross his face before it reached his feet.

"That's enough," Zuko said, to no one.

He was already walking. He did not look back to see if he was followed. He never did.

Jake stood. The tally board slid from his knee and he caught it against his ribs, forearm only, and told Kaito over his shoulder to finish the casks and keep the crew on the wood. Then he went after the prince into the trees, ahead of the ache, with his hands held a little out from his sides.

---
The spring lay in a hollow past the ridge, where the pines opened up and the ground fell away in a bowl of dark, wet stone. Steam rose from it in slow sheets. Jake smelled it before he saw it, a faint mineral sourness like struck flint that sat oddly against all that cold green. The rocks around the rim were bare of frost. So was the moss between them, for a yard in every direction, as if a lamp had been left burning under the pool.

Iroh sat in the middle of it with his arms spread along the edge and his eyes shut. The towel was folded on a boulder. His robe was folded beside it, with his sandals set neatly on top. One of his hands trailed in the water. Jake thought he saw the surface near the fingers shiver, once, as if something warm had been put into it on purpose. The pool had not been this hot when Iroh found it. He had made it so, as he made a pot of tea, because it pleased him to.

Zuko stopped at the rim and did not so much as look at the view.

"Uncle! It's time to leave!" His voice bounced back flat off the far stones. "Where are you?"

"Here," Iroh said, without opening his eyes. "Where else would I be?"

"We need to move. Now. The Avatar's trail is getting colder every minute we sit—"

"Zuko." Iroh sighed, slow and content. "Come in. Soak your troubles away. There is room."

"I don't have *troubles* to soak. I have a mission."

"All the more reason." One eye opened. "You are carrying that mission in your shoulders, nephew. I can see it from here."

Jake had stopped a few steps behind the prince, at the edge of the trees. He kept very still, because he knew what usually came next. Zuko's hand moved in his pocket and Jake felt the silence around it tighten.

"I am not getting in."

"Then I will have to get out." Iroh stood.

There was a good deal of him, and all of it rose in a single, unhurried, steaming motion. Water ran down in sheets. Jake had the presence of mind to look at the sky. Zuko had no such luck. He made a sound as if he had been struck in the throat, flung his forearm across his eyes, and turned his whole body halfway round toward the trees.

"Put it away," he said. "Put it— *Uncle*."

"You said we should leave."

"On second thought—" His voice went up and cracked, and he dragged it back down. "On second thought, why don't you take another few minutes? But be back at the ship in half an hour, or I'm leaving without you!"

"Very generous," Iroh said, and lowered himself back into the water with a long groan of pleasure. His chin settled on the surface. His eyes were already closing before the ripples reached the rim.

Zuko strode up the slope with his arm still half over his face. He passed Jake without slowing. Jake stayed a moment longer. Iroh opened one eye, lazy, and found him, and Jake gave him what he could manage, a small tight wince that said *I'm sorry, he's like this, you know he's like this.* Iroh's mouth curved. Then the eye closed again.

Jake followed the prince back into the trees.

---
The half hour took a very long time.

It passed in Zuko's strip of ground by the fire, and by the end of it there was a visible groove worn in the ferns. The casks were full. Three of the wood-gatherers had come in and were being very busy near the stove. Jake spent the time rewrapping nothing, because he could not do it here without someone seeing, and sat on the cask lid again with his hands hanging between his knees. When Zuko finally stopped, the half hour was one minute over, and he said only, "Two of you. With us," and pointed at the nearest soldiers.

They went back up the ridge at nearly a run. Jake kept pace behind the prince, a stride too slow, hands tucked tight against his chest. By the time the hollow opened beneath them he was breathing through his teeth.

The steam had thinned. That was the first thing wrong. The pool lay clear and still, with nothing sitting in it, and the mist had drained off until it was only a skin on the water.

"Uncle?" Zuko said it at normal volume, then louder. "Uncle! The half hour was up—"

The rim was empty. The towel, the robe, the sandals, all of it was gone. Jake went down the slope behind the prince and stood with his hand at his side, and let his eyes do what they always did first, which was to look at the ground.

The ferns on the far side lay flattened in a wide, scuffed arc. Beyond that, the soil had been turned, not in any pattern a boot made, and not a boar's rooting either. It looked as though a hand had pressed down on the earth and lifted again. At the edge of the turned ground a stand of small stones had been piled against the slope above the spring in a tight heap, jagged and raw, with fresh gray faces, standing out against the smooth river rocks that ringed the pool.

"Prince Zuko," Jake said.

"I see it." Zuko was already moving. He crossed the hollow in four strides and stood over the rock pile with his arms rigid at his sides and his head bent. Jake saw the anger go out of him the way water goes out of a basin. What stayed behind was cold and exact.

One of the soldiers came up on the other side. "Landslide, sir? These hills, in the thaw—"

"Look at the slope."

The soldier looked.

"The hill goes up behind it." Zuko pointed without turning. "Everything above that pile is higher than where it's lying. Land doesn't slide uphill." His hand dropped toward the cracked faces of the stone. "These rocks didn't move naturally. Somebody put them there."

Nobody spoke. Jake's pulse had begun to fill his ears.

"My uncle," Zuko said, "has been captured by earthbenders."

It reached Jake as a drop in his stomach, quick and cold. Not Iroh. Iroh was the one who stayed. Iroh had folded the towel. He had sat in the water with his eyes closed and a hand trailing in it, easy as a man in his own house. Jake had walked away and left him there, and had been *sorry* to, a little, because the hour had been so plainly a kindness. He had thought the worst thing out here was the engines.

He put his hands to the tops of his gloves and pulled the cuffs down, hard, to settle them. Something shifted in the left palm and let go. The warm trickle that followed felt almost like relief. He did not look at it. He worked the leather smooth over his wrist and kept his face still.

Zuko had turned. His eyes found Jake's without searching, as they always did, and held. There was no argument in them. They did not ask if Jake could, or whether he should. Jake nodded once.

"You two," Zuko said to the soldiers, "back to the ship. Tell Lieutenant Jee to bring up the steam and have everything ready to leave the moment we return. Nobody sails without us." He did not wait for acknowledgment. "Go."

They went. Their boots receded up the ridge.

Zuko was already walking the other way, along the churned line of ground where the earth had been disturbed, bent forward with his eyes on the soil. Jake fell in beside him, a half-step back and to the left, where he always walked. Neither of them said anything at all.

---
The tracks were not hard to follow. Whoever had taken Iroh had not troubled to hide them.

Past the hollow the ground climbed into rock and scrub pine, and the churned earth turned into a line of deep, three-toed prints pressed into the dirt, each one the size of a dinner plate. Ostrich horses. Heavy ones, loaded, from the depth. Between the prints lay the short, scuffed drag of something carried or hauled, and then it was gone, because the trail had gone up onto stone and the stone kept its own counsel.

Zuko did not slow. He climbed the way he did everything, as if the hill had insulted him. Jake went after him and felt the first real cost on the third step that needed a hand.

It was a small ledge, chest high, with a good hold on top. He reached for it and stopped, and found another way, forearm flat across the rock and the weight carried on his elbow while his boots scrabbled. The heel of his hand brushed the stone as he pulled. Nothing so sharp as pain, only a white flash behind the eyes, like a lamp lit and dropped. He made no sound. He was over the ledge and on his feet before Zuko, five paces up the trail, could glance back.

"Keep up," Zuko said, which was not the same as noticing.

"I'm here."

The next slope was loose shale, and he went up it on his knees and wrists, hands curled into loose fists so that only the leather backs touched. Warm wetness spread under the linen with every fistful of grit. He thought about the weight of the knife at his belt, and about nothing else at all, because it was much easier.

The trail bent around a spur of gray rock, and Zuko stopped.

He had gone down on one knee. Jake came up beside him and saw what lay on the path, half hidden by a fallen needle-bed: a sandal, a plain wooden-soled one with a faded cloth strap, worn pale at the heel. The sole had turned over. Jake knew it before Zuko picked it up. Nobody else in the Fire Navy kept a sandal that long. It had come the whole way from the capital, and Iroh had refused each time anyone suggested new ones.

Zuko turned it in his hands. He touched the strap where it crossed, then lifted it and held it close under his face for a moment, and Jake looked away at the rocks to give him the moment.

"He left it," Zuko said. His voice was flat. "On purpose."

"He'd have to hop," Jake said, before he thought. "To lose one on a slope."

Zuko made a short sound that was nearly a laugh and was gone before it finished. He stood, shoved the sandal through his belt, and went on up the trail faster than before.

They reached the ridge at the top of the next climb. It was bare stone and a few stunted trees, flung up out of the forest like a knuckle, with the ground falling steeply away on the far side into folded gray hills. The wind found them as soon as they cleared the spur. It came cold off the sea and smelled of salt and distance, and smacked him full in the face. He lifted his head into it, grateful. Zuko stood at the crest and swept the land below with a hard, scanning stare, hunting for the tracks.

Something cold slid across the stone at Jake's feet.

It was a shadow, long and slow, pulled over the ridge like a sheet. Jake looked up.

It was far off already, a big white shape drifting out over the valley with its wide flat tail beating the air, six legs hung beneath it like a swimmer's. He knew it. He had watched it climb out of the wreck of Kyoshi's harbor with two small shapes on its back. Now it carried a few dark specks near its head, dwindling, with the wind behind it, all that great soft shape moving over the hills as if the ground had nothing to do with it.

"The Avatar!" Zuko said.

He did not shout it. It came out strangled and low, as if he had been struck. He took a step forward with his arm half-raised, and then he stopped, and his hand went to his pocket.

He drew out the ribbon.

It lay across his scarred knuckles, dark blue and dirt-stained, the strap torn clean through, the small pale stone swinging at the end of it in the wind. Jake knew it too. He had known it on the rig, in the moment he saw it lie in Zuko's palm, and he had said nothing about it to anyone. He said nothing now. Zuko stood with the stone in his closed hand, staring at the sky, and his breath was very quick.

Jake could see it all. If they ran back now, down the ridge and through the forest to the ship, they would have steam up by the time the bison cleared the bay. Zuko would be making the numbers in his head: how far, how fast, how long. His mouth moved on them. The boy in blue was somewhere under that shape, and the thing he had chased across the whole bright ocean was slipping past in plain daylight, and every muscle in him wanted to go.

Jake stopped beside him and did not touch him. He kept his hands where they were, held a little out from his sides, and let the wind take the blood-smell off them. He did not say *Iroh*. He did not say *the ship*. There was nothing he could say that would not be a hand on Zuko's back, and this had to be Zuko's own walking. His heart had gone up into his throat and stayed there. He thought, quite clearly, *Please*, and told himself he had not.

The bison turned in the distance, small now, a pale blot above the far hills, and Zuko said, almost under his breath, "He's *right there*."

Neither of them moved.

Then Zuko looked down.

His eyes dropped from the sky to the dirt at the edge of the ridge, where the ostrich horse tracks cut away along the stone, down into the folded country on the other side. Heavy prints. Close together. Going the opposite way from everything else he wanted. He looked at them for what felt, to Jake, like a long time.

He shoved the ribbon back into his pocket.

He did it all at once, hard, like a man putting out a fire with his hand. Then he turned his back on the sky and went down the slope along the tracks at a dead run.

Jake did not need to be told. He was moving before the boots had left the stone, and the sudden pull of his own stride tore the left glove at the palm and he did not feel it. Behind him the great shadow was all gone from the ridge. Ahead lay the dirt trail, narrow and walled in with rock, and Zuko's back, dark against the gray, pounding on down it as though no sky had ever existed.

Something loosened in Jake's chest, bigger than relief, and he did not look at it directly, because the trail was steep and Zuko was fast. He only knew that the pain in his hands, which had been a thing he carried, had turned into a thing he was paying for something. He ran harder.

They crested the next rise together, scrambling up the last rocks on elbows and knees, and lay flat at the top with their faces in the cold grit.

Below them, in a hollow of broken stone, someone was shouting. There was the heavy sound of rock being dragged across rock, and a low laugh, and under it, thin and unmistakable and unhurried, a voice that Jake would have known in any dark.

---
Iroh was kneeling in the dust of the hollow in his underclothes.

That was the first thing Jake took in, and it stayed with him through all the rest. The old man had been stripped to a cotton undershirt and loose drawers, and his belly and bare feet were gray with grit. He knelt before a flat slab of stone with his wrists chained in front of him, and he did not look afraid. He looked like a man waiting for a late meal. Five soldiers in green-brown armor stood in a loose ring around him. A sixth, wearing the heavier plates of an officer, stood a little apart with his arms raised.

Over the officer's head, turning slowly in the air, hung a boulder the size of a cart wheel.

Jake's breath stopped in his chest.

"The Dragon of the West," the officer was saying, quite pleasantly. "Six hundred days you sat outside Ba Sing Se. Burned every farm from here to the Serpent's Pass. I'd say you've done enough with those hands, wouldn't you?"

"I'd say I have done a great deal with them," Iroh agreed, "and that I am very fond of them."

The boulder dipped. Jake saw it settle in the air above the slab, directly over the chained wrists, and the soldiers move back a pace to give it room.

Zuko did not say a word. He was up on his feet on the rock before Jake had finished understanding what he saw, one hand already pulled back, and Jake's own body moved without asking him. His left palm hit the ledge as he came up beside the prince. It split further along the old tear, and the pain went through him white to the elbow. He did not slow. There was a thin dark stream running down the rock beside him, snowmelt, a few finger-widths across, and he put his hand flat to it and *pulled*, and felt the water answer. His whole arm sang. He held it anyway.

Zuko went off the rock.

He came down in the middle of the ring like a thrown spear. His heel struck the boulder at its lowest point and drove it sideways out of the air. It tore from the officer's grip with a sound like a landslide, spun across the hollow, and cracked against the far wall in a spray of grit. Before it had finished falling Zuko had planted his other foot and whipped the first one round in a flat arc. Flame burst from the heel of his boot, and the chain between Iroh's wrists rang once and parted.

Iroh looked at the two ends of the link in his hands. He looked up.

"Excellent form, Prince Zuko."

"You taught me well," Zuko said. He was breathing hard, and his face was savage with a joy Jake had not seen on it in a year.

Then the soldiers charged.

Jake dropped from the ledge into the dust with the stream of water curled around his arm. There were four of them coming now, two for Zuko's back and two for Iroh, and the officer was already dragging a plate of rock up out of the earth. Jake cut left across the hollow, circling, and the nearest soldier turned toward him with a stone cocked back in his fist.

A canteen hung at the man's hip. Jake held his hand out toward it and closed his fingers, and the cork shot out, and the water jumped to him.

His palm screamed. It felt like closing his hand on a live coal. He kept his face still. He was aware of his own jaw, set, and of the cold clear thought that came down over everything like a lid: *Not the officer. Leave him to them. Keep them off Zuko's back.* The water whipped out from the canteen in a short hard line and took the soldier's legs from under him. The man went down on his shoulder with the stone still in his hand.

One. Jake wheeled.

Behind Zuko a second soldier had hurled a rock, a flat slab, aimed at the base of the prince's skull. Zuko was fighting two others and could not see it. Jake threw the water up and across, into a ring in the air between the slab and the back of Zuko's head. He did not try to stop it. He turned it. The stone struck the surface and slid off to one side, and went past with a soft whistle, and cracked into a boulder. Zuko did not look round. He had not needed to.

Jake's hands were wet now in a way that was not the water. He could feel it moving inside the gloves, warm, sliding between the linen and the leather every time he closed his fingers. He did it again anyway. The water coiled back to him and he caught it and let it go, and the third soldier, rushing Iroh with his arms spread, stepped on a sheet of glazed ice no wider than a doorstep and landed on his back.

Iroh, standing now, with the two halves of his chain swinging from his fists, blinked down at him.

"Thank you, Jake," he said.

Across the hollow the officer was still bringing up stone. He had a wall of it half erected between himself and Zuko, and the fourth and fifth soldiers had turned their attention to the prince, who was a tornado of fire that moved too fast for either of them. Steam rose in a hiss from the wet ground wherever his flames met the melt. The air was thick with it, hot on Jake's face and cold in the cracks of his lungs.

Iroh moved.

It was a little thing. He took three loose steps along the edge of the fight in his bare feet, spinning the chain in one hand. It sang. Then he let it go. It wrapped twice around the officer's ankles and Iroh leaned back on the other end like a man hauling a net. The officer cried out and fell. The wall of rock he had built came loose from the earth at the same moment and fell with him, and it came down across his chest and legs in a heap, and stayed there.

"Oh dear," said Iroh, mildly.

For a moment nothing moved in the hollow. Dust drifted. The last two soldiers stood in front of Zuko with their hands half raised and looked at the officer, and at the chain, and at the prince. One of them dropped his stone.

Zuko stood over the nearest man, panting, with the heat still coming off his shoulders. His hair had come loose across his forehead and the old burn along the side of his face had gone dark with blood. He looked at his uncle.

"Now," he said, "would you *please* put on some clothes?"

Iroh looked down at himself with great surprise.

Jake let the water go. It slumped out of the air and ran down his arm and into the sleeve of his coat, and he felt the strength go out of his hands with it. The gloves squelched when he opened his fingers. He held them out from his body and kept them there, carefully, because he did not trust himself to lower them. The stream came back to its bed on the rock without a sound.

Iroh was walking across the hollow, humming, to a small pile of cloth that had been thrown over a stone. It was the robe, the towel, the folded sandal. He picked up the robe and shook the dust out of it with a great deal of care. Then he looked across the clearing at Jake, over the edge of it, and for the length of one breath did not look like a man who was humming at all.

Jake held his eyes. Then he nodded, once, that he was all right, and watched Iroh believe him exactly as far as he meant to.

---
They did not stay to see the soldiers wake.

Zuko had stood over the officer's heap of stone for as long as it took to be certain the man was breathing. Then he kicked the nearest fallen helmet out of reach and walked away without another glance. Iroh followed him, tying the sash of his robe. Jake came last, a few paces behind, with his hands held out from his sides like something he was carrying.

The trail back ran down through the pines. The afternoon had begun to go. It had not turned dark yet, but the light came in low and flat through the trunks and made long stripes across the path, and each time Jake crossed one it laid a little warmth on his face before the shade took it back. The wind had dropped. In its place there was the sound of the forest settling, needles ticking against one another, a stream somewhere off the trail, the soft thud of dead cones underfoot. His coat was wet at the cuffs where the water had run into it. He could feel the damp going cold against his wrists.

The sandal was back on Iroh's foot. Zuko had given it to him at the edge of the hollow. He had pulled it from his belt and held it out without turning his head, and Iroh had taken it and said nothing at all, which in Iroh was a good deal. Now the old man walked with a slight limp, one foot in leather and one in the dust, and did not seem to mind.

Jake fell into step beside him.

He did not decide to. His eyes had been going over Iroh since the hollow, and now they went on doing it in the quiet, quickly and thoroughly, as he went over a deckhand after a fall. The robe was dusty to the knees. There was a graze above one elbow. The skin of both wrists was red and raised in a wide band, rubbed raw where the chain had sat, and there was a scrape on the left shin that had bled a little and stopped. Nothing was crooked. Iroh moved his shoulders freely. When he turned his head, he turned it as far as it would go.

"Your wrists," Jake said.

"Are wrists." Iroh glanced down at them with mild interest. "They have been worse. A little stiff, perhaps. I will say the spring did a remarkable job on my joints before all this began."

"The shin—"

"A scratch, Jake."

"It should be washed."

"On the ship. With hot water and tea." Iroh turned toward him then, and put out his hand, and took hold of Jake's wrist.

It was not a hard grip. The fingers closed around the leather of the cuff and stayed there, warm through it, with the thumb resting along the inside of the joint. Jake stopped walking. He had not meant to, but the hand held him there and he found that he could not pull away from it without making a point of it.

Iroh was looking at his gloves.

They were dark to the knuckles. Jake had known that, as he knew the weather. The palms were the worst of it. The leather over them had gone black and glossy, and while he stood there something heavy let go inside the left one and soaked through and touched the edge of Iroh's fingers. A small dark bead appeared on the cuff. It ran down toward the thumb, slowly. Neither of them looked at it for very long.

He waited for it to be said. *You should have told me. You should have stayed on the ship. You should have let someone else.* He had the answers ready, one after another, folded small and tidy. He had been carrying them since the morning.

Iroh did not say any of it.

He looked at the gloves, and then up at Jake's face. For a moment his mouth worked once, as if he were about to speak. Then it eased, and what was left in his eyes was very plain and very tired, and Jake looked away from it first. It was not a look he could answer. It was gratitude with a grief folded inside it, and the old man did not make him explain what either was for.

"Come," Iroh said at last. He let go of Jake's wrist, gave it one slow pat, and went on down the path.

Ahead of them, Zuko had stopped.

He stood in a pool of the low gold light where the trees thinned, with his back to them and his head tipped up. Jake saw his armor take the sun along one shoulder. He saw the line of the prince's arm, and the dark of his pocket where his hand was not, and he knew where Zuko was looking before his own eyes followed. West, and up, to the far hills over the tops of the pines. A rag of cloud lay across it. There was nothing else.

He felt his whole body go still and ready, as it did before Zuko's temper broke. He thought about the grooves in the fern, and the tracks on the ridge, and the sound the prince had made at the top of the hill. It would come now. Something would be kicked, or burned, or shouted at the sky, and then at him, and he would stand through it and take what came.

Zuko breathed out.

It was a long breath, slow and ragged at the end, as if it had been a long time coming from somewhere deep. His shoulders dropped half an inch. He reached up, found the strap of his shoulder plate where the climb had dragged it crooked, and pulled it straight. Then he turned and started walking again toward the coast.

He said nothing. He did not curse the earthbenders, or the hills, or the day. He did not look at the sky a second time.

Jake stood in the path for a heartbeat longer than he needed to. Then he went after him.

It took a few quick steps to close the gap, and every one of them jarred through his palms. He did not mind. He came up on Zuko's left, a half pace behind, where he always walked, and then, without thinking about it, level with him. Their boots found the same rhythm on the packed dirt, heel and heel, a dull steady crunch. Zuko did not look round. His eyes were on the trail. The color had gone out of his face and left it plain, and a dry line of blood had cracked along the old scar where something had caught him in the fight.

The pocket of his tunic was flat and heavy. It stayed shut.

Neither of them spoke. Behind them Iroh's slow tread had picked up the same beat without being asked, so that all three boots fell together, and the sound went ahead of them down the trail, steady and tired and unhurried. Jake kept his eyes on the ground and watched the stripes of light slide under his feet, and carried his hands in front of him, and thought of nothing at all except that he would have followed this walk a good deal farther.

The pines thinned. The air changed, wetter, with salt in it. Three days to the solstice, and then the coast closed, and the ship would have to move tomorrow whatever the engines did. Zuko would spend the whole evening deciding how to run the sound on a cold engine, and Jake would sit at the table and do the arithmetic he had been doing since Ketu Harbor.

Then the last trunks fell away and they came out onto the shingle with the whole gray width of the water opening in front of them, and there, riding at anchor in the failing light with one lamp lit on her bridge, sat the ship.
