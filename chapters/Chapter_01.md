# Chapter 1: The Reality of Exile

The rope had frozen solid sometime in the night, and now it fought him for every inch.

He worked it free coil by coil, kneeling on the frost-slick deck with his knees soaked through his trousers, ice crusted thick along the rigging where the spray had frozen as it fell. His hands, wrapped in worn leather, ached from the cold even through the gloves, but that was nothing new. Three years of southern water had taught his hands to expect it. He picked at a stubborn knot with his thumbnail until it gave, then coiled the freed line and moved to the next.

Around him the ship groaned the way it always did in heavy ice, a long low shudder that ran up through the deck boards and into his bones. Small floes scraped and cracked against the hull, a sound like something enormous chewing very slowly. Fire Nation steel wasn't built for this. None of it was — not the rigging, not the metal fittings that turned brittle in the cold, not the crew who huddled inside every chance they got. He'd learned to read the ship's complaints the way he read weather, and today it complained more than usual.

He didn't mind the work. The work was simple. The work had an answer.

Further along the deck, the prince was not working.

He was pacing — had been pacing for the better part of an hour, a tight track worn between the rail and the mast, his boots striking the frost with more force than the footing required. He'd come up in only his under-robe despite the cold, sleeves pushed back, and steam rose faintly off his bare forearms where restless heat leaked out of him without his noticing. His topknot had come half-loose in the wind. Every few strides he stopped at the rail to stare out at the gray horizon, jaw tight, and then resumed pacing as though the horizon had personally disappointed him.

The scar was easiest to see from this angle — the ruined, shining stretch of skin that pulled at the corner of his eye, more visible in the flat light than it was belowdecks. He didn't turn to hide it anymore. He hadn't in a long time. Three years would do that, wear the instinct to flinch down to nothing, the same way it wore down most things.

Jake didn't need to look up to know when Zuko had stopped again. He could hear it in the change of rhythm, the absence of boots on planking. He finished lashing the line, moved himself two paces closer to the rail without appearing to have moved at all, and reached for the next coil of rope.

It was an old habit. He'd stopped noticing he did it.

"Anything?" Zuko asked, not looking at him. He never looked at him when he asked it. He asked the horizon, mostly, and Jake happened to be standing nearby.

"No smoke. No sails." Jake glanced out at the ice all the same, because Zuko would want to know he had actually checked. "Same as an hour ago."

Zuko made a low sound in his throat that wasn't quite a word and went back to the rail, knuckles whitening where he gripped the cold metal. Jake let him have the silence. There was nothing useful to say to three years of the same horizon, and Zuko had never wanted comfort so much as company — someone else awake and watching, so the waiting didn't feel like something he was doing alone.

Jake picked the ice free of the next stretch of line and let his eyes drift out past the rail, out to where the floes broke apart into open black water and then closed together again into endless white.

It caught him before he expected it to. It always did.

There was nothing about the ice that should have meant anything to him. It was only water, frozen and refrozen a thousand different ways, stretching out flat and blinding under a colorless sky, broken here and there by pressure ridges that threw long blue shadows across the snow. He had never set foot on southern ice before this voyage. He had no memory of a village, a home, a mother's face, nothing that this landscape should have been able to reach. And yet something in him kept turning toward it the way a compass needle turns without asking permission, some old current running underneath his ribs that recognized the cold, the light, the particular gray-white color of the sky, in a way his mind flatly refused to.

He'd stopped trying to name it a long time ago. Naming it only made it worse.

His hand had gone still on the rope. He noticed that before he noticed why, and made himself start moving again, working the frost out of the fibers with more attention than the task required. Cold got into a man's head out here, that was all. Iroh said so often enough, usually right before pouring tea. Long stretches of white could make anyone imagine things — faces in the ice, voices in the wind, the phantom sense of somewhere they'd never been. It was the kind of thing that happened to sailors on long postings. It didn't mean anything.

He told himself that, and mostly believed it, and went back to his knots.

Down the deck, Zuko had started pacing again, faster now, his bare arms goosefleshed despite the heat still coming off him in faint visible waves. Jake watched him from the corner of his eye — the tight shoulders, the restless hands that kept curling and releasing at his sides, the way his gaze cut across the same stretch of horizon again and again as if repetition might eventually produce a different answer. Three years of exile had worn most of the softness out of him. What was left underneath was harder, thinner, strung tight enough that Jake had learned to feel the tension in the air before Zuko ever said a word about it.

He finished the last of the rigging and stood, knees stiff from the cold, and moved himself unhurriedly toward the rail — close enough that Zuko wouldn't have to raise his voice if he wanted to say something, not so close that it would look like anything other than a man finishing his rounds. It was a distance he'd learned by feel over three years of shared decks and shared silences, and he kept it now without thinking about it, the same way he kept his hands moving so the cold wouldn't settle into them.

The ice stretched on ahead of the ship, endless and empty and strangely familiar, and Jake made himself stop looking at it.

It was only the cold. It had to be. He turned his attention back to Zuko, and to the horizon Zuko was so certain would eventually give something up, and let the ship's slow grinding progress through the floes fill the silence between them.

***

The light came without warning, and for a moment Jake thought the sky itself had cracked open.

A column of brilliant blue-white erupted somewhere far off across the ice, so bright it threw shadows across the deck that hadn't existed a heartbeat before. It rose impossibly high, a shaft of pure light boring straight up into the overcast until it seemed to disappear into the clouds themselves, and for several long seconds nobody on deck moved at all.

Then Zuko was at the rail, both hands gripping the cold metal, staring at it with an intensity that made Jake's stomach tighten before he understood why.

"Finally," Zuko breathed, and then, louder, turning: "Uncle! Do you realize what this means?"

Behind them, seated at a small folding table someone had wedged into the lee of the deckhouse, an older man in Fire Nation robes looked up from the tiles arranged in front of him. He was broad through the shoulders in a way that suggested old strength gone comfortable, his beard neatly kept, a cup of tea steaming faintly at his elbow despite the cold. He held one tile between two fingers, considering it, entirely unhurried by the light still blazing on the horizon.

"I won't get to finish my game," he said, and it wasn't quite a question.

"It means my search is about to come to an end." Zuko had turned back to the light, both hands white-knuckled on the rail, watching it the way a starving man watches food.

The older man sighed — a long, put-upon sound Jake had heard a hundred times before, usually right before tea was offered as a solution to something tea could not solve — and set his tile down on the board without looking at it. "That light came from an incredibly powerful source," Zuko said, rounding on him again, gesturing hard toward the horizon where the column was already beginning to thin, to waver, to shrink back down into the ice from wherever it had come. "It has to be him."

"Or it's just the celestial lights." The old man studied his tiles instead of the sky, turning one between his fingers — a small stylized symbol, an arrow, though Jake was too far to make out the detail. "We've been down this road before, Prince Zuko." He set the tile aside and gestured to the empty stool across from him, his voice unbothered, almost fond. "I don't want you to get too excited over nothing. Please — sit. Why don't you enjoy a cup of calming jasmine tea?"

Zuko's hands came off the rail like it had burned him. "I don't need any calming tea." His voice climbed fast, temper catching the way it always did when hope and doubt collided in him at the same moment. "I need to capture the Avatar." He was already turning, already moving toward the helm, robe sleeves flaring with the force of it. "Helmsman! Head a course for the light!"

Jake didn't move from where he stood near the rigging, but something in his chest had gone very still and very alert, the way it always did in the half-second before a plan changed underneath him.

Three years. Three years of empty horizons and cold decks and Zuko staring at nothing, and now the nothing had answered back.

He watched the old man — Iroh, the crew called him, though Jake had never needed the name to know who he was, had known him nearly as long as he'd known anything — watched him pick his tea back up with the same unhurried calm as before, though his eyes stayed on his nephew's back a moment longer than the gesture required. Jake recognized that look. It wasn't disbelief. It was the particular patience of a man bracing to be disappointed on someone else's behalf, and choosing not to say so out loud.

Beneath them the deck shifted, engines groaning up from something closer to idle into full power, and the whole ship gave a long shudder as it began to come about. Ice cracked and split somewhere along the hull, louder than before, the bow shouldering through pack ice it had been content to skirt an hour ago. Jake widened his stance without thinking about it, an old reflex, and reached out to steady the coil of rope he'd left balanced on the rail before it could slide.

Somewhere below, he could hear the first shouts of the crew reorganizing themselves around a heading, boots hammering on metal stairs, someone barking about the boiler. The whole ship had the feel of something coming awake after a long sleep, and Jake felt the change of it land in his stomach before he'd fully worked out what it meant.

They were no longer wandering. They had a direction now, and the direction was straight into the heart of Water Tribe territory — toward whatever, or whoever, had thrown that much power into the sky.

Jake looked out at the place where the light had been, now nothing but a smear of afterimage against gray cloud, and felt the first thread of something colder than the wind work its way under his collar.

***

Time bled together the way it always did on ship's routine — a stretch of open water, another night in the ice, and then the deck cleared for drills as if the light on the horizon had never happened at all. Except Zuko hadn't slept. Jake could see that in the flat, overworked look behind his eyes, in the way his movements had gone sharp and clipped instead of merely rigid. Two nights of staring at a heading and calculating distance had worn something thin in him, and it was going to come out somewhere.

It came out on the sparring deck, midmorning, under a sky the color of old iron.

Jake had set himself up near the water barrels with a coil of hose and the excuse of checking the ship's freshwater levels, close enough to the drill to watch without looking like he was watching. He'd learned that particular skill early — how to make himself useful and present in the same breath, a body doing one task while his attention did another. Two soldiers in firebending stances faced Zuko across the cleared deck, both of them wearing the particular caution of men who'd sparred with the prince enough times to respect what happened when his temper outpaced his form. Iroh sat off to one side on a low camp stool, hands folded over his stomach, watching with the patient stillness of a man who had seen this exact drill run a hundred times before and expected to see it a hundred more.

"Again," Iroh said.

Zuko opened with fire flung wide from both arms, a broad showy attack that both soldiers slipped without much effort, ducking and stepping clear in practiced unison. He shifted his weight low to avoid the answering blast, came up fast, fists already cocked for the next exchange — and Jake watched the whole sequence with the eye of someone who had watched it too many times to be impressed by the flames and instead watched the feet, the shoulders, the breath. The breath was wrong. It had been wrong for two days.

Iroh saw it too. He rose from the stool with a long-suffering sigh, waving a hand to stop the drill before Zuko had finished resetting his stance.

"No." He crossed to his nephew, unhurried, and set two fingers lightly against Zuko's ribs. "Power in firebending comes from the breath, not the muscles. The breath becomes energy in the body. The energy extends past your limbs and becomes fire." He demonstrated with a short, controlled blast that curled from his palm and dissolved harmlessly a hand's width from Zuko's face, close enough to make the point without singeing a hair. "Get it right this time."

Zuko's jaw worked. Jake recognized that too — the particular tightness that meant Zuko was holding something back mostly out of respect for the audience rather than any real change of heart. "Enough," he said, low and clipped. "I've been drilling this sequence all day. Teach me the next set. I'm more than ready."

"No, you are impatient." Iroh returned to his stool without any particular urgency, as if the conversation were already finished. "You have yet to master your basics." He raised his voice, unbothered. "Drill it again."

Jake kept coiling the hose and did not look up, though every part of him had gone still and attentive the way it did whenever Zuko's temper crested toward a certain pitch. He knew the shape of what came next. He'd known it since Zuko's breath had gone shallow two exchanges ago.

Zuko turned back to the soldiers, and something in his stance had shifted from drill to threat. He pivoted hard and drove a fire kick at the nearer soldier with far more force than the exercise called for — not a controlled strike but a real one, thrown with the raw, unspent frustration of two sleepless nights and a horizon that refused to give him answers. The soldier tried to block it and couldn't. The kick caught him square in the chest and threw him backward onto the deck, and where he landed, flame licked hungrily along the front of his uniform, catching on oil-stained cloth in a way training fire wasn't supposed to.

"The sages tell us the Avatar is the last airbender," Zuko was already saying, voice climbing, entirely fixed on his uncle and not on the man now scrambling and slapping at his own burning sleeve. "He must be over a hundred years old by now. He's had a century to master the four elements. I'll need more than basic firebending to defeat him." He was pointing now, jabbing a finger at Iroh as though the old man were personally responsible for the gap between Zuko's skill and a fully realized Avatar. "You will teach me the advanced set!"

Jake was already moving before the fire had finished spreading.

He crossed the deck in a handful of fast, economical strides, dropped to one knee beside the downed soldier, and pulled a stream of water from the barrel behind him with a short, sharp motion of his wrist — no wide Water Tribe sweep, no flowing arc, just a tight burst that snapped through the air like a whip uncoiling and struck the burning cloth dead center. Steam hissed up in a thin column, sharp and immediate, and the flame died in the same instant it had truly caught. A second smaller pass doused the smoldering edge along the deck planking before it could find purchase in the tar-caulked seams.

The soldier lay blinking up at gray sky, chest heaving, more startled than hurt. Jake was already checking him — hands moving fast and sure over the scorched fabric, feeling for heat that meant a real burn underneath rather than surface char.

"You're all right," Jake said, low, mostly to the soldier, mostly a fact rather than a comfort. "Sit up slow."

Behind him, Zuko hadn't so much as glanced down. He was still facing Iroh, still mid-argument, as though the small fire he'd just started and Jake had just ended were nothing more than a mildly annoying interruption in a conversation that mattered far more. That was how it always went. Jake didn't expect anything else. Zuko wasn't ignoring him out of contempt — he was ignoring him the way a man ignores his own hand doing something it had already been trusted to do a hundred times before. Jake handled the mess. Zuko argued about the things that actually kept him up at night. It had worked this way for three years, and neither of them had ever needed to discuss why.

The soldier's uniform had a scorched, blackened patch across the chest, ruined but not dangerous, and no blistering beneath it that Jake could find. He helped the man sit upright, checked his hands where he'd tried to beat out the flame himself, found nothing worse than reddened skin.

"I've got worse doing laundry," the soldier muttered, with the particular flat humor of someone who had been caught in Zuko's crossfire often enough to have a joke ready for it. Jake allowed himself the ghost of something almost like a smile and helped him to his feet.

Iroh, for his part, had weathered the accusation with the same placid patience he brought to everything Zuko threw at him, and once the flames were out and the deck secure, his expression shifted into something almost pleased.

"Very well," he said, in the reluctant tone of a man making a great concession — and then, as he bent to retrieve something wrapped in cloth from beside his stool, his face brightened entirely. "But first, I must finish my roast duck."

Zuko didn't answer that. He stood in the middle of the cleared deck with his fists still loosely closed, breathing hard, staring at nothing in particular while the anger drained slowly out of his shoulders and left something more tired behind it. Jake watched him from where he crouched beside the soldier, and felt none of the anger he probably should have. Zuko wasn't cruel. He was frightened, in the specific, unspoken way that made a man throw fire at anything within reach rather than sit still with the size of what he was about to face. Jake understood that kind of fear too well to resent the mess it left behind. His job, as it had been for three years, was simply to make sure the mess didn't cost anyone anything permanent.

He straightened, checked the soldier once more out of habit, and went back to coiling the hose he'd abandoned, steam still curling faintly off the wet planking behind him.

***

By the time Zuko retreated off the deck to change, the light had gone from flat gray to the deeper blue of early evening, and the ship's engines had settled into the steadier rhythm of a vessel closing distance rather than searching for it. Jake followed him below without needing to be told to, already running through the checklist of what would need tending before they got wherever they were going.

The heat hit him first, the way it always did coming in off the deck — that thick, artificial warmth pumped up from the engine room below, so different from the cold outside that his skin prickled with it, gloves and all. Zuko's quarters held the heat close, trapped it against the metal walls hung with the deep red of Fire Nation banners, and after the wind outside it felt almost suffocating, though Jake had long since stopped minding it. He'd take suffocating heat over the alternative.

He knelt beside the trunk at the foot of Zuko's bed and began laying the armor pieces out in the order he always laid them out — breastplate, shoulder guards, the reinforced gauntlets, the straps and buckles that held it all together — his hands moving through motions worn smooth by repetition. He checked each strap for fraying before he checked it for fit, ran his thumb along the edge of the breastplate to be sure nothing had rusted or warped since its last use. It was a task with a clear beginning and a clear end, and he was grateful for that in a way he didn't examine too closely.

Behind him, Zuko paced the narrow floor between his bed and the wall, shoulders drawn tight, breathing in slow deliberate counts that kept losing their rhythm halfway through. He'd start a breathing form, hands moving through the motions Iroh had drilled into him a thousand times, and then abandon it three breaths in to resume pacing instead, as if stillness itself had become intolerable.

"You think it's really him." It wasn't quite a question. Zuko said it to the wall, not to Jake, the way he said most things that mattered.

"I think you saw something big enough to change course for." Jake kept his eyes on the strap he was inspecting. "That's what I know. The rest is guessing."

"It has to be him." Zuko's voice had the particular hard edge it got when he was arguing with a part of himself rather than with Jake. "Nothing else could put that much light into the sky."

Jake didn't argue the point. There wasn't much use arguing with Zuko about the Avatar; he'd learned that years ago. He set the breastplate aside and reached for the gauntlets instead, checking the clasps one by one. "Either way, you'll want your gear sound before we get there. Can't fight anybody if your strap breaks in the middle of it."

That got something that wasn't quite a laugh out of Zuko — a short exhale through the nose, more habit than humor — and for a moment the pacing slowed.

It didn't last. A knock sounded at the door, brief and familiar, and Iroh let himself in without waiting for an answer, already midway through a yawn. He'd traded his day robes for looser sleeping clothes, his hair loose around his shoulders instead of its usual careful arrangement, and he looked, for the first time all day, like a man who actually intended to rest.

"I'm going to bed now." He stretched, unhurried, taking in the scattered armor and his nephew's restless pacing with a glance that missed nothing. "A man needs his rest." He waited a beat, as though hoping the suggestion might land on its own, and when Zuko only kept pacing, he tried again, more plainly, his voice gentler than his words. "Prince Zuko. You need some sleep. Even if you're right, and the Avatar is alive, you won't find him tonight. Your father, your grandfather, your great-grandfather — they all tried, and they all failed."

"Because their honor didn't hinge on his capture." Zuko stopped pacing at last, turning to face his uncle fully, and something raw showed through the anger for just a moment before he covered it again. "Mine does. This coward's hundred years in hiding are over."

Iroh looked at him a moment longer, and Jake, watching from the floor with a gauntlet still in his hands, saw the flicker of something pass behind the old man's eyes — not quite disappointment, not quite worry, something closer to the tired patience of a man who had made this same argument before and lost it every time. He didn't push further. He only nodded once, said goodnight to them both in a voice gone soft with real fatigue, and left the door to swing half-shut behind him.

Zuko stood in the silence afterward, breathing harder than the conversation should have left him.

Jake set the checked gauntlet down with the others and rose, crossing to where the breastplate waited. "Arms up."

Zuko obeyed without argument, the way he always did for this particular ritual, raising his arms while Jake settled the plate against his chest and began working the side straps into place. It was a routine three years deep, familiar enough that neither of them needed to speak through it — Jake's hands finding each buckle without looking, tightening, checking, moving to the next, while Zuko stood rigid and let himself be handled in a way he would never have tolerated from anyone else on the ship.

Jake's hands stayed steady. That much, at least, he could control.

His mind, though, had already started running ahead of his hands, out past the cold hull and the ice and toward whatever lay at the end of this course Zuko had set two nights ago. Southern water. Southern ice. A homeland he had no memory of, that had never once felt like his in any way he could point to — and here he was, buckling a Fire Nation prince into his armor so the prince could go hunt through it for a boy who might be the last hope the Water Tribes had left.

He tightened the last strap at Zuko's shoulder and made himself think about the strap instead of anything else. It held. That was enough for now.

"You'll want the gauntlets too," he said, reaching for them. "Ice throws sparks back at you if your grip's wrong."

Zuko held out his hands without comment, and Jake fitted the gauntlets into place, one and then the other, his gloved fingers working the small clasps with the same steady patience he brought to everything that had an answer. Somewhere beneath the patience, something else sat curled and heavy and unexamined, and he kept it there the only way he knew how — by finishing the strap, and then the next one, and not looking further ahead than his own hands.

***

The cold outside hit harder after the heat of the quarters, sharp enough that Jake felt it settle into his chest before they'd crossed half the deck. Zuko didn't slow for it. Fully armored now, he moved toward the prow with the same restless purpose he'd carried since the light two nights ago, and Jake fell into step a pace behind him, close enough to be useful, far enough to leave him room.

The sky ahead had gone the flat charcoal of deep evening, the kind of dark that never quite settled all the way over the ice, and the ship's engines had dropped to a lower, more careful register as they picked their way through denser floes. Somewhere out there, invisible in the dark, was whatever had thrown that column of light into the sky. Jake watched the horizon without expecting it to give him anything, mostly because Zuko was watching it too, and old habit kept his eyes wherever Zuko's went.

A flare went up without warning — a streak of red fire cutting into the black sky from somewhere out on the ice, bursting into a slow, drifting bloom of light that stained the snow beneath it the color of a wound. Fire Navy. Jake recognized the signature of it even before his mind caught up to what it meant: a wrecked ship out there, and someone on it still alive enough to signal.

Zuko had his spyglass up before the flare had finished falling, tracking something Jake couldn't see at this distance with the naked eye — small shapes, maybe, moving fast across open ice. Jake watched Zuko's whole body sharpen around whatever he'd found in that narrow circle of glass, shoulders forward, breath gone shallow and controlled.

"The last airbender," Zuko said, low, more to himself than to Jake, though the words carried in the cold, still air. He tracked the movement a moment longer, and something in his voice shifted toward something almost like admiration, sharpened at the edges into contempt. "Quite agile. For his old age."

Jake stood a half-step behind him and said nothing, though his chest had gone tight in a way that had nothing to do with the cold. He'd never seen the boy Zuko was watching. He probably never would, not through his own eyes — only through the narrow, secondhand window of Zuko's spyglass and Zuko's certainty. It didn't make the moment feel any smaller.

Zuko lowered the glass just long enough to turn and bark an order at the nearest soldier. "Wake my uncle. Tell him I found the Avatar." The soldier was already moving before the sentence finished, boots ringing against the metal stairs behind them.

The spyglass came back up. Jake watched Zuko's jaw tighten as he tracked something new — the two distant figures no longer walking but running now, cutting fast across the ice toward some fixed point Jake couldn't see. Zuko followed the line of their flight with the glass, adjusting, steadying, and Jake could read the exact moment he found what he was looking for by the way his whole posture went suddenly, coldly still.

"As well as his hiding place," Zuko said, and there was nothing uncertain left in his voice at all.

Jake followed the angle of the spyglass out into the dark, though he had nothing to see it with but his own eyes, and after a moment he found it anyway — a faint cluster of shapes low against the horizon, softened by distance and dark into something that could have been ice formations if he hadn't known to look for a village. Small. Huddled together against the wind the way anything built to survive this cold had to be. Firelight, maybe, or just moonlight catching wrong on packed snow; he couldn't be sure at this range. It didn't matter. He knew what he was looking at.

Something in his chest went very quiet and very cold, quieter than the wind around them, colder than the ice under the hull. He had never seen this village before in his life. He didn't know a single name that belonged to it, didn't carry a single memory of streets or faces or fires that might once have been his. And yet looking at that small dark smudge on the horizon felt closer to looking at a home than anything else he had seen in eighteen years, closer than the palace that raised him, closer than the ship that had carried him for the last three.

Soon, and by his own hands as much as Zuko's, he was going to help tear it apart.

He didn't move. He didn't let his breath change where Zuko might notice it. He only stood there, a half-step behind the prince he had sworn himself to a hundred times over without ever saying so aloud, and watched the ship's prow cut steadily on through the ice toward the village neither of them had any right to call his.

Beside him, Zuko lowered the spyglass at last, his face set into something hard and satisfied, already turning half away to watch for his uncle's approach. Jake stayed where he was, hands curling slowly into fists inside his gloves, and said nothing at all.
