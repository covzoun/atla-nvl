# Chapter 1: The Reality of Exile

The freshwater line had frozen to the rail sometime before dawn, and now it fought Jake for every inch.

He worked it free with a mallet, one careful rap at a time, because a line chipped in a hurry snapped at the ice's mercy and the ship needed every strand of rope it had. Frost had crept over the deck in a fine grey fur. It lay on the rigging, on the belaying pins, on the shoulders of the two men at the bow who stood hunched in their coats like birds. Every exhale went up white and stayed a while.

Three years this had been his life: cold water, colder work, and a ship that complained about both.

His hands complained first. They always did, before his back and before his knees, a deep ache that ran under the leather of the gloves and settled in the scars, as if the old burns kept a record of every degree of cold the sea sent down. Iroh had given him those gloves in his first week aboard and spoken of them once, about the sun and the linen, and never mentioned them again. Jake wore them without discussion. They hid what they were meant to hide and protected what needed protecting, and out here that was the whole philosophy of a thing done well.

He heeled the loosened ice overboard in a grey slide, coiled the freed line, and moved to the next stretch.

Further along the deck, the prince was not working.

Zuko had been pacing since the second dog watch, a tight worn track between the mast and the rail, his boots striking the frost with more force than footing required. He had come up in his under-robe with the sleeves pushed back, and steam lifted off his bare forearms where the heat inside him leaked out without his noticing. His topknot had come half loose in the wind. Every few strides he stopped at the rail of the rolling dark water, and every few strides he began again. Jake kept count of it. He had long since learned not to trust the count.

"Anything?" Zuko asked, not looking at him. He never looked at him when he asked it. He asked the horizon, mostly, and Jake happened to be standing nearby.

"No smoke. No sails."

Jake glanced out at the ice all the same, because Zuko would want to know he had actually checked. "Same as an hour ago."

Zuko made a low sound in his throat that was not quite a word and went back to the rail, knuckles whitening where he gripped the cold metal. Jake let him have the silence. There was nothing useful to say to three years of the same horizon, and Zuko had never wanted comfort so much as company — someone else awake and watching, so the waiting did not feel like something he was doing alone.

Jake picked the ice free of the next stretch of line and let his eyes drift out past the rail, out to where the floes broke apart into open black water and then closed together again into endless white.

It caught him before he expected it to. It always did.

There was nothing about the ice that should have meant anything to him. It was only water, frozen and refrozen a thousand different ways, stretching out flat and blinding under a colorless sky, broken here and there by pressure ridges that threw long blue shadows across the snow.

He had never set foot on southern ice before this voyage. He had no memory of a village, a home, a mother's face, nothing that this landscape should have been able to reach.

And yet something in him kept turning toward it this way, the way a compass needle turns without asking permission. Some old current running underneath his ribs recognized the cold, the light, the particular gray-white color of the sky, however his mind flatly refused to.

He'd stopped trying to name it a long time ago. Naming it only made it worse.

His hand had gone still on the rope. He noticed that before he noticed why, and made himself start moving again, working the frost out of the fibers with more attention than the task required. Cold got into a man's head out here, that was all. Iroh said so often enough, usually right before pouring tea. Long stretches of white could make anyone imagine things — faces in the ice, voices in the wind, the phantom sense of somewhere they had never been. It was the kind of thing that happened to sailors on long postings. It did not mean anything.

He told himself that, and mostly believed it, and went back to his knots.

Down the deck, Zuko had started pacing again, faster now, his bare arms goosefleshed despite the heat still coming off him in faint visible waves. Jake watched him from the corner of his eye — the tight shoulders, the restless hands that kept curling and releasing at his sides, the steady fall of his gaze across the same stretch of horizon. Three years of exile had worn most of the softness out of him. What was left underneath was harder, thinner, strung tight enough that Jake had learned to feel the tension in the air before Zuko ever said a word about it.

He finished the last of the rigging and stood, knees stiff from the cold, and moved himself toward the rail. It was a distance he had learned by feel over three years of shared decks and shared silences, and he kept it now without thinking about it, the same way he kept his hands moving so the cold would not settle into them.

The ice stretched on ahead of the ship, endless and empty and strangely familiar, and Jake made himself stop looking at it.

It was only the cold. It had to be. He turned his attention back to Zuko, and to the horizon Zuko was so certain would eventually give something up, and let the ship's slow grinding progress through the floes fill the silence between them.

There was a knock at the ladder well behind the ship, and three men came up the companionway in a single file, their boots echoing on the grating.

They were the crew he had known for two years now, the ones who had watched him learn the ship from nothing, the ones who grumbled about the cold and the work and then worked it anyway.

Their voices rose in complaint as they stepped onto the deck, their laughter died when all three looked at him. They parted to let him pass, and that told him everything he needed to know.

Jee, the lieutenant, was at the back of the group, his arms crossed over his oil-stained coat, and he looked at Jake the way a man looked at a clock that had been right twice already and was now simply handing out the same numbers.

The others said nothing. They did not have to. Jake knew what they thought.

Three years, and two of them spent walking this same circle of empty water after a boy who might not exist. Zuko staring at nothing until his eyes watered, and now the nothing had answered back, and the answer had come down out of the sky over southern ice.

Southern.

He took up the coil of line and began walking his rounds again, because the boilers wanted stowing and the barrels wanted counting and a man who is useful is a man who does not have to stand still.

Under his gloves the scars had begun their deep burn again. They always did, in the hour after hope. He had never found a name for that which he was willing to say out loud. So he put the name down where he put everything else, and counted the barrels, and did not look west.

---

By the time Zuko went below to change, the light off the deck had gone to early evening. Jake followed him down. The heat hit in the companionway, thick and pumped up from far beneath, a different country from the cold, and Zuko's quarters held it close against red banners.

Jake knelt at the foot of the bed and laid the armor out in its order: breastplate, shoulders, gauntlets, straps. He checked each strap for fraying before he checked it for fit. It was a task with a beginning and an end in it, and he was grateful for that in a way he did not examine.

Zuko paced the narrow floor between bed and wall, breathing in deliberate counts that kept breaking halfway.

"You think it's him.". Not quite a question, said to the wall, the way he said most things that mattered.

"I think something big enough to change course for happened two nights ago.". The rest, he did not know.

"It has to be him."

That was the voice he used against himself. "Nothing else could put that much light into the sky."

Jake set the breastplate aside and reached for the gauntlets, testing the clasps one by one.

"Either way, you'll want your gear sound when we get there. No sense losing a strap inside the last hundred yards."

Zuko's mouth did its not-laugh, the short exhale through the nose, and the pacing slowed for a count of ten.

A knock, and Iroh let himself in already halfway into a yawn, his hair loose, his day robes traded for something softer. He looked, for the first time all day, like a man intending to rest.

"I am going to bed now."

His gaze took in the scattered armor and his nephew's restlessness and missed nothing. "A man needs his rest."

He waited, hoping it would land alone, and when the pacing did not stop he tried once more, gently. "Zuko." "Even if you are right and the boy is the Avatar, you will not find him tonight." "Your father tried." "His father before him." "For a hundred years, patience has defeated every one of us."

"Because their honor did not hinge on his capture."

Zuko turned on him and something raw showed through for half a second before it was covered again. "Mine does."

Iroh looked at him a moment longer. Jake, on the floor with a gauntlet in his hands, saw the thing pass behind the old man's eyes: not disappointment, not quite worry, the tired steadiness of a man who had made this argument before and lost it every time. Iroh only nodded, wished them both good night in a voice gone plain with fatigue, and let the door swing to.

Zuko stood in the silence breathing harder than the talk had earned.

Jake rose and crossed to the waiting breastplate.

"Arms up."

Zuko obeyed, as he always did for this, raising his arms while Jake settled the plate and worked the side straps home. Three years of the ritual sat in their two bodies: the hands finding each buckle without looking, the prince standing rigid and being handled in a way he would have broken any other man for attempting. Jake tightened the shoulder strap and felt, under his gloves, the old scars pulling with the pull of the work.

"You'll take the gauntlets as well," he said.

"The cold turns the steel into a blade's edge before you feel the cut."

Zuko held out his hands without comment, and Jake fitted them in place, one and then the other, and did not look further ahead than his own hands.

---

He had not known that three years could come to an end inside a single night.

It began with a flare. Red fire climbed off the ice far out to port and burst into a slow bloom that stained the snow beneath it the color of a wound. Fire Navy signal. Jake read the shape of it before his mind had finished: a ship in trouble out there, and someone still alive enough to light it.

Zuko had the spyglass up before the last of it had fallen.

Jake watched the whole of him gather into that narrow circle of glass: shoulders forward, breath going shallow and even, the particular stillness that meant the rest of the world had been put down somewhere. Beside that, his own chest had tightened without permission. He had never seen what Zuko was seeing, and could barely guess at its shape. Only the borrowed second-hand of the prince's certainty, a private view shared out of habit.

"The last airbender," Zuko said, low.

Through the glass: two small figures, at a distance, moving fast over open ice near a darker shape Jake took some seconds to read as a hull half-buried in the pack. Not a wreck anyone had come to claim. An old ship left to the floes, like the Fire Navy left everything down here that it had finished with.

"Quite agile," Zuko said, "for his old age."

The figures changed direction. They had been walking; now they ran, cutting across the ice toward a fixed point, and Zuko tracked their line and steadied the glass and went suddenly, coldly, still.

"Wake my uncle," he told the sentry, without lowering the glass.

It was the old voice, the one that had run through his blood ever since the first scar came clean. It was "Tell him I have found the Avatar.". It was also "As well as his hiding place. "

Jake followed the angle of the spyglass into the dark with nothing to see it with. And found it anyway: a low cluster of shapes on the horizon, softened by distance into something that could have been ice if he had not known to look. Small. Huddled against the wind. Anything built here had to be built that way. Lights in the windows, or ice catching moonlight wrong; at this range the eye took what it was given.

Once, just once, he let himself look at it properly. Three years of grey water and he had never seen a southern settlement, not Wolf Cove, not any cove; his own had been taken from him so early that he carried no street in him, no names, no faces.

And still the thing that came up was not memory's thing. It was older than memory and lower than fear, a pull in his chest toward the small huddled lights on the rim of the world. He understood standing there that nothing out of his past had ever once felt like this looking at him, though the feeling was entirely one-sided, and though the village was not his, and never had been.

He had eighty years of somebody else's war between himself and any of it.

And by dawn, by his own hands as much as the prince's, he was going to help tear it apart.

He made his face. It took a moment's work, the same work it had always taken, and then it was done. The sentry was already hammering at the deckhouse door for the uncle, and the deck was filling, and Jake stepped back a half-pace into his accustomed place and had his hands still and his breath even by the time Zuko turned around.

Below, in the midwatch mess, Kaito was teaching two new hands the knot Jake had taught him, and the mess kettles were steaming over the firepot. The ship ran on the quiet understanding that tomorrow's work was tomorrow's and that the men aboard her would do it.

The prince stood at the rail with the glass shut in his fist, looking at the village he intended to take.

Jake stood half a step behind him, watching the same lights, and said nothing at all.
