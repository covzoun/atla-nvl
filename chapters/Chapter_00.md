# Prologue: The Agni Kai

The tea on the council table had gone cold while the maps were still being argued over upstairs, and Jake, who had poured it an hour earlier, stood against the back wall with the empty pot and tried to be furniture. He was a servant-boy at the back of the rail, and the empty pot was his only thing to hold.

It was the only trick he had ever had. If he did not move and did not speak and kept his hands folded on the pot, the room stopped counting him, and men said things in front of him they would not have said to a boy listening. He had heard a great deal that way in thirteen years at the Fire Lord's court, and he had never once been asked what he heard; that was the whole design. Servants got to be furniture, and this one had chosen it.

Below the war gallery the courtyard had been swept for the duel that afternoon: sanded the circle, banked the braziers along the ring, filled the shallow stone basin in the alcove by the west door. That basin Jake had filled himself at first light, because that was his hour and his basin. Now the sand was white as bone again under torchlight, and the men on the maps upstairs had begun to raise their voices.

Prince Zuko's voice carried best.

Jake heard it break upward through the floor: a boy's pitch in a room of grown men, arguing with men who outranked him. He did not need to be told which voice was his. He had known it for years, on the stairs when Jake was carrying a tray too hot to hold and across a courtyard when Zuko sat with him on the fountain steps and talked to him as if Jake were a person. That boy at the ring was not a prince to Jake so much as the one person who had ever looked at him as though he might answer back, and Jake had never thought of it as loyalty so much as the one thing in the room that belonged to him.

"The new division goes in first," a general said from somewhere above the joists. "Untried men. The Earth Kingdom line will spend itself on them, and the veterans walk through the gap."

"They are not a gap," Prince Zuko said. "They are boys from the colonies who have never held a spear. You would spend them like coals and call it a plan."

There was laughter after it, brief and respectful, the kind that closed a door. Then the Fire Lord spoke, and the laughter went out of the room entirely.

A runner came to the doorway. He looked past Jake, as runners always did, and spoke to the steward instead.

"The prince has insulted the war council," the runner said. "The Fire Lord has called an Agni Kai, and the gallery is to attend."

An Agni Kai was a fire duel. Every child in the palace knew the shape of it: two benders in a swept circle, no weapons, no armor, the court at the rail to witness. It settled an insult the way a debt was settled, in front of everyone, and it ended when one of the two could not get up. Jake had seen two, both between officers, both over before he had understood what he was watching. He had never seen one called on a prince.

Jake filled the pot. He did not remember deciding to.

---

They lined the rail above the courtyard: generals, wives, clerks, servants with trays. Prince Zuko came out of the doorway into the torchlight with a mask of hammered brass hanging from one hand, and stopped in front of the stand at the ring's edge. He was thirteen, had always passed for older, tall for his year and already built through the shoulders, and stood looking at the brass mask for longer than the moment wanted.

Then he set the mask down on the stand and turned to the doorway with his face bare to the fire.

Jake, at the back of the rail with the pot, saw his face go smooth and bare and Zuko look up at the gallery, across it really, to the tall doors on the far side where the Fire Lord would come through. Jake knew that face, and the set of the boy's shoulders when he was trying not to show what he felt; he knew the smaller things too, the way Zuko's silence could be either anger or fear, with no way to tell which until it was too late.

"You will not fight me," said a voice from those doors. It did not rise. It did not need to. "You will fight me, Prince Zuko, or you will learn what a prince is who refuses his father."

"I won't fight you." Zuko's voice had cracked on it in the war room once already; he held it whole now. "I said what I said at the council table, and I meant it. Punish me for the words if you mean to punish me, but I won't brawl for you in the sand like a—" He stopped, and finished smaller. "I won't fight you."

"Then you will learn respect," said the Fire Lord, "and suffering will be your teacher."

The men came through the doorway robed in gold, and his father walked out onto the sand with the torchlight running up the sleeves of him. Zuko did not take up the guard his master had taught him, not properly; he stood with his arms loose at his sides, mouth working once, and whatever he saw on his father's face made him stop working it.

Jake watched the fire gather down the long line of the Fire Lord's arm.

There was no order given. There was no bell. The fire lifted and drove across the sand toward the boy in the center of it, and Jake was already moving before he had decided to move. There was a boy in the middle of it, and that boy had once talked to him across a courtyard as if Jake were a person. He put his feet into the ring in four strides and threw the morning's water up off the basin stone in a single flat sheet between Zuko and the torchlight, green with basin-stain, like a lid being raised.

For half a breath he had a roof of water over the prince's body, arms up and hands open and flat beneath it, palm to the sky, and it was the easiest thing he had ever done. Then the fire came down on it anyway.

The water took the force and the force went through the water, because there was too much fire and not enough of it, and what survived boiled. Jake had put himself between the blast and the boy; the water took the worst of it off the prince's side and gave it to him instead. The steam came down onto his uplifted palms in one scalding sheet, palm and thumb and out every fingertip, as if the fire had gripped him through and shaken. Jake screamed at the ceiling of sky he had built, and the basin's water broke apart and fell on him and the sand and the prince in a warm dirty rain.

The blast still found Zuko, across the shoulder and the side of the face, and drove him down in the sand with his arms up, still holding them up, and the gallery made a sound like a cloth being torn. Jake heard himself make no sound at all. His hands were cold on the pot, and the cold ran up his arms into his chest and kept going. He was thinking, quite calmly, that Zuko's hands were open, they were open and empty in the sand, and that he was not going to do anything else.

He was still standing when the guards came off the rail, because he had gone stupid on his feet the way a struck animal does, with his hands curled up against his chest and the skin of them sliding. A man beside the ring shouted it before the others took him down.

"Waterbender!"

It did not belong to him.

He was a boy who had carried water to a basin at first light, and somebody had mistaken the water for him. While he was still trying to find the error, the guards had his arms and were hauling him backwards out of the torchlight.

A servant with a tray of plates came out of the west door and stopped dead on the threshold, standing there with the tray as if the yard had ended at her shoes. The last thing Jake saw before they took him around the wall was the braziers, the white circle, and Zuko on his side in the sand with his good arm working under him, turning his burned face away from the gallery.

At the rail's end the prince's sister stood with her arms folded, watching the wet stone rather than the boy being dragged out of it. Jake could not tell what she had made of anything at all.

---

They put him in a room off the laundry with a grate for a window and did not come back for two days. Jake spent them being useful about not understanding: he made up the thin cot they had put in there, though nobody had told him to, and counted the floor stones, and worked his hands over the wash bucket, palms up, because the linen had gone stiff with salt and the skin under it was hot. The water below them rose an inch and hung there, holding to the shape of his palms. He did not lower them into it. When the sting had eased he set his arms at his sides, and the water lay back into the bucket, and that was the whole of it.

The burns were the plainest part. The skin had gone tight and shining across both palms and wept through the linen it had been wrapped in by some orderly who had not looked at him while doing it. Blistered and cooling, they looked, to Jake, exactly like what they were: he had held fire above his head and his hands were open at the time. He turned them over in the lamplight and memorized them, and made no connection he could say out loud. A man's face came into his head instead, Zuko's father's arm going down the length of the ring. He made himself stop thinking it.

Through the grate on the second morning he watched guards cross the yard to the servants' wing in twos, then come back out with a woman and a man in unmarked kitchen gray, hands not bound but very still, going where they were pointed. The guards had gone in through the door Jake had left by every day of his life — Haru and Emi's door, since he was small enough to carry. Haru and Emi had taken him in when there was no one else to, and had kept him when there was no one else who would. A steward came at noon with a folded list and stood in the corridor reading it, and asked the names of everyone who worked the wing and the hours they had kept. Every name was asked in a tone that had already decided the answer, because the household's error now had a face and the face was a boy. Jake turned that arithmetic over eleven times in one afternoon until it stopped meaning anything.

Jake had one thing left to try. When the steward came back past the grate, he asked it small and early, already half-apologizing.

"Will the rooms need the winter blankets aired? September damp gets into wool, and no one will think of it now."

The steward looked at him for the first time. The look lasted long enough to frighten him, and then the man wrote something on his list and went away.

The third morning the lock came off and the door opened onto a hall Jake had been in four times in his life when he was delivering something.

Uncle Iroh stood in the doorway.

He said Jake's name, as though it were a cup he was setting down carefully.

"The Fire Lord has settled the matter of the boy who interrupted his Agni Kai," Iroh said. "You will consider it settled the same way: say nothing of it to anyone. Not to me. Not to the prince."

"Where am I to go?" Jake asked.

"You are assigned to the prince's household," Iroh said. "Going north. You will want your coat."

---

"By the Fire Lord's command," the herald read from the terrace steps, "the prince's topknot is cut, and the prince goes north, and does not come back."

They cut the prince's topknot in the fountain court at noon, in front of the staff that had not already been dismissed. Zuko knelt with his chin up while the fire-knife took it, and his face was swathed to the hairline in linen, and the hair fell on the stones between his knees. Nobody spoke. Somewhere behind Jake a woman was crying with her mouth shut, which is a sound you feel in your own teeth. He spent the ceremony hunting for her in the crowd instead of watching, because hunting was bearable and watching was not.

"The two from the kitchen were taken before dawn," a woman on the dismissed line said, without turning her head. "Nobody is to speak of it."

He did not see Haru. He did not see Emi. He stood at the back with a bundle of his own in both bandaged hands and listened for their two voices. The ceremony ended, the servants were turned out through the side gate, and they were not in it.

He had one thing in the bundle that was his and nothing else besides: a braided cord charm Haru had pressed into his hand the week before, when everything had still been ordinary, meant as a fairing for nothing at all. Jake had been carrying it in his sleeve for eight days, and it was the stupidest object he owned, and he never once considered leaving it with the winter blankets.

At the docks the ship stood taller than anything in his life had ever been. Crew moved past him in pairs with crates and did not look at the burned-handed boy at the prince's shoulder, and the one man who did look had his wrist taken by Iroh without any shift of expression at all, and did not look again.

"I'm not on the ship's list," Jake said, half way up the gangway, because it had only just occurred to him.

"You are on it now," Iroh said, and did not slow down.

On the deck, out of the crowd's road, Iroh sat him down on a coil of rope and put his hands in his lap and worked the wraps off them with fingers that were quicker than Jake had ever seen them be. The palms shone and tightened in the sea wind. Jake's whole body arched at the cold of it, and he did not make a sound, which cost him more than the sound would have.

"These are plain, and they are ugly," Iroh said, and drew a pair of plain ugly gloves out of his sleeve. He put them on Jake's hands one at a time, over new linen, taking his time about it.

"Leather keeps the linen where linen wants to go, and the sun does not touch what the leather is over. You will wear them, and they will fit, and that is the end of the subject."

From the rope coil, Jake could see where the prince was: Zuko at the far rail with his bandaged head between his shoulders, alone in a lane the crew opened and closed around him like water round a stone. Nobody approached him. Nobody met his eyes. The topknot's hair had been swept off the court stones and there would be no box of it anywhere on this ship. And Jake understood with a lurch: Iroh had put a task into his hands at the exact moment the prince's own had nothing left to hold.

The lines came off the dock. The ship's bell rang twice and the shore began to move.

Jake stood at the far rail in his new gloves with his bundle against his chest and watched the harbor lights go out one at a time. The customs lamps, then the forge row, then the twin fires at the harbor mouth that had not gone out in his lifetime. Between him and the stones the wake closed over itself in a long slow swallow, black into black.

The island put its shoulder between them and the last of the fires went.

Ahead of him there was open water, and it lay flat as far as the dark ran.
