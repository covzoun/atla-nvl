*Above the river valley, late afternoon.*

The wind off the gorge had a bite in it, and Katara pulled her parka's fur collar up around her ears.

Appa flew steadily. Sky and cloud and the tops of far hills slid by on either side, and behind them the roar of the falls had dwindled to a murmur and then to nothing. On Appa's neck Aang sat with the reins loose in his hands, glancing back over her shoulder every few moments.

He did it again now. She caught his eye.

"Aang," she said. "Yeah?"

She had been rehearsing it since the pirate ship, and none of it came out in the order she had planned. She moved forward until she was close enough to touch his shoulder.

"I'm sorry. About the scroll. About the whole thing." She made herself keep looking at him. "I was jealous. You were picking it up so fast, and I was the one who wanted it so badly, so I said other things instead. Things I shouldn't have. You didn't deserve it."

Aang's face went through several expressions and settled on the open one he wore when he was most himself.

"I wasn't even mad."

"You should have been."

"Maybe a little." He grinned, his bruised cheek making it lopsided. "But you came back for me. That counts for more."

She laughed, a little shakily, and put her arm around his shoulders. He leaned into it as if it were the most natural thing in the world, and for a moment the sky was only sky.

"Ahem," said Sokka. He had been quiet at the back of the saddle for so long she should have noticed. Now he sat up like a man who had waited for the right dramatic moment.

"If you two are done," he said, "I have something."

He reached inside his coat and drew out a roll of yellowed parchment, bound with a faded red cord.

Katara stared at it. For a second her mind refused to put the pieces together. Then she was across the saddle, both hands out.

"The scroll!"

"Don't tear it," Sokka said, and let her take it. "I grabbed it off the pirate captain's belt while he was busy being betrayed. Skill. Stealth. Unbelievable grace."

The parchment was dry and fragile, with a faint scorch along one edge where the fire had come close. The painted figures down its length had not been touched. She turned it toward the light and saw a woman with her hands raised, a ribbon of water between them.

"Thank you," she said. "Really."

Aang laughed, and Appa let out a long rumbling groan that might have been agreement, and for a few breaths the saddle was warm and theirs.

Then Katara looked down at the scroll in her hands, and her mind slid away.

She could see the river as plainly as if she were still standing on that ship's deck. Brown water, black mud, the white-hot flash of Zuko's fire on the shore. And in a gap in the smoke, a boy.

She had not known she was going to say it.

"Did either of you see him?"

Sokka's brows went up. "See who?"

"Him." Her voice came out smaller than she meant. "On the shore. At the end. Not Zuko. The other one."

Neither of them said anything. Aang had turned around on Appa's neck, the reins forgotten in his lap.

"There was a boy," Katara said, slowly, trying to find the right shape for it. "A little taller than me. Dark hair. A dark coat with red lining. He was standing right behind Zuko, and he had..." She stopped. "He was bending, Sokka."

"Bending what?"

"The river."

The wind went past them in a long thin note. Sokka let the silence sit a second before he sat up straighter.

"No," he said. "A waterbender. Working for the Fire Nation. We left the South Pole because there weren't any waterbenders left. You're the last one. That's why we're going north, to find a master. That's the entire plan."

"I know what the plan is."

"So there's no way there's some other waterbender standing next to Zuko."

"He wasn't throwing water around," Katara said. "He was fighting."

Sokka's mouth shut.

"I watched him," she said. "The whole time I was pushing the ship out. I looked back, and there he was. He pulled a whole stream off the shallows with his hands and he didn't swing it. He didn't roll it. It went in straight lines. Like a whip. Like..." She could not think of the word. "Like a fire strike. He had his feet together, almost, and his arms were so tight. Everything was sharp. It didn't *flow.*"

"So he's a bad waterbender," Sokka offered.

"No. He's good." She shook her head. "That's what's wrong. He was really good. And it didn't look like anything I've ever seen."

Aang had been very still. "Maybe someone taught him," he said carefully. "Somewhere else."

"Who?" said Katara. "Who would teach him? I'm the only waterbender I know, Sokka. That's why we're going north." She went on more quietly. "And then I look up and there's another one, close to my age, standing next to the one person who..."

"He might not be a bad guy," Aang said.

"He's with Zuko."

"I know. But he looked..." Aang's forehead creased. "Did he look scared to you?"

"I don't know what he looked like," Katara said. She did. She had seen his face for three heartbeats through a rip in the smoke, and it was fixed in her head like a carving. A pale, tired face, soft in the jaw. He had not looked triumphant. He had looked, of all the things she could have imagined, caught.

His hands had been wrapped. Under the gloves, ragged ones, something had been wrong with them, and the water had circled in and out of his fingers without his flinching once.

She touched her own hands and tried to make them move the way his had. Straight, short, hard. The water in her mind went the wrong way.

Her fingers drifted to her throat and found nothing. The place where her mother's necklace had hung was bare skin.

"We should tell no one," Sokka said, in a low voice. "Not until we figure out what we're dealing with."

"There's no one to tell," said Aang.

"Right. Good."

Katara looked down at the scroll in her lap. The woman on it stared up at her with her hands lifted and the water curling between them, patient and graceful and very old. She knew what that kind of bending looked like. It looked like her grandmother's stories. It did not look like a boy in a Fire Nation coat with every line of him drawn tight as a bowstring.

*Who taught you?* she thought, over the water and the miles.

Appa banked gently, and the sun swung across the saddle. The river dropped away behind them, narrow and bright. The wind was cold on her face. She held the scroll against her chest.

Nobody spoke for a long time, and the bison carried them north.
