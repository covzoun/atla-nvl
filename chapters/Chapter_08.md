# Chapter 8: The Blockade

The air at the hatch tasted of ash.

It was not the sharp wet cold of the open sea. This was a flat, heavy chill with a haze in it, the sky and the water the same dull colour, and in the air a faint bitterness he knew before he could name it. Smoke from somewhere far off, thinned by distance and still there. He had smelled it every day of his childhood without noticing it, and now it told him where they were.

Solstice morning.

The linen was on his left palm and the glove over it. He found his coat, checked that the knife sat right on his belt, and felt Haru's cord in his pocket through the leather without taking it out.

Overhead the ship's bell rang, two strokes and a pause and two more, the signal for all hands who were not at the boilers. Jake went up the ladder.

The deck was quieter than it should have been. Men stood at their stations and spoke in low voices, if they spoke at all. Kaito was at the foremast with a coil of line over his shoulder, and when he saw Jake he started to nod, then looked at Jake's gloved hands and looked away instead. Jake went past him toward the bridge, the engines grinding behind him.

Zuko was at the rail at the bow end of the bridge deck, coat off, armor on, both hands wrapped around the wood. His telescope was tucked under one arm, not yet raised. Iroh stood a few paces behind him with his hands folded into his sleeves. Neither of them turned when Jake came up.

Jake stopped at the bulkhead, just out of the wind, and made himself stand without leaning.

"Prince Zuko," Iroh said. His voice was mild, but Jake had heard that mildness before, and it was never the voice of a man talking about nothing. "Do you know where we are sailing?"

"I know where we're sailing."

"Into Fire Nation waters." Iroh waited. The wind took the words and carried them off across the gray. "Of all the foolish things you have done, nephew, this is the most foolish."

"I have no choice, Uncle."

"You have a choice. You have simply decided not to look at it." Iroh stepped forward until he stood at Zuko's shoulder. "Have you forgotten that the Fire Lord banished you? What do you think will happen if you are caught?"

"I'm chasing the Avatar." Zuko's voice was hard and fast. "My father will understand why I'm returning home. When I bring him the Avatar, he'll understand."

Jake watched the tendons stand up in the back of Zuko's neck. A muscle moved in it.

"You give him too much credit," Iroh said. "My brother is not the understanding type."

Zuko did not answer. He lifted the telescope with one hand and put it to his eye, and the other hand stayed on the rail, white at the knuckles. Jake saw the small stiff set of Zuko's shoulders and knew what it was. It was not anger. Zuko held anger loosely, like a tool he was used to. This was something he was gripping to keep it from moving.

Out beyond the bow the haze thinned for a moment. Jake looked where the telescope was pointed and saw it, far off and high, a pale shape against the gray. It was too big to be a bird. Six legs hung beneath it, tucked up like a cat's paws, and the wide flat tail beat the air slowly. He couldn't make out the riders, only a small dark cluster near the head, like spilled pepper.

"There," Zuko said. He lowered the telescope an inch, and his voice dropped with it. "The Avatar. He's heading straight for the coast." He swung round. "Full steam ahead!"

The order carried down the voice pipe, and the deck shook as the engines took it. Jake felt the grinding change pitch under his boots. It went up and coarse, and he thought of the shaft and the coal ledger, and then made himself stop thinking about any of it.

Iroh's eyes found his across the bridge.

It lasted perhaps a heartbeat. Iroh did not shake his head or make any sign. He looked at Jake, and Jake looked back, and they both knew what the other was thinking. Neither of them was going to say it, because there was nothing to say that Zuko would hear. Jake gave him the smallest nod. Iroh closed his eyes briefly and turned back to the sea.

The bridge was very quiet. Even the helmsman seemed to be breathing carefully.

"Steady," the helmsman murmured, more to himself than to anyone.

The mist began to open ahead of them. It did not lift. It parted in slow gray curtains, like a stage being drawn back, and something dark stood behind it. At first Jake thought it was a headland.

Then the dark line broke into pieces, and the pieces had masts, and the masts were hung with red. Ship after ship after ship, bow to stern across the whole width of the water, a wall with no gap in it. Black hulls, black smoke curling from the stacks, and on every foredeck a catapult raised against the sky with the fire pots already lit.

Jake had grown up under that flag. He had watched it raised over the palace courtyard every dawn for as long as he could remember. He knew what the Fire Nation did with those who stepped outside the line it drew, and he had learned very young not to ask what came after.

His right hand moved toward his pocket and found only leather. He pressed the fingers flat against the cloth where the braided cord lay and held them there.

"Uncle," Zuko said. It was the first word he had spoken since the order, and it came out wrong. "How many?"

"Enough." Iroh stepped up beside him and turned to face him squarely. All the lightness had gone out of his face. "Zuko. You are still in Earth Kingdom waters. Turn back now and they cannot arrest you. Turn back, and we find another way to your father's good graces, one that does not end with chains. I am begging you."

Jake had never heard Iroh say that word to anyone.

Zuko stood with his hands on the rail and did not look at the line of ships, or at his uncle, or at Jake. He lowered his head. His eyes closed. For a moment he looked exactly as he had at thirteen, standing in a stone courtyard with the whole court watching, and Jake's throat tightened.

"I'm sorry, Uncle," Zuko said.

His eyes opened. They were dry and fixed.

"Run the blockade!"

For one breath, nobody moved. Then the helmsman leaned on the wheel, the bell began to ring below, and the ship swung its bow toward the black wall and picked up speed. Iroh stood with his hands open at his sides. Jake took one step forward, and then another, until he was standing where Zuko could have reached him without turning his head. He said nothing, and stood there cold from his scalp to his heels, with the blood warm and slow inside his gloves.

Ahead, on the nearest ship, an arm swung down.

A line of fire rose out of the mist, one boulder, then three, then a dozen, burning orange against the gray. They climbed slowly and almost gracefully, and then they began to turn over at the top of their arc, and Jake saw that every one of them was coming toward the ship.

---
Jake was off the bridge ladder before the first boulder came down.

He didn't decide to go. His feet knew where the work would be, and by the time he thought of Zuko he was on the main deck with the planks jumping under him and Lieutenant Jee's voice cracking out over the noise.

The first stones hit the water short of the bow and went up in white columns that stood for a moment, spitting steam, then fell back across the deck as hot rain. Jake felt it sting through his coat and smelled sulfur and scorched brine. The second volley came lower. One boulder passed over the mast so close that the rigging hummed, and another dropped off the port quarter and shoved the ship sideways. Men went to their knees, and someone was shouting a name over and over that Jake could not place.

"Sandbags," he said. Nobody heard him. He said it again, louder, in the voice he used for the boiler room, and a knot of deckhands turned. "Sandbags from the forward locker. Wet them first. Lay them along the aft bulkhead, the wall at the back of the deck, and around the hatch coamings, the raised lips that keep water out of the hold. Kaito, take the hose crew. Keep the line off the deck."

"Sir!" Kaito was already running.

Jee gave him one quick look across the deck, the look of a man who had stopped being surprised, and went on bellowing. Jake went with them.

The bags were heavy and half-frozen, and he could not close his hands around them. He hooked his forearms under the first one and heaved it onto his chest, and the weight settled against the linen and the leather and turned both palms to fire. He took three steps with his teeth locked and dropped it against the bulkhead. The glove stuck to him when he let go. He felt the left one tear a little further.

The sea was right there, a body-length from the rail, gray and heaving and full of fire-lit foam. It was all he would have wanted. Every low, familiar part of him leaned toward it, and he stood on the deck and did not look at it. A hundred yards off, a hull the color of coal slid by with officers on its deck. Any one of them could have turned a glass his way. Jake dragged the next bag out with his chest.

The third volley found them.

He did not see it land. He saw the light change, from gray to orange, and he heard something enormous tear through the air above the mast, and then the world went sideways. A fireball struck the aft section with a sound like a door the size of a hill slamming. He was thrown against the rail and slid down it, and the sky behind him was suddenly all fire and black smoke rolling forward along the deck in a thick, fast wall.

Heat came after the noise. It hit the back of his neck like a hand. The paint on the rail beside his shoulder blistered and curled.

"Aft!" Jee's voice, hoarse now. "Hoses aft! Get the line on that fire!"

Jake got up. His ears rang with a high, thin note. Men were down all along the deck, some crawling, some not. Through the smoke he made out the aft bulkhead, where planking had split and flames licked up the seam, and beside it a crumpled shape in a crew tunic that wasn't moving.

He ran. Smoke closed over him, bitter and greasy, and he pulled his collar over his mouth and kept going bent double. It was Kaito, on his side with one arm flung out, the hair above his ear scorched to a crust. His chest was moving.

"Kaito." Jake dropped to his knees. "Kaito. Up."

Nothing. A strip of the bulkhead above them came away and fell, flaming, a pace from the boy's boots.

There was no time to think about what it would cost. Jake caught a double fistful of the tunic at the collar and the shoulder and pulled. The pain that went up his arms was so complete that he made a noise he could not have stopped. His fingers did not hold, then did, then the leather ripped and held again. He hauled backwards on his heels. Kaito's boots dragged on the planks, one foot catching the burning timber and coming free. Ten feet. Fifteen. The warm wet inside his gloves had turned slick.

"Take him forward. Away from the bulkhead. Go." Two deckhands came out of the haze with a hose between them, and Jake shoved the boy toward them.

They went. Kaito's eyes cracked open as they lifted him. They were unfocused and wet, and his mouth shaped the word *sir*.

"Stay down," Jake said. "That's an order."

He turned back. The hose crew had the nozzle up and the pump was hammering, but the stream was thin. It broke on the burning wall in a spatter of white steam and did almost nothing. Behind them the fire spread through a stack of rope and crates, bright and fast. Jake grabbed the slack of the hose behind the nearest man and hauled, taking the weight off his arms, and the pain went through his wrists like wire.

*Use the sea.* The thought came whole and clear, in his own voice. He could feel it in his chest, a pull like a hook set deep. *One surge over that wall, and it's out.*

He thought of the black hull a hundred yards away.

"Wet down the crates," he shouted instead. "Not the wall. The crates! Starve it. Pull them off the aft hatch. Don't let it reach the hold!"

The crew swung to it. Men dragged crates away from the fire hand over hand, and Jake went down the line yanking the nearest one by its rope while his palms bled through the leather. Another stone hit the sea close astern, and the ship rang from keel to masthead. Jake lost his footing, caught a rope, and stood up again.

Above him, through a rent in the smoke, he could see the bridge.

Zuko stood at the rail with his back to the deck. He had not moved. Fire rained across the water on both sides, and he faced it with his shoulders level and his head up, like a man waiting to be let in. Jake could not see his face. He did not need to. He had seen that stance before, in a stone courtyard.

They are shooting at him, Jake thought. It arrived slowly, like a bruise coming up. The ships were red-flagged. The men at those catapults wore the same armor as the men standing around him on this deck. They had seen a ship flying the Fire Nation colors, with a prince of the Fire Nation on its bridge, and had let their arms fall. No signal, no demand to stop, no warning flag. Just the stones.

And somewhere on that line a man in command had watched them come on and had said *yes*.

"Engines!" The voice came from the forward hatch, ragged and shrill. The chief engineer hauled himself up out of it, his face sooty to the eyes, one sleeve burned off at the elbow. He cupped his hands and bellowed up at the bridge. "Prince Zuko! The engines are damaged! The aft housing's cracked, we're losing pressure! We have to stop and make repairs!"

Zuko did not turn.

"Do not stop this ship."

He said it flatly, without raising his voice, and somehow it carried across the whole deck. The engineer stood with his mouth open and his hands still raised. Then he dropped them and went back down the hatch.

The hatch cover banged open again. "My prince," the engineer said, and his voice had gone smaller. "The pressure's still dropping."

Jake looked at the men around him. Nobody said a word. Jee was staring up at the bridge with a face that had no expression at all, and then, very slowly, he turned and began shouting orders again. The crew went back to the hoses. They did it without complaint and without hope, because the alternative was standing still.

The next volley fell wide.

Jake saw it only because he happened to be looking: a line of fire arcing high and then dropping short and to the left, into open water. The one after that landed even farther off. The ship was shuddering badly and listing a little, the stern heavy, thick black smoke boiling out of the aft hatch. But the stones were falling behind. Then they stopped falling.

He stood in the quiet that followed and could not make it mean anything.

"Nothing's coming," a man said, as if he were reporting the weather.

The wall of ships lay all around them, close enough that he could count the rivets on the nearest hull. Their catapults were still raised, and not one was firing. Along the whole line the engines were cutting off one by one, and Fire Nation sailors stood at their rails and watched the burning ship go by. He saw no cheering and heard no shouts. They were only watching.

Zuko's ship limped through the gap without a hand lifted against it. The sea closed behind them, and the smoke followed them out, a long black plume dragged across the sky from the broken stern.

Jake's knees finally gave out. He sat down on the wet planks with his back against a crate and his hands held out from his body, palms up, and could not feel them at all. Warmth ran down the insides of his sleeves.

Nobody had stopped them. He did not know why, and he did not like it.

---
The smoke followed them for the better part of an hour.

It rose from the broken stern in a thick black column and bent over in the wind and trailed away across the water behind them, so dark he could see it from anywhere.

Below decks the engines labored in an uneven, wheezing cough, and the deck had a slight list that made every step a small correction. The fires aft were out, put out by the crew with the pump and a great many wet blankets, and now the men sat where they had stopped, along the rail and against the hatch coamings, sooty and silent.

Kaito lay propped against the foremast with a wet cloth over the singed side of his head and a dazed, flat stare.

"Sir, the stones," he said, in a voice like a child's.

"They've stopped," Jake said, and stood up again before Kaito could see his face.

He was coming back along the main deck when Zuko spoke.

"Why didn't he do it?"

Zuko had come down from the bridge. He stood at the stern rail with the smoke boiling up past him, his armor streaked gray and his hair stuck to his forehead, looking back along the wake. Iroh stood a few paces off. Jake stopped near the mainmast, close enough to hear.

"What's he up to, Uncle?" Zuko said. "Why didn't Commander Zhao arrest me?"

"Because he wants to follow you." Iroh's voice was low and tired. It still held the old patient shape of a man laying out a board. "He knows you will lead him to the prize you are both after. The Avatar."

Zuko did not answer at once. His eyes moved along the smoke from the stern to the sky where it fanned out and thinned. Jake saw the change come into his face. It was not relief, and nothing like hope. Something in him had locked into place.

"Then he can follow it," Zuko said.

"Nephew—"

"If Zhao wants to follow a trail of smoke, that's exactly what I'll let him do." He turned from the rail. "Uncle, keep heading north. Zhao will follow the smoke trail, while I use it as cover."

"In what?" But Iroh knew. Jake watched him know. His eyes went to the davits on the starboard side, and his mouth eased into something that was not a smile. "I see."

Zuko was already walking. He passed Jake with his eyes fixed ahead and did not look at him or say a word. Jake fell in behind him.

The boat was a plain, shallow, dark-timbered launch, no more than a dozen feet long, with two thwarts — the cross-benches a rower sits on — and a pair of oars lashed inside the gunwale, the top edge of its sides. Zuko reached the davit and stopped. Jake went past him and got to the line first. His hands were already wrecked, and he could not leave Zuko to fumble at the knots alone.

He knelt on the planks and began to work the cover free. The lashings were stiff with salt. His fingers would not bend around them, so he used the heels of his hands and his teeth, and each pull on the cord laid a thin line of heat across both palms. A dark smear marked the rope where he'd held it.

Zuko saw it. Jake knew by the sudden stillness of his boots. For a moment the prince stood with his hands half-lifted, and Jake waited for him to say *stay with the ship*, or *someone else can do this*, or *you're bleeding*.

He said nothing. He took the other fall of the line and began paying it out hand over hand.

Between them the boat dropped from its slings by degrees. Smoke rolled over the rail in a black blanket. Jake coughed and kept feeding the rope through his fists, and when the keel kissed the water he let the line run and found that he could not make his hands open. He had to look at them and tell them to.

"Jake."

It was Iroh. He had come up behind them without a sound, which he could do, large as he was. He did not say anything more. He looked at the stained leather, at Jake's face, at Zuko's back as Zuko swung one leg over the rail, and closed one broad hand around Jake's shoulder and held it there, not hard.

"Both of you," Iroh said, very low.

"Yes," Jake said.

It was all he could say. He gave the shoulder a single pressure and let go. Jake went over the side after Zuko with his arms trembling and did not look back until he was in the boat. By then the smoke had closed over it, and he could only see the mast, and one hand lifted against the dark.

Zuko had taken the stern seat, facing forward over Jake's shoulder, with the steering line looped around one wrist. Jake took the middle thwart. He hooked the grips under his palms, let the wrap take what it could, and pulled.

The first stroke put a hot wire through both wrists. The second was worse. By the fifth he had found a rhythm that was not so much strength as refusal, long and level and shallow, using the hinge of his back and keeping his hands as loose on the grips as he could. The linen turned wet under the leather, and he felt it pull free of the cut and stick again with every stroke. He kept rowing.

The smoke ran with them for a while, thick as a wall on either hand. Then it thinned, and the wind turned a little, and the stench of burned iron and boiler tar went pale and drifted away. When it was gone the quiet was enormous. He heard the dip of the oars, the drip of water from the blades, and Zuko breathing behind him, slow and measured through his nose. Somewhere far behind them a ship was still coughing smoke across the sky.

The water was dark and glassy, with a slick surface that scarcely moved. It was very close to his hands. He rowed and did not look at it.

"Left," Zuko said. Jake pulled the starboard oar harder, and the bow came round. "Hold. A little right."

That was nearly all either of them said. Jake did not need more. Zuko's voice came a half-beat before the change of the water, and Jake's body answered it before his mind did, shortening a stroke, leaning on the other oar, easing. He had done this beside Zuko before, in drills and in knots of rope on a hundred nights. Now he did it on the open sea with no hull under them, and it was the one easy thing in a very long day.

He looked over his shoulder once. Zuko sat bolt upright with the steering line tight around his wrist and his eyes fixed ahead on the haze. The smoke had gotten into his hair and left black streaks along the scar. His back was as straight as a post.

Jake looked at that rigid line and understood something plainly, as he might have read it off a chart. Zuko had burned everything: his ship, his crew, his uncle's deck, the whole clean road home. There was nothing to go back to.

He turned back to the oars and pulled.

The mist came on gradually, then all at once. It crept in from the sea and closed the horizon, and the world shrank to a circle of gray water about the width of a courtyard. Jake rowed through it with his head down. The air had a new smell, faint at first, that he took for the last of the smoke until it thickened into a flat sour sulfur catching at the back of his throat. Beneath it lay something deeper, hot and stony, like a forge with its doors shut.

"There," Zuko said.

Jake looked.

The mist ahead of them was not uniform. A dim bulk showed through it, black against the gray, and as they drew nearer it rose out of the haze one piece at a time. First a long curve of dark shore, shaped like a drawn bow. Then a cliff above it, and above the cliff a mountain with its upper slopes gone red where the light struck them from behind.

And on the mountain's shoulder, tier above tier, stood a temple. Its roofs rose in sweeping red-and-gold eaves, each layer narrower than the last, as if a building were trying to climb the sky. Bronze doors glinted at its base. From this far it looked as quiet as a painting.

Jake's hands stopped on the oars.

He knew buildings like this. He had walked past their cousins in the palace gardens as a boy, scrubbing the steps with a rag while the sages in their robes went by without noticing him. He knew the incense, the deep brass gongs, and the cold flagstones under his knees.

"Don't stop," Zuko said, without turning.

Jake bent his back and rowed.

The shore came up fast. The water went from glass to black stone, a rim of jagged volcanic rock heaped in broken plates. The boat slid in on a long slow swell. Jake brought the oars in, braced his boots, and felt the hull grind onto the rock with a groan that carried a long way over the silent water.

There was a stair cut into the rock above the landing, a long switchback of black steps worn smooth in the middle by generations of sandals. Zuko took it two at a time. Jake followed, and the heat came down to meet them as they climbed, rising off the stone until his coat clung to his back and sweat ran into the linen under his gloves. Every time he curled his fingers to balance he felt the cuts pull open along their old lines.

At the top, a narrow service door stood ajar in the foot of the outer wall. Zuko put a palm flat to it, listened, and slipped through. Jake went after him into a corridor of dark red wood and stone where the air smelled of burned sandalwood, of hot iron, and under both, faintly, of sulfur. A single oil lamp burned in a niche. There were no guards. No sages. A deep, slow vibration came up through the floor into the soles of his boots, as if something very large were turning over in its sleep.

He knew that smell. Under every Fire Nation temple the incense had been burned to cover it. He had never once thought to wonder why.

Zuko stopped at a junction where a staircase climbed to a gallery on the right and a wider passage ran left toward a great arched opening. He stood a moment with his head cocked, and from somewhere ahead came a faint sound, a shuffle, a swish of cloth. Then he turned and met Jake's eyes.

He did not speak. He lifted two fingers and pointed at the stair, then at the floor. *Up there. Stay.* Then he pointed through the arch, and the line of his mouth went flat. *I'm going in.*

Jake understood. He held the escape route, the corridors above, the road back to the boat. It was what he was for. A dozen arguments rose in him and went quiet at once, because none of them would have changed the look on Zuko's face.

"I'll be here," he said instead, very low.

"Don't—" Zuko said, and nothing more came out of him. He hesitated. It was brief. His eyes dropped for half a second to the gloves, to the dark leather, and rose again, and Jake could not tell whether the end of that sentence was *come in* or *get hurt* or something else. Then Zuko was gone through the arch, and the sound of his boots faded into the dark.

Jake went up the stair.

The gallery above ran along the inner wall of the temple like a balcony, a narrow ledge of red-lacquered wood with a rail at waist height and heavy pillars at intervals. He found a place between two of them where the shadow was deepest and set his back against the wall. Then he checked the working knife with his thumb and settled where he could reach it without looking.

It was an old, familiar weight. The sheath lay flat against his hip, and the grip's linen wrapping lay against the cut in his right palm like a nail. He tightened his fingers on the hilt anyway, and in the dark he counted his heartbeats.

He got to ninety. Then the shouting began.

"Seal it! Seal the doors!"

It came from below and a long way off. A boy's voice, high and breathless, cut off mid-word. Another voice, deeper, rough with effort. Then the slap of bare feet on stone, a rising babble of older voices.

Jake's chest went tight. That was Zuko.

He moved. He crept along the gallery in a crouch, keeping the wall at his shoulder, until the rail fell away from the pillars and gave onto open air. Below him lay a vast chamber, tall as a ship's hold, floored in polished black stone. At the far end loomed a pair of enormous doors, bronze-bound and carved with a flame pattern, taller than any gate in the palace. A knot of men in red-and-gold robes crowded in front of them, their heads shaved and bound in topknots, their hands raised in confusion.

And in the middle of the floor stood Zuko, with his arm hooked around the boy.

The airbender. Jake recognized him at once, though he'd only ever seen him at a distance, wearing orange on the deck of a flying beast or leaping from a rooftop at Kyoshi. He was small. Smaller than Jake had remembered. His head was shaved bare, and a faint blue arrow ran up it. His feet kicked in the air, and he struggled like a trapped bird, all elbows and desperation.

"Seal the doors!" Zuko shouted again. His voice cracked on it. "Now!"

The sages looked at one another. For a long moment nobody moved. Then the boy twisted, and a gust of air burst out of him and shoved Zuko half a pace backward, and the arm around his chest slipped. The boy dropped to the floor and ran, straight at the great bronze doors. A pair of the sages flung themselves at the iron handles. The doors began to swing closed. The boy dove through the narrowing gap, and the doors met behind him with a boom that shook dust from the rafters.

Bolts shot home. A latch of black iron the size of a man's arm swung down across the seam. The sound went on after the doors were still, a long ringing fade like a struck gong.

Zuko stood in front of them with his hands open at his sides.

Jake started to rise. Every part of him wanted to be down there, at Zuko's shoulder, in the smoke and the noise, doing something. Then, from the far arch, he heard armored boots.

He froze, one knee on the gallery floor.

They came through the archway in two lines, a dozen of them, in the red-lacquered armor Jake had grown up looking at. Their faces were hidden behind plate. The line parted in the middle, and a man walked down it at an easy pace, hands behind his back, the plume on his helmet nodding with each step.

Zhao.

He crossed the black floor as if it were his, and the sages scattered from his path. He stopped a dozen paces from Zuko. Jake could not see his face from above, but he saw his shoulders, relaxed and almost lazy, and he saw Zuko's whole body shift into a fighting stance and hold there.

"It was a noble effort, Prince Zuko," Zhao said. It was warm and rich, and pleased. "But your little smokescreen didn't work."

Zuko's hands flared. A curl of flame spat from one fist. Before it could grow, two of the guards on the nearer line took a single stride each, one catching Zuko's right arm in both gauntlets while the other took the left, and bore him down against their weight. He roared and kicked, and a third guard hit him across the back of the knees with the butt of a spear and he dropped to his knees on the stone.

Jake's hand closed on the knife hilt until his teeth ached.

"Two traitors in one day." Zhao stepped closer. He almost seemed to be enjoying himself. "The Fire Lord will be pleased."

*Traitor.*

The word fell into Jake like a stone into a well. He heard it strike the bottom, and then it was still falling. He had heard it before, in the servants' passages, in lowered voices, about men in other years who had stood in the wrong place and never came back to the kitchens. He had never heard it said of a prince. He had never heard it said of Zuko, who had spent three years on a ship's deck with his jaw set, doing the only thing he had ever been told would bring him home.

*Traitor.* Not exiled. Not banished. Not an embarrassment, a shame, a mistake to be kept out of sight. A traitor was a man you did not bargain with, and every man who followed him was a traitor beside him. And Jake, who had slept three years in an alcove off a prince's cabin, was a thing that burned well under that name.

An iron manacle snapped shut over Zuko's right wrist with a clank that rang off every pillar in the chamber. Then the left. A chain ran between them, short and thick, and the guards hauled it taut.

"You're too late, Zhao!" Zuko said. His voice was raw, but it did not shake. He lifted his chin and stared up at the commander. "The Avatar's inside, and the doors are sealed."

Zhao glanced at the doors. He took his time about it. Then he looked back down at Zuko, and Jake could hear the smile in his voice.

"No matter," he said. "Sooner or later, he has to come out."

He turned and raised one hand, and the guards fanned out across the chamber and took up positions facing the great bronze doors, their palms low and ready. Two stayed behind with Zuko, a third over him with a spear.

"Wait," Zhao said. "Let him come to us."

Jake knelt on the gallery floor in the dark with the knife hilt under his palm and his heart slamming against his ribs. It was twenty feet to the rail and thirty to the floor, and a dozen firebenders and a commander who wanted them both dead stood between him and Zuko's chains. He could not spend one drop of water without bringing the fleet down on every person he knew. He could not move without being seen.

Zuko turned his head. His eyes swept the shadows of the gallery, once, slowly, and passed over the place where Jake knelt, and did not stop.

Jake shifted his weight onto the balls of his feet. His palms were bleeding freely now into the leather, and he tightened his grip until it hurt, and kept it there.

---
The doors began to glow.

It started at the seam. A thin line of orange crept down the join between the two great bronze leaves, bright as a wire pulled from a forge, and the light spread along the carved flames until every one of them seemed to burn. The latch of black iron went from dull to red to white, and Jake saw it sag. A low hum rose through the floor into the gallery planks under his knees, and the smell of the temple changed. The sandalwood vanished, and in its place came a clean, scorching smell, the air above a blade on a whetstone.

"Steady," Zhao said. "Be ready." Below, his men shifted. Palms came up. Twelve firebenders took a half-step back from the doors and set their feet, and Zhao himself did not move.

Zuko was on his knees between two guards, head lifted, chains hanging between his wrists. He was staring at the doors. Whatever was in his face was not triumph and not fear. Jake could not read it from this far, only see that Zuko had gone entirely still.

The hum became a roar.

The doors blew open.

They did not swing. They burst outward off their hinges in a single blinding flash and slammed against the pillars on either side, and a wall of light poured through the gap. Jake threw his arm up across his eyes and still saw it, red through the skin of his sleeve. The heat arrived a heartbeat later like a physical blow, forced him back against the pillar, and the rail beside him cracked.

Something stood in the doorway.

He saw it between his fingers, a shape in the center of the light. It was a figure, vast and upright, taller than any man he had ever seen, wrapped in a mantle of fire. Its outline was a man's, in layered robes and a high ornamented headdress, but it did not stand on the floor. It hung above it, and its eyes were two white furnaces. Everything around it was lit from within, and all the shadows lay behind the men on the floor as sharp as cut paper.

He had expected the boy. He understood, in some part of him not yet capable of fear, that the boy was in there somewhere. The glowing thing had the same shaved head under its crown and the same lines on its brow. Beyond that, nothing about it was a child.

It raised one arm.

Fire answered. It rolled out of the figure in a single, roaring tide, higher than the pillars, wider than the chamber, a wave of white and gold with a red heart. It crossed the floor faster than Jake could follow. The firebenders tried to meet it with their own flames. Jake saw their blasts go out like sparks hitting a river.

Then the wave took them, all of them, and flung them backward across the black floor like leaves. A man in red-lacquered armor went past the gallery rail at head height and struck the far wall. He was on his back on the stone, one arm across his face, his cloak smoking.

The wave struck Zuko. Jake's heart stopped.

It did not burn him. It rolled over him and the two guards holding his arms, and the guards were hurled aside. Jake watched the chain between Zuko's wrists go bright, then white, then drip. The manacles sagged and fell from his arms in glowing strings, and Zuko pitched forward onto his hands, free.

He did not stay there for even a breath.

Zuko surged up off the floor with a cry and ran. He did not run for the doors or the glowing thing in them. He ran for the stair that climbed to the gallery, and he took the steps three at a time, with his head down and his bare wrists scorched red, looking straight at the shadow where Jake had been kneeling for the past hour.

Jake stepped out of it.

He did not remember deciding to move. He stood at the head of the stair with the light shining straight through his coat, and when Zuko reached the landing Jake caught him by the forearm with his bleeding glove. He hauled him over the last step and felt the whole weight of him come against his own. The pain in his palm was a bright white nail. He held on anyway.

"This way!" he said.

Zuko gasped something. It might have been Jake's name. He did not stop to say it twice. He turned with Jake at once, and they ran.

They had barely cleared the gallery when the temple began to shake.

It started deep. A groan came from the roots of the mountain, a sound too big for any ear, and then a series of hard slow concussions, like a giant's fist beating on a door. The floor bucked. Jake nearly went down and caught himself against a pillar, and ash and plaster rained from the ceiling. Below them the chamber was filling with light, and the light was changing color from white to deep, ugly red.

"The floor," Zuko shouted. "It's going!"

It was. Behind them a crack ran across the gallery planking from wall to wall, and the boards on either side buckled upward. Heat poured out of the gap, and with it came a glow, thick and slow and orange. Magma. Jake had seen it once in a drawing in a servant's book, a neat red line under a cross-section of a mountain. This was the inside of the world.

They ran. The corridors that had been silent half an hour ago were alive with noise: the roar of the mountain, the screech of tearing timber, the crash of falling stone. Walls leaned. Jake ran with his arm clamped against his chest and his boots skidding on the hot flagstones. Zuko ran beside him, keeping pace, one hand always an inch from Jake's sleeve.

Twice they reached a junction and Zuko slowed. Twice Jake pointed without a word, left, left, because he had counted every turn on the way in, and Zuko turned as his hand said.

At the third junction the floor ended.

A crack had split the corridor from side to side. It was already six feet across and widening as they stood there, and the glow that came up out of it was so bright that Jake's eyes watered. On the far side the passage continued, sound and whole.

A heavy beam of dark wood spanned the ceiling above the gap, thick as a mast. From a crusted iron ring on it hung an old temple hoist rope, thick as a wrist, for hoisting the brass incense burners to the upper floors. Its tail had fallen on their side of the crack and was looped around a rusted post, tied off out of long dead habit.

"We can't jump it," Zuko said. His face was streaked red-orange from below. "It's too wide."

"No."

Jake drew the working knife.

His hands could not saw with it, and he did not trust them to try. The cut had to come from the rope's weight and not his grip. He laid the rope over the rusted post, where its own heaviness held it taut, and set the blunt-nosed edge of the blade against the underside of it.

Then he hauled the free tail across the steel with both arms from the shoulder. Something in the rope gave with a creak, and the line sighed down along the beam and hung free over the glow. The motion had cost him nothing in the fingers and everything in the palm; the leather went hot, and a wet line ran down inside the right sleeve.

"Take hold of the rope," he said. "Not of me."

"What?" Zuko stared.

"Hold on!"

Zuko caught him around the waist with one arm and a fistful of his coat, his free hand wrapped around the hanging rope. Jake wound the other end twice around both his forearms, crossed them at the wrist, and leaned back, and they kicked off the edge together.

The pain was enormous. It came up through both arms and met somewhere behind his eyes and exploded. The leather burst along the left seam. The rope hung all its weight on the crossings above his wrists, not the ruined grip at all, and he knew even while he swung that he could not have held a thing. His jaw was locked so tight his teeth squeaked.

For one long moment the whole world hung underneath them in a bowl of orange light, and the heat on his boots was a living thing. Then the arc brought them over the far lip. He shook his arms apart, the rope unraveled and went back, and they struck the stone on their knees and rolled. Behind them the old floor gave way with a long ripping crack, and the beam sagged and fell in a shower of sparks.

Jake lay face down for a count of two, with his hands curled against his chest and a rushing sound in his head.

"Up," Zuko said, and he hauled Jake upright by the arm, his voice shaking. "Up, come on. Move!"

They moved.

The last stretch was a blur. A stair, a door, another door, and then a rush of cold air that struck Jake's face like a slap, and the night opened above them, huge and black. They burst out onto the stone steps they had climbed a short while ago, and behind them the whole mountain was glowing.

Jake looked back once, and the temple was sinking.

It went slowly, with a dreadful grace. The red-and-gold roofs tilted and settled one over another, and the bronze doors at its base slid into a widening seam of fire. The upper tiers folded inward. A great gout of lava heaved up the mountainside and spilled over the cliff in a slow river, and the whole scene lay reflected in the sea below.

"Don't look," Zuko said hoarsely. "Run!"

They ran down the black stair, slipping on the hot stones, and flung themselves onto the landing rocks. The boat lay where they'd left it, pulled up on the glassy shingle with its oars in the locks. Zuko hit it with his shoulder and shoved, and it grated off the rock into the water. Jake went over the gunwale, landed on the thwart half on his side, and fumbled for an oar. Zuko came in after him and set the second one in the lock.

"Row!"

They rowed. Jake did not know how his hands closed on the grips. He knew only that every pull was a long scream with no sound behind it. Zuko worked beside him, teeth bared, shoulders heaving. The heat of the island pressed at their backs like a furnace door, and the sweat on Jake's face turned cold as the first open-water spray came over the bow. The spray on his lips tasted of salt, and under it, ash.

They did not stop until the roar had faded behind them to a dull, steady growl and the air around them smelled of nothing but sea.

Jake let the oars fall slack and slumped over them. His head hung. The inside of his gloves was warm and wet, and his fingers shook uncontrollably. He did not try to stop them.

Beside him Zuko had stopped rowing. He sat with the oar across his knees and his face turned up to the sky, and Jake lifted his head to see what he was looking at.

A shape was climbing out of the glow above the island. It was huge and white and shaggy, with a broad flat tail and six legs tucked beneath it, rising on the hot air with a slow, powerful beat. It crossed the red face of the mountain and entered the dark. On its back, for a moment, Jake saw a few small shapes huddled together, tiny against the sky. Then the stars took it.

Neither of them spoke.

Zuko did not move. His eyes followed the empty place where the shape had been. His wrists were bare and angry red where the manacles had melted, and he held them loosely in his lap, away from his body. In the glow behind them his face looked very young, and for once entirely unguarded. Jake had seen him furious, and seen him broken. This was something else. Zuko looked like a man who had watched the ground open.

Jake looked away first.

There was no ship. He turned that over once, flatly. The ship was somewhere to the north, trailing smoke for a commander to follow, with Iroh on its deck and a crew who were not going to wake from this. They had one boat, two oars, and a coat each. Behind them a commander who could order fire on a prince was pulling himself up off a floor. Ahead lay a dark sea and a country that had a name for what Zuko was now.

The island burned. The little boat rose and fell on the swell, and nobody rowed.
