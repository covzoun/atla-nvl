# Chapter 1: The Reality of Exile

The freshwater line had frozen to the rail before dawn, and now it fought Jake for every inch.

He worked the hemp free with a wooden mallet, one measured tap at a time. Frost chipped in a hurry would snap a brittle line straight through, and the ship needed every length of sound rope left in her lockers. Hoarfrost coated the iron decks in a dull grey fur. It clung to the mast rigging, to the wooden pins lining the rails, and to the shoulders of the two lookout hands hunched in padded coats at the bow. Every breath rose white in the bitter air and lingered.

Three years out of the Fire Nation capital, this had become his world: frozen iron, salted canvas, and a thirty-man patrol cruiser held together by stubbornness and spare rivets.

Jake's hands ached before his knees or back did. The deep cold worked straight through worn deerskin into the scarred undersides of both palms, tightening old burn seams that ran from his wrists to his fingertips. General Iroh had handed him those oversized gloves during their first week at sea, muttering about salt spray and winter sun, and had never once asked to inspect what healed beneath them. Jake kept the leather buckled tight. The gloves covered what had to stay hidden from strangers and cushioned what still split in dry wind, which out here served well enough.

He was sixteen now. He had climbed the gangplank at thirteen with both hands bound in pine-salve linen. The palace rolls had struck his name off every list after he jumped into Ozai's Agni Kai ring to keep his prince from burning to death on the sand. Most of the ship's thirty sailors had spent those three winters deciding what to make of him. The stokers called him useful. The deck watch called him the prince's shadow. One older hand with a scarred ear called him the palace water-boy once beside the galley scuttle. He stopped calling him anything after Jake tipped a full mop bucket over the man's boots and walked down the ladder without turning around.

He kicked the shattered rime through the scuppers, the deck drains along the hull. Then he coiled the cleared line onto its peg and strapped his plain working knife tighter against his hip.

Further forward along the port rail, Prince Zuko was not working.

Zuko had walked the same six-stride track between the foremast and the iron bulwark since midnight. His heavy boots struck the frost with more force than the rolling sea demanded. He wore only a sleeveless linen under-tunic despite the southern gale, sleeves rolled past his elbows while faint heat shimmered off his skin into the grey wind. The ragged black topknot at the crown of his shaved skull had worked half loose. Every third turn he stopped short to scan the broken sea ice, then pivoted back to pace again. Jake kept silent count of each turn in the back of his head without meaning to.

"Anything?" Zuko asked, keeping his gaze locked south over the empty water. He rarely turned around when he spoke on watch; he addressed the horizon and expected Jake to hear him anyway.

"No smoke," Jake said, stepping up to check the drift for himself because Zuko mistrusted answers offered from memory. "No sails either, sir. Same white shelf we raised before midnight."

Zuko let out a sharp breath through his teeth and gripped the frozen iron rail. His broad shoulders stayed drawn tight toward his ears, coiled round the scarred left side of his face like an old bruise that never settled. After the Agni Kai, Ozai had offered his banished son one impossible condition for returning home: capture the Avatar, the master of all four elements who had vanished before the Hundred Year War began. Three years of exile had passed since that courtyard ring; two of them had gone to scouring blank southern seas on thin rumors and cold charts.

Jake returned to the next frozen mooring line and kept his mouth shut. Comfort annoyed Zuko; steady company kept him anchored, so he did not feel like a boy shouting alone into wind.

Jake cracked ice from the stiffened hemp and let his gaze drift out past the iron prow, where black channels split the flat pack into drifting slabs before grinding shut again beneath a leaden sky.

The pull in his chest caught him off guard.

There was no reason the bottom of the world should stir his blood. It was only frozen seawater piled in jagged blue-shadowed ridges under pale daylight that barely warmed a man's brow. Jake had been raised from infancy behind volcanic stone walls in the Fire Nation capital by Haru and Emi, two palace servants who had never spoken a word about any southern coast. He remembered kitchens, red tile eaves, and Zuko throwing stale bread to turtleducks—never a snow tent, never a face shaped like his own.

Yet every week they crept deeper past the outer floes, a quiet tug answered beneath his ribs. The sharp mineral smell of sleet, the particular flat blue under the pack ice, the ringing silence between squalls—his mind dismissed them as weather, while his body recognized them anyway.

His gloved thumb paused over a knot. He caught the lapse before Zuko glanced back, shook frost from his cuff, and drove the wooden mallet down harder against the ice. Iroh blamed cabin fever for ghosts on long voyages. Sailors staring at blank white water long enough began seeing faces in drifts and voices in wind. It meant nothing.

Behind him, the iron hatch to the ladder well clanged open and three off-watch hands climbed into the grey chill, boots clattering up the steep steel steps. The oldest sailor was complaining loudly about damp straw in the crew berths and greasy salt fish in the morning pot until he spotted Zuko motionless at the bow. His voice cut off mid-word. All three men edged past the foremast along the far side of the deck, stepping carefully around the prince the way deckhands skirt a half-tamed hound whose temper has turned sour.

"Morning watch is clear along the forward scuppers, Lieutenant," Jake said as the final man hauled himself through the hatchway.

Lieutenant Jee paused at the top step, arms folded across a soot-stained watch coat, four days of poor sleep hollowed dark under his eyes. Jee's gaze traveled from the neatly coiled freshwater line to the shivering bow lookouts, then settled briefly on Zuko's bare, steaming forearms.

"Didn't go below again," Jee muttered under the wind.

"Walked the foredeck straight through third bell," Jake said.

Jee blew mist into the cold air. "Two years chasing frost at the bottom of the map, and he paces it like the sea owes him an apology."

"He'll ask to shake out the foresail once full light breaks," Jake kept his voice low, pitched under the hull rumble. "Two extra knots matter to him right now."

Jee eyed him sideways, weighing the warning without resentment. "Tell your prince we trim canvas after sunrise. I'm not sending half-frozen hands up icy yards in the pitch dark for two knots the current will steal back by noon."

"I'll pass the word along," Jake said.

"You'll put it in soft leather first," Jee grunted, though his mouth twitched without humor. He barked orders aft toward the quarterdeck, scattering the relief watch toward ropes and buckets before Jake could reply.

Jake coiled the last thawed hemp length, eased the stiffness from his knees, and crossed toward the forward rail. Down the deck, Zuko had resumed pacing faster than before, dark hair blowing across his good eye while cold sweat glistened behind his scarred ear. Three hard years had stripped every softness off the thirteen-year-old boy who used to argue over siege scrolls in the palace gardens. What remained was lean, tight-wound wire waiting for an excuse to spark.

Jake halted two paces off Zuko's left shoulder—a distance measured over three winters of shared decks, near enough to catch an order without crowding his heat.

"Only winter wind," Jake murmured to himself, fixing his eyes on the empty southern pack while the iron hull groaned through brash ice beneath their boots.

---

Night fell early, and the impossible came out of the ice just past dusk.

Jake was down in the cramped galley, warming his fingers near the coal stove while the ship's cook ladled thin barley broth into tin mugs. By evening the lower decks always smelled the same: damp wool, coal smoke, salted pork grease, and thirty tired men cooped too close together in iron ribs. Two stokers sat hunched over a worn Pai Sho board on an overturned crate, arguing over a corner move. Jake leaned against the warm bulkhead and debated whether to fetch Iroh's evening teapot before climbing to his sleeping alcove.

Then the cruiser lifted beneath their heels.

A long shudder hummed through the steel hull plates. A shout rang out from the foredeck lookout, followed by running boots drumming overhead. Jake set his tin mug cleanly on the counter and vaulted up the iron companionway two rungs at a time.

Cold wind slapped his face as he cleared the hatch. Every man off-watch stood packed shoulder to shoulder along the left rail, silent and staring.

A pillar of blue-white light stood upright on the southern horizon.

It rose straight out of the unbroken pack ice like a lance forged inside a furnace. Though thinner than a lighthouse beam, it bleached the low cloud deck from below in a pale electric bloom. Every ridge and jagged shelf for miles flashed sharp silver-blue against the dark sea. Across the cruiser's rail, frost sparkled like cut glass, and the weary faces of thirty Fire Nation sailors went slack with sudden wonder.

The shaft held steady for one long indrawn breath. Then it narrowed, faded upward into the clouds, and left the southern rim blacker than before.

Cold prickled across Jake's ruined palms inside the leather, sharp and sudden as touching wet iron in midwinter.

"Finally," Zuko breathed from the foredeck steps, rising cleanly out of his crouch by the rail. He spun toward the small folding table wedged beneath the lee of the deckhouse, where brass lanterns swayed beside a battered wooden Pai Sho board. "Uncle, do you realize what this means?"

Iroh sat calm as a harbor stone inside a quilted bearskin coat, his grey topknot neat above two red woolen shawls, holding a wooden lotus tile poised between thumb and forefinger. He tilted his head down to peer mildly over the tile's painted rim toward his nephew.

"I won't get to finish my game?"

"It means my search is about to come to an end!" Zuko strode two paces closer, gesturing fiercely back toward the darkened southern sky where faint luminescence still lingered against low clouds. "That light came from an incredibly powerful source. It has to be him!"

Iroh let out a measured sigh and set his tile gently down upon the board, resting the carved airbending spiral cleanly atop a red firebending piece. Across the deck, two deckhands exchanged wide-eyed glances and shifted closer to the hatchway.

"Or it is just the celestial lights," Iroh answered smoothly, smoothing his beard while gesturing toward the empty camp stool opposite him. "We have been down this road before, Prince Zuko. I don't want you to get too excited over nothing. Please, sit. Why don't you enjoy a cup of calming jasmine tea?"

"I don't need any calming tea!" Zuko snapped, loud enough to make the lantern glass hum, his single gold eye blazing above the tight scar. "I need to capture the Avatar! Helmsman—head a course straight for the light!"

Up inside the armored bridge housing, the helmsman gripped the wheel spokes, hesitating a fraction of a second as his glance dropped toward the retired general seated beside the deckhouse.

"Plain to it," Iroh called upward in the level, unmistakable cadence that had once moved siege lines at Ba Sing Se. "Bring her south-southeast."

Orders cracked down the deck at once. Men bolted for halyards, the heavy ropes lifting the black canvas foresail, while a runner vanished down the iron ladder toward the boilers. Canvas snapped full overhead. Beneath Jake's soles the steam turbines shifted pitch, biting deeper into dark water until the iron bow drove straight into thicker shelf ice with a slow grinding roar like splitting oak.

Jake waited until the watch cleared the foredeck, then crossed to stand beside the Pai Sho table where Iroh still rested both plump hands inside his fur sleeves.

"He won't sleep tonight," Jake said.

"No," Iroh agreed, watching red lantern glow ripple over dark churning water ahead of the bow. "And if we reach that shelf and find only cracked blue ice and a seal hole, the morning will be very ugly for everyone aboard."

Jake waited. He had spent three years learning that Iroh hid his sharpest warnings inside polite comments about weather and tea.

"Bring him a warm pot to the prow anyway," Iroh added, sliding the wooden airbending tile neatly back into his leather pouch. "He will refuse to drink it, of course. Still, a hot porcelain cup keeps frozen fingers occupied, and busy hands hold less temper than idle ones."

Jake nodded once and ducked below decks.

Heat slapped him like a wool blanket the instant he descended the ladder into the boiler room. Coal dust hung thick in red furnace glare. Outside, a polar gale tore past the iron hull ten feet away. Inside, the chief engineer stood stripped to a sweat-soaked singlet, barking damper timings at two bare-chested stokers shoveling crushed anthracite into twin grates.

When Jake stepped down from the final rung, the chief didn't need to ask whose order he carried.

"Full steam ahead," Jake said plainly above the hiss of relief valves. "The prince wants us against that ice shelf by sunrise."

"Sunrise." The chief spat black dust onto the grate floor and wiped his brow with an oily rag. "Tell your prince pushing both boilers wide open against winter pack burns four days of bunker coal between dusk and daylight. Ask him if he intends to row us back to Ketu Harbor when the bins rattle dry."

"You tell him, Chief," Jake said, pouring boiling water from the galley kettle over fresh jasmine leaves inside an iron pot.

The chief had spent twenty years chewing coal dust and kept his own counsel; he stared at Jake three slow heartbeats, then shook his head with a tired grimace. "I'll argue fuel when the gauge drops low enough for his ears to work. Right now he's deaf. Take your tea upstairs before the deck freezes solid under your boots."

Jake carried the warm iron pot and single thick cup back into the slashing gale.

No trace of blue light remained along the southern clouds now—only black swells grinding pack floes against the cruiser's armored stem. Zuko stood fixed at the peak of the prow, leaning forward into freezing spray with both bare hands white along the rail. When Jake set the steaming cup between his palms, Zuko closed cold fingers around the porcelain out of habit without lifting it to his lips.

"Jee says the foresail canvas stays reefed until full dawn," Jake said, pitching his voice clear of the wind. "He won't risk a frostbitten boy sliding off the upper yard in the pitch dark for one knot."

Zuko's lip twitched—brief and crooked, scarcely an inch short of wry amusement. "He told you that."

"He told the mast near me," Jake corrected.

"They think I hear the ship and ignore the men." Zuko rotated the cooling tea cup slowly between his scarred palm and whole one, eyes never leaving the black horizon. "Leave the yard alone until light. We'll need every hand steady when we drop the boats. This coward's hundred years in hiding end tomorrow."

Jake didn't reply. Three years had taught him that arguing with Zuko's certainty only sharpened it into stone, and besides, part of Jake dreaded what waited behind that light far more than any empty sea. He took his accustomed place half a stride behind Zuko's left shoulder, letting the steady thunder of the engines carry them deeper south.

---

By first light the wind had dropped, leaving the wooden upper deck glazed under a thin sheet of black ice, and Zuko cleared the midship planks to drill.

Two off-watch firebenders squared off near the main hatch in padded leather sparring vests, breath misting steadily while they waited. General Iroh sat bundled to his chin in a cushioned chair hauled out from his cabin, wool blanket draped over his knees and a steaming mug of spiced tea cradled inside both palms.

"Again," Iroh called calmly.

Zuko lunged forward without waiting for a count. He moved fast—he had always been faster than boys twice his size—whipping a wide crimson crescent across the frozen planks. The pair of guards pivoted cleanly, crossing braced forearms to shatter the flame before it caught their cuffs, then answered together with twin low jabs aimed at his ribs and knees. Zuko vaulted cleanly between both bolts, twisted mid-stride, and drove dual arcs downward until the icy timber hissed gray steam beneath the guards' boots.

"No!" Iroh called, rising effortlessly despite his bulk so the wool blanket tumbled unnoticed across the planks.

Zuko froze, chest heaving ragged white clouds into the dawn chill.

"Power in firebending comes from the breath, not the muscles," Iroh said, planting his feet square as he demonstrated a slow, steady expansion across his ribs. "The breath becomes energy in the body. The energy extends past your limbs and becomes—" Iroh snapped a lazy palm forward, sending a thin lance of gold heat across six paces that dissolved cleanly an inch short of Zuko's chin "—fire! You are throwing your shoulders around like a fisherman hauling nets. Get it right this time."

Jake crouched beside the starboard pin rail splicing a chafed lanyard with his belt knife. Up ahead, the familiar ridge of stubbornness hardened along Zuko's neck. Zuko had heard that breathing lesson four hundred times since Year One, yet every morning he heard it fresh as an insult.

"I've been drilling this sequence all morning!" Zuko flared, lowering both fists as heat prickled off his collar. "Teach me the next set. I'm more than ready!"

"No, you are impatient," Iroh replied evenly, settling himself back down into his wooden chair and pulling his blanket neatly over his knees once more. "You have yet to master your basics. Drill it again!"

Zuko turned away with teeth bared tight behind shut lips, then whipped his heel upward in a blistering spinning kick straight at the nearest guard.

The blast wasn't playful sparring heat. A heavy crimson wedge tore across the air and smashed straight through the soldier's crossed forearm guard, slamming the man flat onto his spine against the frozen planks. Thick wool along his chest coat blackened instantly, catching an orange edge of flame that smelled sharply of charred lye and singed hair.

The entire forward watch stopped moving.

Jake didn't run; sprinting across icy timber only made sailors panic around fire. He dropped his knife straight into its belt sheath and strode smoothly toward the fallen man. A thick, wrist-wide ribbon of frigid seawater rose out of the starboard scupper gutter at his heel. One fluid arc of his left arm curled the cold stream across the burning wool, snuffing orange flame in a sharp hiss of white steam. Before the vapor cleared, he looped the remaining ribbon neatly around the soldier's cuffed sleeve to strip lingering heat out of the cloth.

The guard lay blinking up at empty sky, cursing softly through clenched teeth as burned wool smoke curled away in the morning breeze.

"Hold still," Jake said, dropping onto one knee in the slushy puddle beside him. "Let me check that sleeve."

"Nothing wrong with it, sir," the soldier muttered, pride bruised deeper than skin.

"Pull the cuff back anyway."

The sailor obeyed reluctantly. Underneath charred leather, an angry red welt ran three inches along the soft inside of his forearm, a clear blister already rising near his wrist crease where wet wool had trapped steam. Painful for four days; not deep enough to stiffen a tendon.

Jake bit the thumb of his right glove and tugged the deerskin free to check for heat with bare skin.

Every seasoned hand aboard had seen Jake's scars dozens of times while hauling salt casks or working the mess kettles. After three winters together, old crewmen treated his palms like any sailor's crooked nose or missing finger. Near the mainmast, however, a newly rotated fifteen-year-old stoker stood frozen halfway through tipping a sand bucket. The boy's eyes locked straight onto the shiny, taut pale scar tissue striping Jake's open palm. He jerked his glance away toward the horizon, then deliberately forced himself to look straight back, trying hard to act unfazed.

"Down to the galley," Jake told the burned guard, easing his right hand back inside warm leather. "Tell the cook I ordered cold lard salve, clean linen, and an hour off his feet."

"Aye, sir."

"When the hour's up, come back aft and haul the spare stern fender down from its hooks. A seam tore clear open during the last squall. Nobody else has spare hands for heavy needlework today, so stitch it right and keep weight off that wrist."

The tight resentment in the guard's face loosened into practical focus. "Yes, sir. Best fender on the rail by dusk."

Jake hauled him smoothly upright by his uninjured shoulder, watching the younger stoker hurry off with his bucket before anyone else found excuse to gawk.

Behind them, Zuko hadn't budged an inch from his pivot mark. He stood rigid with both fists closed tight against his thighs, staring at the scorched black ring steaming on the planks rather than at Iroh or Jake.

"The sages say the Avatar is the last airbender," Zuko said, forcing each syllable flat and level despite the raw edge underneath. "He must be over a hundred years old by now. He's had a century to master all four elements. I'll need more than basic forms to bring him down!"

"You have been drilling all morning," Iroh noted calmly.

"Uncle—"

"You will need to stay standing past the first breath," Iroh corrected, rising to pluck an iron skewer off his folding tray with a sudden, bright shift of expression. "Very well. I will teach you the advanced set—but first, I must finish my roast duck."

Zuko gaped at the steaming bird held aloft between two fingers. "The duck?"

"A truly magnificent roast duck," Iroh murmured happily, tearing off a crispy wing as he sank back into his cushions. "Besides, the Avatar has waited a hundred years. Surely he can spare ten minutes while I dine."

A short muffled snort escaped one of the younger bow hands before he clamped both palms over his mouth.

Zuko spun on his heel and stalked straight past the mainmast, shoulders square and boots hammering the planks until the iron companionway door slammed shut behind him below decks.

Jake exhaled slowly, rolling the leftover slush from the scuppers back overboard with a small flick of two fingers before slipping his glove all the way home. Around the mast, deckhands bent back to ropes and scrubbing brushes, leaving the scorched timber ring dark against clean frost.

"He isn't angry with the boy, Jake," Iroh remarked gently around a mouthful of duck.

"I know," Jake said, lowering onto an overturned salt barrel beside the chair where the deckhouse blocked the cutting wind.

"Ah. Do you?"

"He hates losing his temper while you and I are watching."

"He hates feeling small when the hour finally nears." Iroh lowered his skewer slightly, his warm eyes sharpening past his usual jovial haze. "Sit straight, Jake. Your left hand is stiff."

Jake dropped both gloved palms cleanly between his knees where the general couldn't measure how tightly the scarred skin pulled. The ship rolled heavily through thickening brash ice, twin boilers humming steadily beneath them all day without slackening speed.

"He will become a great firebender one day," Iroh said softly, staring down the empty deck toward the hatchway. "He has fierce fire in him already. Yet anger is poor fuel; if a man lets rage hold the rudder, his flame drives him like an ill-broken horse drives a green rider." He broke off a tender breast strip and held it out toward Jake without ceremony. Jake accepted it and chewed slowly, grateful for hot grease and salt. "You moved swiftly with poor Masaru's coat."

"Only a scorched sleeve," Jake answered.

"Only a sleeve," Iroh agreed mildly. "And now Masaru has bruised pride, a small blister, and a fender to sew. He will spend tonight boasting about neat needlework instead of wondering whether his prince meant to cook him alive."

Jake swallowed and watched sea mist drift past the rail. Three years aboard had taught him that Iroh rarely struck straight at a target; if you listened patiently through three remarks about poultry, the real point arrived last.

"Some of the newer hands are still jumpy around him," Jake said.

"They fear what his anger might strike next," Iroh said, wiping grease cleanly from his fingers with a silk square. "That is natural around an impatient commander. None of them have feared *you* in two winters, Jake."

Jake kept his eyes fixed on the dark leather over his knuckles.

"In our first year at sea, even the quartermaster crossed to the opposite rail whenever you climbed up from below with fresh bandages around your palms," Iroh continued gently. "Too many whispers had followed us down from the capital harbor. Half the ship didn't know what sort of water-creature I had smuggled out of the Fire Lord's own sand ring."

Iroh folded his silk cloth neatly inside his sleeve. "Now a boy of fifteen stares straight at your bare scar because he thinks looking steady proves his courage. That trust took two years of shared decks and cold watches to build. Which is why I would prefer you remained aboard the cruiser tomorrow if we raise a Water Tribe shoreline."

"I won't stay aboard," Jake said flatly, without raising his voice. "If he lands, I land."

"I know," Iroh said, sounding neither surprised nor eager to argue. "I am describing the ice ahead, not asking you to steer around it. If Zuko goes ashore into a southern settlement, he will want you right beside him. Keep your hood low and strap both gloves tight. Stay at his shoulder through the shouts, and let no stranger look twice at your hands or hear your palace vowels."

"I know how to stay out of sight."

"You know how to protect your prince," Iroh corrected softly, lifting his teacup to catch the last dregs of warmth. "They are not always the same skill."

---

Zuko's cabin stayed stifling even when frost feathered the porthole glass outside.

Iron ducts ran straight behind the aft bulkhead from the main boilers, pumping dry metallic heat into a narrow steel box hung with crimson dragon banners that smelled faintly of cedar smoke and lamp oil. Jake stopped noticing the closeness after three years except right after coming down off a night watch, when heavy indoor air closed over his cold cheeks like a damp rag.

He knelt beside the foot of Zuko's iron bunk and laid the raid armor out across a tarred canvas cloth: curved breastplate, spiked shoulder pauldrons, articulated forearm gauntlets, rawhide laces. Working steadily down the row, Jake pulled each leather strap between his fingers, testing old brass rivets and replacing one salt-rotted buckle near the right collar guard with his belt knife and an awl.

Two steps away, Zuko paced the three yards between desk and bulkhead, breathing deliberate slow counts that kept breaking off halfway through the fourth beat.

"You think it's him," Jake said without looking up from the rawhide punch.

Zuko stopped mid-stride. "What?"

"That's what you've been asking the horizon since dusk." Jake worked a fresh copper rivet neatly through the leather tongue and tapped it flat with his knife handle. "You haven't said the words inside four walls yet. Might as well say them."

Zuko stood stiff beside his brass map chest for three long breaths, poised on the edge of some sharp, princely retort. Then the tension left his ribs all at once, and he sank onto the edge of the narrow cot, elbows braced over his knees. No general's nephew, no scarred commander—just a sixteen-year-old boy hunched inside a hot iron room miles past the edge of the map.

"I think it has to be him," Zuko muttered at his own boots. "What else shoots blue lightning straight into cloud cover out of barren pack ice?"

"Submarine steam vent," Jake offered mildly.

"Don't."

"Fallen star."

"Jake."

"An exceptionally ambitious signal lantern."

One corner of Zuko's mouth pulled sideways before he hid it by pressing the heel of his palm hard against his scarred left temple. Seeing that crooked half-grin loosened a tight knot behind Jake's ribs that had sat there all afternoon.

"Thirty men, one rusting cruiser, and two whole years staring at empty water," Zuko said, his voice rawer without the deck watch listening. "And the single night the south finally answers back, my uncle blames whales and offers me jasmine tea. If we sail up to nothing but broken bergs tomorrow—"

"It's him," Jake said simply.

Zuko raised his head, gold eye narrow and suspicious.

"I don't know it for a fact," Jake amended, setting the awl aside and brushing metal shavings off his knees. "I just know weather doesn't throw straight blue columns ten miles into overcast sky. Even if we reach that shelf and find only wreckage or smoke, getting an honest answer beats chasing ghosts till the coal runs out. One true answer is worth four days of bunker coal."

Zuko sat silent while the hull groaned through shifting pack outside the brass porthole.

"My uncle says things like that," Zuko remarked softly.

"Your uncle gets fifty years' practice on me."

Two brisk knocks sounded against the steel door before Iroh eased himself inside mid-yawn, grey topknot untied around his shoulders and soft quilted sleeping robes belted neatly around his belly. For the first time since dusk, he looked like a man ready for six hours of unbroken rest.

"I am going to bed now," Iroh announced softly, pausing in the narrow doorway long enough for the pointed stillness to settle between them. "Yep. A man needs his rest."

Zuko kept his chin tucked toward his chest. Jake kept both eyes strictly on the oiled breastplate straps.

"Prince Zuko, you need sleep," Iroh added, dropping the mild banter for flat, tired honesty. "Even if you are right, and the Avatar is alive after all these years, you won't find him tonight. Your father tried. Your grandfather and great-grandfather tried before him, across an entire century of open war."

"Because their honor didn't hinge on the Avatar's capture," Zuko shot back, rising fast from the bunk with bright defiance in his stance. "Mine does. This coward's hundred years in hiding are over."

Iroh studied his nephew steadily across the lamplit cabin with the quiet sorrow of an older soldier who had watched boys stride stubbornly toward sharper blades than they understood.

"Good night, Prince Zuko," Iroh murmured, pulling the heavy iron door softly shut behind him.

The latch clicked into place.

Jake lifted the heavy breastplate from the canvas cloth and stepped clear of the bunk. "Arms up. Let me test the new collar strap before morning."

Zuko raised both arms without protest. Three years aboard ship had buried that habit deeper than pride: Jake's gloved fingers sliding iron buckles home without light, Zuko standing still for fittings he would have struck any guard for attempting. Jake drew the replaced shoulder lace tight across Zuko's tunic and tugged once to test the brass tongue under load. Then he unbuckled the catches and set the breastplate onto the iron stand by the door beside Zuko's helmet and gauntlets.

"It'll sit flush over your padded coat," Jake said, setting the last gauntlet cleanly atop the stand so zero steel rattled during night swells. "Get five hours' sleep while the hull stays steady. You'll want fresh legs before morning."

"And you?" Zuko asked, eyeing the open doorway into the narrow side passage.

"First light watch starts in four hours," Jake said, picking up his lamp. "I'll wake you before Jee lowers the boats."

Jake ducked through the curtain into his narrow alcove, little more than an oversized supply closet tucked between the cabin bulkhead and the aft companionway. One thin straw pallet filled the left side, with a bolted sea chest at the foot. Inside lay a spare cotton shirt, waxed sail twine, a whetstone for his belt knife, and Haru's small braided leather cord tucked deep beneath folded socks. Haru had pressed that knotted twine into Jake's palm three days before Ozai's Agni Kai, joking lightly about luck for kitchen errands. Jake hadn't untied the cloth around it since Year One. Knowing the cord sat safe inside cedar wood served better than staring at it in lamp glow.

He sat cross-legged on the pallet, eased both heavy gloves off finger by finger, and studied his bare hands under low tallow light.

White seam lines crisscrossed both palms, pale and raised against tan skin where Iroh's pine salve had knitted ruined flesh shut three winters ago. Cold made the left palm draw tight down its longest diagonal scar. Jake caught his right thumb worrying at the raised edge beside his wrist, stopped deliberately before skin split, and pulled both leather gloves right back on.

Beneath the planks, heavy grinding ice had softened into smoother open crunch. They were closing on coastal shelf water now.

Jake had never laid eyes on a Water Tribe settlement. Carried out as an infant before words stuck to faces, all he owned of the south was a vague tug behind his breastbone whenever grey frost blew across the decks. Weather without memory, blood without names. Yet if they raised a village past the horizon tomorrow, he would buckle a Fire Nation prince into spiked steel. Then he would step down the gangplank half a stride behind him into streets filled with strangers shaped like his own face.

He blew the tallow lamp out and stretched flat along the narrow mat with gloved hands tucked neatly inside his wool tunic. Through the thin steel bulkhead, Zuko shifted twice across the iron bunk, settled for twenty quiet minutes, then startled upright with soft hurried footsteps pacing the floor before climbing slowly back beneath his blanket.

Sleep came late for Jake, frayed with broken flashes of courtyard sand, steam rising off a copper cooling basin under noon sun, and Haru's missing footsteps down a palace stair. He woke twice with his gloved fists pressed tight against his ribs before dawn watch rattled the ladder outside.

---

The morning passed under low slate clouds and silent empty floes. The flare erupted mid-afternoon.

Jake was midships checking grapnel hooks and coiled boarding lines when a red star burst clean above the southern horizon. It bloomed crimson against grey cloud two heartbeats before the faint boom rolled across their deck.

Every sailor along the rail stiffened at once. Every Fire Nation hand knew emergency naval powder by color before anyone spoke a name.

Zuko already had his brass spyglass locked to his good eye.

Jake stepped silently up behind his right elbow, tracking how Zuko's entire body gathered tight behind that narrow brass tube. Weight pitched forward. Breath leveled silent. The world narrowed down to a single gleaming lens, and even without glass, Jake felt his own pulse jump hard against his collar.

"The last airbender," Zuko breathed, low and sharp.

"You see him?"

"Look." Zuko tilted the spyglass half an inch without glancing away, letting Jake step closer beside the rail while keeping his own eye pressed to the rim. Far off across flat grey sea ice rose the tilted, half-buried iron hull of an ancient Fire Navy warship. Some forgotten hulk snapped fast in the pack since the opening decades of the war.

Through the drifting sea haze, Zuko tracked two tiny dark dots leaping straight down from the ruined main deck onto snow below.

"Quite agile," Zuko murmured tightly, "for his old age."

The two distant figures picked up speed across open pack ice, running hard toward a ragged darker smudge clinging against the coastal cove wall miles further east. Zuko swung the spyglass cleanly ahead along their path, froze motionless against the bulwark, and slowly lowered the brass tube an inch.

"Wake my uncle," Zuko snapped over his shoulder toward the nearest sentry, never shifting his gaze off the distant coast. "Tell him I have found the Avatar." He raised the lens one final moment toward the tiny shadowed curve of bay, mouth hardening into cold triumph. "As well as his hiding place."

Jake squinted hard south through bare salt wind until his eyes watered. At first he saw only flat grey snowbanks tucked into a crescent inlet. Then tiny dark mounds resolved against the drift. Hide domes banked low against southern gales, smoke threads curling pale from cookfires, and faint yellow lantern pinpricks winked softly along an ice wall too low to stop a sea lion.

For three long seconds Jake allowed himself to look straight at it.

Three winters at sea, and he had never once seen a living Southern Water Tribe camp. Yet looking across miles of bitter wind toward those fragile smoke threads, a raw ache pulled low beneath his ribs, older than his palace childhood and heavier than fear. None of those people down on that beach would recognize his face or know Haru's name. A hundred years of somebody else's war stood wide and bloody between that shore and the boy wearing Fire Nation wool at Zuko's side.

And before sunrise, he would stand right beside his prince while twenty armed men smashed straight through their snow wall.

"Jake."

Jake smoothed every muscle across his cheeks until nothing showed except readiness. "Sir."

"Boarding gear," Zuko ordered crisply, his single gold eye shining cold and bright above the scar. "Two landing boats first, six hands each. Pick men sure on ice and leave anyone slow behind. I want every gate covered and spears outward. We aren't hauling prisoners. We want the boy."

"Yes, sir."

"Tell Jee boats go into the water one hour before dawn. Hot grain and tea before the ramp drops. I won't have this morning spoiled by shaking fingers or empty bellies. Boilers stay banked hot until full steam." Zuko turned back toward those tiny coastal lights, his voice dropping nearly gentle with terrible patience. "Three years out of the palace, two on this ice, and it comes down to one sunrise."

"Aye, sir," Jake said, and turned cleanly down the ladder well.

Below decks, word of the red flare had already lit the lower mess like dry kindling. Men pulled wool hoods down over ears, checked bootlaces, and filed short iron pike points while tin plates clattered beside the galley door. Kaito, nineteen years old, knelt beside a deck lantern swearing to two nervous younger recruits that hemp rope obeyed polite speech better than curses. He finished by bragging loudly that second-boat detail meant warm coats and easy glory.

"Second boat draws the outer flank on the frozen ridge," Jake corrected calmly from the stair foot, dropping his hand onto his plain wooden knife hilt out of habit while checking both youngsters' cuffs. "Wrap clean wool strips inside your mittens tonight. Sea wind off shore cuts straight through wet leather faster than deck sleet, and a man who can't grip a boat hook gets left behind in snow."

Both younger hands blinked, murmured "Aye, sir," and hurried toward the linen lockers without another word from Kaito.

Jake climbed aft to the stern boat davits, the heavy arched iron arms bolted along the hull edge to swing landing skiffs clear into black water. He found Lieutenant Jee already checking tackle blocks by lantern light. When Jake delivered the pre-dawn timetable, Jee stood silent while his weathered brow worked through coal burn, boiler readiness, and exhausted watches.

"He wants it neat," Jee said, testing the oiled winch pin.

"He wants it finished," Jake replied.

"Not the same trade," Jee murmured, turning his steady gaze straight onto Jake's shadowed hood. "Where are you standing when the bow drops?"

"At his left shoulder."

Jee spat neatly over the frozen bulwark into churning dark water. "Didn't think to ask twice."

Steel chains groaned softly as the first landing skiff swung clear above cold black swells, ready for the pre-dawn drop. Out past the jagged shelf, faint campfires flickered yellow against grey twilight. There, an ancient boy with blue arrows waited inside strangers' walls with no notion of the iron prow bearing down on him through the dark.

Jake stepped into the galley to ensure bread trays stayed warm beside the iron kettle. Then he climbed back into the biting wind and took his place beside Zuko's left shoulder. Together they watched those pale shore lights draw nearer without speaking a word.

*Wolf Cove, that same night.*

Katara had never seen her village so loud before dusk, or so dead quiet after dark.

Two days after the blue-white light cracked skyward off the pack floes, Aang's broad white bison had lumbered slowly over the snow ridge behind camp barely an hour after the red flare died. Only six-toed tracks lingered behind him under drifting spindrift. Out beyond the southern wall, Sokka stood beside a sagging watchtower with seal-fat warpaint smeared hard across his brow. Cold wind rattled dried fish racks behind him while he lectured six sleepy eight-year-olds over wooden practice spears.

Inside the main hide tent, seal-oil lamps burned low and sour.

Katara sat cross-legged beside the heavy fur door flap with her knees pulled tight beneath her chin. Through a narrow crack she watched Sokka pace the empty snow gate with his club strapped across his shoulder. Her arms still ached clear to the elbows. First from fighting the paddle before their canoe snapped in the floes, then from screaming at her brother hard enough to split an entire mountain of blue ice right down the spine.

She hadn't told Sokka the whole truth about that wave, not once, even when he called her weird.

When she screamed over the salt drift, the ocean hadn't thrown itself up by wind or fluke. Water had obeyed her anger, climbing higher than her shoulders and snapping ancient glacier ice clean apart until a glowing twelve-year-old boy with blue arrow marks tumbled out into daylight from a hundred-year sleep.

Then had come penguin sledding down blue tunnels and laughter sharp enough to hurt her ribs. Afterward they had climbed an old rusted Fire Navy hulk perched crooked in the snow, where a tripwire shot a red star straight across the sky.

An hour later, villagers waiting outside the ruined snow gate had met them with pinched, frightened faces instead of smiles. Sokka planted his club in the drift and banished Aang as a Fire Navy spy, while Gran-Gran folded calm hands over her sealskin apron and agreed. "There goes my one chance at becoming a waterbender," Katara had snapped into her grandmother's steady eyes before storming under the tent flap.

"Stop glaring through the seam, child," Kanna's soft voice drifted from behind the simmering broth pot. "Cold air cuts straight across the bedding furs."

Katara let the thick hide drop shut without turning around. Her fingers closed tight around the smooth pale stone resting against her throat on its braided blue ribbon, her mother's necklace, worn thin along the clasp.

"He wasn't signaling them, Gran-Gran," Katara said flatly, staring at dark ash rings inside the hearth stones. "We tripped a rusty wire inside that wreck. He didn't even know there was a war."

"Intent matters little to a warship steaming straight down the coast," Kanna answered evenly, lifting two horn spoons out of warm broth and setting one beside Katara's knee without pressing. "Your father left you and Sokka thirty women and small children to guard through winter. A boy who vanished for a hundred years carries trouble larger than one cove can shelter."

Katara picked up the horn spoon and set it right back down untouched.

She knew every old story Gran-Gran whispered round winter fires: Fire Nation iron hulls emerging out of grey fog, smoking tents, and waterbenders dragged off across black water. Nobody remained anywhere along the southern ice except one clumsy fourteen-year-old girl hiding down by tidal pools so her brother wouldn't laugh when she dropped half a puddle in the snow.

She wasn't afraid of the warship yet, not while anger kept her chest tight. She was sick to her bones of watching the only other bender she had ever met walk out alone across black dunes. He had promised to fly her all the way to the Northern Water Tribe to find a real teacher, and now even that hope had vanished over the ridge.

Outside the skin wall, Sokka snapped a sharp bark at one of the little boys for yawning on watch, his voice cracking high on the final syllable before dropping back into low, stubborn bravado.

"Eat while it's hot," Kanna said gently, banking peat coals low beneath the kettle so zero ember glow showed past the tent poles. "Whatever followed that red smoke across the water won't wait until noon."

Katara bowed her head and wrapped both frozen fingers tight around her mother's cold stone pendant. Outside, wind hissed endlessly along the packed snow wall while she waited in the dark for morning, with no idea how close the iron prow had already come.
