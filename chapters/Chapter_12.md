# Chapter 12: The Quiet at the Center

The gulls left all at once, without a single wheel or cry, and they went inland over the steep gray coast until they were a fleck too small to follow. Jake was scrubbing the forward head, the toilet at the bow, when the deck went ordinary overhead, and by the time he came up the stair the last of them were gone. Nobody on the crew said anything about it. Kaito coiled line with his face turned away, and nobody on the deck said anything at all, and that was how Jake knew.

The ship limped north at half speed with the stern riding a hand low and the port pump going. It had gone to a two-man relay, second watch and third, and Jake sat the last relay himself most nights because his hands wanted the salt water and the salt water asked nothing of the palms. That was one of the few jokes the sea had left him. Fresh water made the scars ache and pull and itch. Sea water went into the splits like a hand into a glove and let him keep them until the pump sucked air.

The coal was counted twice a day now, once by the chief and once by Jake, and by the second evening their numbers had stopped agreeing. The chief's count ran a quarter ton high every time and he read the same pile as burning slower than it would, which on his figures put them nine days out under easy steam, the ship barely under way, and four out under hard. Jake's own figures were seven and three — a day of coal burned since the count in the hold — and the three was the one he believed.

Zhao's ships had been sighted three times at the top of the haze since the river: the same lean hulls, working the same coast, not closing. A deckhand had called one of them "the same wake as the river," and nobody had said more than that.

Iroh came up on the fourth morning with his sleeves rolled and stood at the weather rail with his face into the wind, and stayed there so long that Jake carried a pot of tea up and set it on the binnacle ledge without asking. Iroh drank half of it hot and the rest cold and said, "Thank you," as though it were noon tea in the palace.

"There is a storm to the north," he said then. "A large one. Possibly a very large one."

Jake looked north. The sky was the wrong kind of empty, the pale flat kind, and the sea had gone greasy under it.

"The coast bends east ahead," Iroh went on. "There is anchorage past the bend. I have seen the charts. We run for the anchorage, we let the weather spend itself, and we lose perhaps a day and a half. The ship takes no water she has not already taken. The crew sleeps in a horizontal posture for one whole night, which I am told is what crews are for."

He had put it as a joke, softly, in the voice he kept for jokes. Jake had learned to hear what he did not put in the joke, which was everything else: the cracked housing, the pump, the shaft bearing chewing itself three days from seizing, a crew that had not slept on a flat deck in a week.

"Storms spend themselves in hours at this latitude," Jake said. "If it's the big kind, it spends itself over us."

"Exactly so. It will spend itself on us on one day or it will spend itself on us in one hour, if we outrun its edge. We cannot outrun it at half speed with a bilge," said Iroh, and drank the rest of the tea.

Zuko heard all of it.

They would learn in the next hour that he had heard all of it. He came down the deck fast, with the hood of his cold coat down and his wrists bound in their gray wraps, and he did not slow. The crew heard him before they saw him; long habit had taught them to hear the prince arrive behind any conversation that concerned them.

"We're not stopping, and we're not turning," he said. "I set the heading north. It stays north."

"The anchorage is north, nephew," Iroh said mildly. "East of north. The chart—"

"I've seen the chart." Zuko's voice was flat. His eyes went past his uncle to the wheel and then came back. "The Avatar went north over these waters. There's a fire crew station on that coast or near it, or there isn't, and we're not wasting three days of coal finding out. If the storm comes, the crew pumps. That's what they're paid for. The Avatar comes first."

*The Avatar comes first.* He said it in the tone he used for most of his worst sentences, plain and level, with no cruelty in it at all. That was the part that did the damage, every time. A cruel man says a cruel thing on purpose. Zuko said true things at the wrong volume, and the wrong volume was anything above a whisper.

Lieutenant Jee was four paces off at the wheel, and everyone on the deck watched him hear it.

Nobody spoke. Zuko did not wait for anyone to. He turned, went below to his quarters, and shut the door with the soft, deliberate click of a man who did not want to slam it.

Iroh looked out at the water for a while.

"Lieutenant Jee," he said at last, pleasantly. "Have you heard the word 'tempest' used as any part of a insult? No? It is a good word. Sons of admirals are raised on it."

"Sir." Jee's jaw was working.

"He did not mean it."

"Sir, he always means it. He says it in those words, those same words, and we take water for it."

"He says it in those words because he has no others that will survive being spoken out loud." Iroh folded his hands into his sleeves. "You have been at sea twenty years, Lieutenant. You know what a man sounds like when he means the words as against when the words are all he has left. Give him as long as the storm takes, and he may yet surprise you."

Jee looked at him. He gave no sign of obedience — he gave the look of a man filing a complaint in the only drawer he had, which was his own memory, and Jake watched the drawer shut, and did not like the sound of it.

The swell came up that evening, while the light was still going, one long slow walker of a wave that lifted the bow and set it down again as though it were looking for something. Jake had pump watch. He sat on the sea chest beside the pump with his forearms on the plunger and listened to the water run and did sums about time instead of letting himself listen to the hull, which had begun to talk.

By full dark the horizon had gone to soot in every direction but astern, and the wind had come around into their faces with a wet straw smell in it. The rain arrived all at once, in sheets, with no slack in it at all. The deck lit and went dark and lit again. There were no boats left to bring in and lash, because there were no boats.

Everything up on the mast that could be taken in had been taken in before dusk, and everything that could not be taken in had been double lashed by Kaito and the bosun. Jake had checked it all over twice in the dark with his hands, which could still read rope better than they could hold it.

The storm's manners, they would learn, were these: it began politely.

Jee found Zuko on the bow beneath the spray hood, both hands on the rail.

"So," Jee said. "He was right about the weather. What a surprise. A man who reads clouds. We should get him a hat."

Zuko did not turn around. "Mind how you speak of my uncle, Lieutenant."

"Oh, I'll mind it, sir. I'll mind it right down to the pump handles." Jee came around to his face, took the rail himself, close, an arm too close, the old man's anger that has stopped caring about the gap. "Twenty years I've kept a deck. I was keeping decks when you were learning to walk on one. I've taken orders from a boy of sixteen like they came down off the mountain, and I'll take them again tomorrow, sir, because that is the work. But don't tell me about respect. You talk to your crew like hired muscle, and this man here at your left hand like a—"

His hand opened toward Iroh and stopped itself.

"Say it," said Zuko.

"Like a deckhand's grandmother, sir."

The silence that followed had weather in it. Rain came off the spray hood in ropes. Below them the ship grunted and rose and fell into troughs. Zuko turned all the way around, and the rain ran off the scar and off the good side of his face equally. Jake, three steps back at the corner of the deckhouse, saw his shoulders drop into the coiled readiness he had seen at a hundred drills, low and balanced, hips over the heels. Rain hissed off his fist.

"Then I will teach you some," Zuko said. "Here. Now. Unless you'd like to take it below, out of the water."

"With pleasure," said Jee, and the old man's hands came up.

"Noodles," said Iroh.

He was between them. Jake had not seen him cross the deck, and nobody ever did. He stood in the rain in a soaked robe holding a covered clay pot in both mittened hands as if it were a ceremonial object, and the steam that came out when he lifted the lid was the only warm thing on the ship.

"Have noodles," he said. "It is impossible to fight with honor after noodles. It is possible to hold the pot. I am holding the pot."

The pot steamed between two enemies. Rain went into it and was forgiven.

Jee looked at the noodles, and at the prince, and at the noodles again. Then he took a bowl.

"Twenty years," he said, mostly to Iroh. "A man holds a deck for twenty years and eats noodles in a storm with—"

"Better bowls than fists," said Iroh, and offered Zuko one.

Zuko looked at the bowl for a long time. He was shaking very slightly, Jake saw, all down his arms, and it was not cold only. He took the bowl. He ate standing up, fast, the old ship's way, meal as fuel and no ceremony, watching the bow all the while as though his father's navy might come at him out of the rain wearing his uncle's face.

Iroh put a hand on his shoulder.

"And I do not need your help keeping order on my ship," Zuko said. "Not from noodles. Not from anyone." He lifted the hand off his shoulder, not hard, moving it like a rope he did not want but could not drop. He carried the empty bowl to the bow and stood there with the rain coming down and ate the rest of it standing up, watching the black water.

"A fine prince," Iroh said to the noodles, mildly. He packed the pot under his arm and went below to tell the cook so.

The crew went below with the pot.

Jake stayed until Iroh went below, and then went to the bow rail.

Zuko stood at the point with his hands loose. There was a long minute when the ship rose and fell and neither of them spoke, and the bow wave glowed faintly green in the light they had not lit.

"You should sleep," Jake said.

"So should you."

"I have the pump until second bell."

"Then I'll take it," Zuko said.

"You won't."

"Try me."

"No." Jake said it plainly. "Your wrists are raw under those wraps, and you'll fall down the ladder in the dark, and I'll have to tell your uncle you fell down a ladder in the dark. You'd hate that more than you'd hate standing your own watch. Sit in the dry. Sleep. I'll wake you if anything moves but the water."

Zuko looked at him. Lightning went somewhere behind the clouds, and its thunder came through the deck into the bilge, deep and muffled, like sound through a wall.

"Wake me," Zuko said, "if the wind comes around. The wind coming around means it is looking at us."

"If the wind comes around."

He went below. Jake worked the pump and listened to the ship, and the ship talked, and somewhere past midnight the wind came around.

---

It came around at two bells, and after that it stopped being weather and became a fact.

The wind arrived broadside and it arrived to stay, and it did not gust so much as lean, one long animal weight against the hull that made the whole ship crab sideways through the water with her bow canted off her heading. The rigging sang. Spray went up the length of the ship in a single sheet. Jake came off the pump with his hands burning and his ears full of it. There was no more below and no more above, only the one roaring place that the lamps hung in, swinging on their brackets like slow pendulums that had lost their clock.

They took water. Of course they took water. The port pump kept up for an hour, and then it did not keep up. The chief came up the ladder with his face doing arithmetic and said the word *forepeak*, and Jake went down into it before the old man had finished the sentence.

The forepeak was forward of the cracked housing, the lowest and driest of the ship's bad rooms, until tonight. A seam had let go where the planking met the stem, a hand's width of black water moving under the floor planks with the pulse of the swell. Not deep yet. Just thinking about it.

Jake got the hatch coaming cleared and the scuttle open, and then he did what three years of night pump work had taught him to do without thinking. He put his hands out over the bilge, palms down, and lifted.

The water came up as water comes up when it is invited properly, obedient, sheeting between the deck frames in flat brown ribbons and hanging for a breath above the planks before it poured for the scuttle. He fed it out and drew it again and fed it out, and the level went down the way sand goes down in an hourglass, faster and faster. The two stokers who had come to bail behind him stood with their buckets hanging and did exactly nothing, which was right. Their buckets were for after. His arms were for now.

The level went down. Once, palms-down over standing water in a room with a grate window, he had held a roof of it instead of a floor, and that was a room he did not visit. Somewhere in the middle of it the ship rolled deep and he lost the sheet for a moment, and a bucket's worth slapped down across the plates. One of the stokers — the boy with the burn — said nothing at all and simply took up station on Jake's off side, matching his rhythm with his bucket, pour for pour, until the plates came up dry.

"There," Jake said, when it was dry. "Now it's a matter of the seams. Somebody put cotton in them at the next calm and I will pretend it's been there all along."

The stoker grinned in the lamplight. Overhead something cracked like a rifle shot, and the whole deck seemed to jump half a hand, and Kaito's voice came down the ladder, tight: "Davit wreck's gone over. Somebody get a line on the spar before it takes the plating off her stern."

"On it," Jake said, and went up into the roar.

---

Ships never run the drill they finally need. The spar coming off the stern was a caber's worth of splintered iron and timber, snapping back and forth on two feet of wrecked line like an animal fighting a rope. Every time the stern kicked, it came within a hand of taking the port plating down its whole length. Kaito had hold of a stay and was shouting distance calls. Jake lay out along the poop rail in the rain with two men's hands on his belt and reached.

Drills were for crews with arms. Jake had a river.

He pulled, and the wreck's broken line, whipping loose over the rail, went stiff in his direction, up and over, and laid itself across the deck like something offered. Then he let it go and the crew had it, belaying it, walking it in, and the spar came aboard in pieces without taking the plating off anything. The whole watch hauled on Jake's line and called his name, which embarrassed him, because he was there for one breath and then a hand-off.

"Stow the modesty," the bosun shouted, cheerful and soaked through. "We'll credit the rope with an opinion."

---

Jake brought the broth down at third bell. The cook had made it, and supplies were not so far gone that a prince could not have broth on the worst night of his ship's life, and it was better than watching the cook wring his rag. He took the aft passage because it was the longer way and the dry one, and it passed his own quarters, and it also passed the prince's door.

The door stood open a hand's width, and the lamp was out, and the glow that came through the gap was not lamplight at all.

Four candles burned on the deck by the cot, set out in a square, none of them taller than a thumb. The flames stood up straight as pinpoints, and as Jake watched they all four rose slowly together, higher, wobbling, and then sank, and rose, and sank. Breathing. Zuko sat with his back to the door on the deck with his legs folded, somewhere behind the light, shaping each breath to fit the candles. He did not turn around.

Jake set the tray inside the door and went away without a word.

The crew were not in the mess. They had carried the coal stove out of the galley and set it under the bent lamp, and men were packed around it shoulder to shoulder, closer than men sit on a ship when they can choose. The ship was rolling and the bench seat bounced, and the deck plating was the steadiest ground left aboard.

Kaito was there with the burn on his shoulder unwrapped and drying dark. The stokers. The big island man who could not swim and had never once said so. A deckhand Jake knew by his nicknames and nothing else. Twelve men, with the stove-light orange on their faces and the corridor roaring with water-sound through the hull behind the plates.

Jee was saying something low and furious that Jake did not catch, and the men around him were nodding, and Jake had his hand on the frame of the passage and should have gone up and did not.

"Who does he think he is?" someone said. The big island man.

"A boy." That was the boy with the burn. "A sixteen-year-old boy with a boat, and his father gave him men to lose."

"He doesn't lose them," said Kaito, unexpectedly. "He's never lost one. Not a man. Not once."

"Doesn't mean he isn't spending us," said the boy.

"Do you want to know?"

Everyone turned. Iroh stood at the head of the corridor with two bowls already in his hands and the pot steaming under one arm, and his voice was mild. Something in how mildly the question had been put made the whole pack of them go still.

"Want to know what?" said Jee.

"Why he is what he is," said Iroh.

Nobody answered that out loud. Iroh came down the corridor anyway and sat, cross-legged on the deck plating, an old man among boys, and put the bowls down, and pushed one across the boards to Kaito, who took it. For a moment the only sound was the rain going by outside and the stove eating coal.

"He wanted to sit in the war councils," Iroh said. "He was eleven."

The story had a shape Jake knew from the other side of it, all its landmarks his. He had poured the tea, and swept the gallery floor, and stood in the corridor where generals went in twice and once came out carrying their pride of office in their hands like something they had been told to take home. (Jake had known that room since he was a boy barely bigger than the washtub he scoured in it, and had been sorting clean cups in the passage outside when the argument broke. He had caught only fragments through the doors and the words men repeated afterward.)

"He was eleven," Iroh said again. "He came to me and he said, Uncle, I want to sit in the councils. How can I rule this nation if I don't know what a war is?" Iroh smiled at the remembered rudeness of it. "I told him no. He asked me again the next day, and the next, and I exhausted every answer that a tired old man keeps in stock, and in the end he won. That is the kind of boy he was, and is. I took him in. I sat him by me. I told him, Nephew, these are old men and they are sensitive, keep your hands in your lap and your mouth shut, and nod."

"And he didn't," said Jee.

"And he did not." Iroh looked into the stove. The small flames bent. "A general stood up and proposed a plan. A new division, fresh men, untrained men, marched up the middle of an Enemy entrenched line to soak their first volley, so that the veterans could come around behind and through while the Earth Kingdom was still counting its own dead. Two thousand boys. Farm boys, mostly. He proposed it over tea, in the tone a man uses to propose a different route to the same harbor."

The pieces fit in the dark behind Jake, and he was not startled to find that they fit, because he had been carrying every one of them for three years in the order he had watched them happen. He had been carrying a great deal that nobody had ever once asked him about.

"My nephew stood up," Iroh said. "He said, Those are our men, our boys, and you're proposing to feed them into a sword line like coal into a furnace, and I will not allow it." Iroh's voice stayed level. "He was thirteen. And he was right."

"Right," repeated the boy with the burn, like a word in a language he was deciding about.

"He was right, and it was not his place, and there are prices, and the price was called an insult to the Fire Lord." Iroh drank. "A challenge was issued. Agni Kai. He rose to it as he rose to everything, head-on, on the spot, because a prince does not think about an insult twice. It was to be fought against the general who had proposed the plan, and everyone in the gallery understood that it was a formality. A boy facing an old man with a bad hip. A lesson."

"And?" said the big island man, gruff. He was not impatient: he knew there was no easy exit from any room his voice carried into, and he walked through it anyway.

"And the doors opened," said Iroh, "and it was not the general."

The lamp swung. Somewhere far forward a plate rang off the hull with a boom like a signal gun, and the men's faces moved around the stove and nobody commented.

"His own father walked out to fight him," Iroh said. "In front of the whole court. And my nephew understood, at that distance, what he had actually said in that room, and who it had been to. And he knelt down on the sand and he apologized. He said he had spoken out of turn. He said he had the Fire Nation's interests at heart and he would accept any punishment the Fire Lord saw fit, but that he could not raise his hand against his own father, not for anything. He said it three times. Begging. And the third time was the one that counted, the one that made it final: he said he would not fight."

Jake stood in the dark at the edge of the light's radius with his hands and his shoulders very still, and kept his face settled at workmanlike neutrality, and did not think about his own hands.

"What did the Fire Lord do?" said Kaito, very quiet.

"What would you do, Kaito, to a thirteen-year-old boy in front of your whole court who gets on his knees and tells you no?"

The silence had a texture.

"You will learn respect," Iroh said, "and suffering will be your teacher." He said it in the same level voice, and then he stopped the story and looked up, and the old man's face was nothing at all for a moment, none of his own face on it.

"I looked away," Iroh said. "That is my part in it. The general on the dais beside me, the guards, his sister, the whole gallery — we watched. I looked away."

"I always thought it was a training accident," the boy with the burn said.

The words hung there. Somewhere up on deck something banged loose against a stanchion, and nobody went to see to it.

"It was no accident," Iroh said.

"So he never--" The big island man started it and didn't finish it.

"He was banished," Iroh said, level again, the fond-grandfather voice gone out of it. "Sent north, out of home, out of the country, with a crew's worth of men to carry the weight of him at sea. And no orders to bring him back until he does the one thing that no one in that court believes a boy of his years can do. The Avatar. He is to capture the Avatar and bring him home, and the door of his own country opens when he does, and not one hour sooner. There is no safe course for him and there never has been. He is not chasing glory. He is chasing home."

The stove ticked. Twelve men looked into it.

Then the boy with the burn said, "My brother's in the army. Went north last spring."

"That's different," the big island man said, automatically. He stopped. Looked down at his own hands, big as spades, scarred across the backs.

"Aye," he said. "It's not."

Jee had not said anything since Iroh began, and he sat forward now with his forearms on his knees, a big man folded small.

"Three years," Jee said. "I've called him, to his face, in my head, some words. I've wanted him relieved of his command. Resign, abdicate, whatever the word is for a prince. I've prayed he would fall sick so I could take the deck off him and keep it." He looked around the circle like a man taking a count. "He was thirteen."

"He was thirteen," the boy agreed.

"So the hunt," said Kaito, and Iroh said, "is hope. If you take a man's only rug away he will burn the house down to keep warm. Our prince has one rug. Don't stand on it."

The arithmetic had been going round the circle the whole time Jake was in it, and nobody had done the sum out loud. He had felt it pass him. He had watched it move. He had watched Kaito's eyes come to where he stood and stop and be answered by no one, whatever the question on them had been. The prince had been thirteen. The waterbender who went over the rail at that ring had been the same age, and a servant nobody had ever once asked. Nobody asked now.

"Right," said Jee finally, to the stove, in the voice of a man closing a ledger. "That's the whole of it?"

"That is the whole of it," said Iroh.

"Then that's where it stays. Below this deck. None of it goes above it, and none of it goes ashore." Jee pushed himself up by the bulkhead, taking his years with him. "And nobody wakes the prince to tell him we've had a meeting about him, because that's not help, that's an ambush."

Nobody answered. The stove-light had gone pale. Men looked at their knees and then at each other; Jake felt the shape of the agreement settle over the circle like hatches going closed, unspoken, whole, done. The door of his own story closed as certainly as if a hand had pushed it, and the only sound in him was the corridor, and the rain, and the pump he had not yet gone back to.

He was three steps up the ladder when Iroh's voice found him:

"Jake."

He stopped. Iroh climbed half a rung, unhurried, and looked up at him from the level of the lamps. His eyes in that light were unreadable and old.

"A telling is a roof," Iroh said.

"Yes, sir," said Jake, and did not stop to find out what was under it, and went up into the storm.

---

The second-to-last thing the storm did to them was take the mainmast's topmast line and whip it against the sky like a man beating a carpet. The last thing it did was put lightning into the ship.

The hit came with no warning Jake's body could read, which was the frightening part. There was no gathering of it that a man could duck. The world went white while his eyes were shut against the rain, and the sound did not come through the air but up through the deck and the soles of his boots and his teeth. For a moment after, every sound on the ship was the ringing of it, and the smell was the smell of a forge working.

"Where are we hit?" Zuko was already on the deck with the rain running off him. Nobody had heard him come up the ladder. The candles, Jake thought, and did not have time to think anything else. Jee was shouting it too, and men were answering in their separate voices, aft, amidships, the wheel, and every answer was nothing, and then a voice up by the bow said the word *crow's nest*.

The helmsman had been at the wheel through the middle watch, and the middle watch had ended when a boarding sea went over the bridge and took the wheel half a turn out of his hands. He had done the correct thing and the only thing: he had gotten above it. He was up the mast and onto the crow's nest rails, and he had hung there through the rest of it. Now the nest was fouled with its own rolled canvas, and he was hanging under the rail by his hands, sixty feet up, boots swinging. The mast was rolling through arcs that no tree on land had ever survived.

The whip line still whipped at the masthead whenever the ship rolled, snapping back and forth an arm's length from the ladder.

"Somebody kill that line," Zuko said.

"Kill it with what?" Nobody could climb to it. The mast was not for climbing now; the mast was for holding on while it killed you.

Jake spat the rain off his lip, set his hips at the base of the mast, and pulled.

He had the whole sea to reach with, and the sea obeyed as if it had been waiting for the order to be useful. A single rope of water went up out of the scuppers, over the rail, and around the masthead, and he took the whip line into it gently, with the whole flat of his palm, and the line stopped. It hung against the mast, limp and streaming, going nowhere. Kaito saw it first and pointed, and the whole watch saw it, and there was a half second of silence on the deck that a man could hear, because every man on the watch had just watched a rope die.

Then Zuko was climbing, and Jake forgot the line.

He went up the wet rungs fast and without three points of contact, which is not how anyone climbs a mast in that sea. Jee went up behind him, because Jee was not going to be the man who stood at the bottom of a story. The ship rolled and they went with it, out over the water and back, like two figures hung on a door.

Lightning came down the second time.

Jake saw it as a column of light falling toward the waist of the ship, and he saw an old man walk into it.

Iroh had come out of the deckhouse into the open, ahead of everyone, at his own ambling pace, and he stood in the middle of the empty waist with his feet apart and his arms out. The bolt came down and went into him and through him and out again from his other hand, flung up and sideways, back into the rain it came from. The ship lit twice: once by the bolt, and once by the old man carrying it.

He stood a moment afterward with steam rising off his shoulders and his robe smoking faintly at the sleeves, and then he patted the front of the robe twice, as a man pats a dog, and walked toward the mast.

"Uncle!" Zuko shouted down. "Stay out of the open!"

"It was a small one," Iroh called back. "And I have been wanting fresh air all evening." And then, lower, only to Jake as he passed him: "Nothing is coming aboard tonight but weather. See to that."

The helmsman fell.

He fell at the worst of the roll, out from under the fouled rail, and he fell swinging and he fell quiet. The only reason his name is on no stone is that a sixteen-year-old boy was twenty feet down the mast of a dying ship with his hand already out.

Zuko caught his wrist in the air.

The jolt took them both, ship and men; Jake felt it in the rail he was holding; the whole mast chimed. Zuko did not try to stop the weight — no arm in the world stops a falling man — he bent his arm with it, rode it, turned a dead straight fall into an arc that ended at the mast collar, where Jee had both hands ready. Jee took the helmsman's weight on his shoulder and went down the ladder with him, calling for the word that would bring the galley blankets and the surgeon's kit, and it arrived, and the man was alive. His wrist, where Zuko had held it, went round with a red stripe for a week.

Zuko came down last, slow, with the mates' eyes on him the whole length of the mast.

No one said anything. The mast creaked and the rain came on and the ship steered itself in wide slow circles. Jee, at the foot of the ladder, turned around, and looked up, and put his fist over his own heart, and then made himself busy with the blankets as if the gesture had been the shadow of something that had not happened. Zuko stood a moment with the rain straight into his face and seemed not to feel it.

---

The wind died between one wave and the next.

There was no warning and no easing. One swell was fury and the next was glass, and the ship came upright in the middle of it like an animal waking, and the rain stopped as if shut off with a tap. Men stood up out of their work slowly, one at a time, as though loudness of any kind might call the storm back. The rigging creaked and settled. Water ran bravely off the yardarms and dripped from the shrouds and drummed on the deck, and the drip was the loudest thing aboard.

They were sitting in the one room the storm keeps, the quiet at the center, and the walls of it went up forever.

All around them the cloud stood in a black ring, arched and lit from inside at intervals by sheet lightning, and above the ring a lid of cloud had drawn back off a small pale circle of sky. The sea inside the eye was long low swells, mindless, unhurried. The ship rode them as if she had been the one to choose to stop.

"Sound off," Jee called, hoarse, from somewhere amidships. "Aft!" "Port!" "Forepeak dry, sir." That last one was Jake's, and he took a breath and let it out and found his knees were shaking and not with cold. "Starboard pump is holding," he added. "Hold it," said Jee.

Kaito sat down on the deck where he was, laughed once at nothing, and put his head back against the mast and closed his eyes. The old bosun vomited over the rail with dignity, in silence, and then spat and went back to work. Sixty feet up, in the crow's nest, the fouled canvas flapped for no reason, and nobody looked up because nobody had to.

Zuko stood at the bow rail, dripping, facing into the ring, and his shoulders were down for the first time in three days.

"Uncle."

Iroh was there. Iroh was always there; nobody ever saw him cross a deck.

"I sailed us into this," Zuko said. He did not turn. His voice was quiet and it shook. "I called it a crew matter and then I spent the crew. You told me what the charts said and I—" He stopped. Started again, worse. "I'm sorry, Uncle."

"Come inside," said Iroh. "You are soaked."

"Uncle—"

"You are soaked," Iroh said again, gently, "and the apology has been received on behalf of the entire crew, the ship, and the storm, which also took no notice."

It was so exactly the wrong thing to say that it was the right thing. Zuko made a sound that wanted to be a laugh and was not, and wiped his face with one raw wrist, and allowed himself to be steered toward the deckhouse door. At the door he stopped and looked once around the deck, at every man standing up out of the wreckage of the night. Jake watched him do it and watched him say nothing, because whatever he had was never going to fit in a voice.

---

Something rose out of the sea ahead of the bow.

It rose the way land does not rise. One moment the eye's swells were empty and the ship wallowed alone in her small pale circle of sky; the next there was a whole shape standing on the water, huge, streaming, white, climbing. It broke upward out of the swell and blocked the pewter light, and men were shouting before anyone had decided anything. It kept climbing, the vast wet arc of its body proof against gravity for the length of one held breath, legs gathered, tail wide and flat against the wind.

A sky bison. Rising out of the storm center, into the circle of it, with three small riders lying flat along its sloping back.

"Sir," Jee breathed. "Sir, that's—"

"I see it," said Zuko.

It beat upward, slow, monstrous, patient. The wind that came off it moved the rain water in the scuppers. At the top of the climb its shape narrowed against the pale circle, and it paused there, spread across the whole light of the eye like a hand across a lamp. One enormous paw hung above the deck the storm had been trying to sink all night.

On its neck the boy turned his head.

They looked at one another. Sixty feet, quiet air between, with the whole ruins of a ship and the whole spent strength of a crew along the bottom of it. There was nothing to shout.

The boy's face was open even at that distance, and there was nothing of the animal or of the hunted in what looked back down at them, only a child looking at another child across a distance neither of them had chosen. Zuko did not move. His hand came up off the rail an inch and stopped, and hung there, and went back down, and Jake watched a whole argument leave the prince's shoulders while he stood still. Then the bison banked away north with a whistle of wet fur and was gone into the wall of cloud.

"Sir," said Jee, hoarse. "The Avatar. Right there. Do we pursue? Our engine—"

"No." Zuko had his hand down and his face forward, and the cloud wall was already turning gray at the edges. "Let him go. Not today." He looked around the deck, at the watch standing with their hands on the ruined rigging, the two men with the pump, his chief holding his own side, Kaito's grin across his wet face. Then he looked up at the mast, at the line Jake had killed for him, hanging limp and dead where it could do no harm.

"Set a course for safer waters, Lieutenant," Zuko said. "And thank your crew for me."

Jee looked at Iroh, and Iroh's face said absolutely nothing at all.

"Aye, sir," said Jee. "Safer waters."

---

The back edge let them out an hour after first light into ordinary gray morning, a rolling sea with wind in it and cloud at gray altitudes that did not signify anything, the simplest weather in the world.

Damage came in with the daylight. The davit loss was final, and a ship with no boats at all seemed suddenly the largest problem on her. The forepeak seam took three turns of cotton and a willingness to be cheerful about it. The galley had taken a boarding sea through its vent, which meant breakfast was biscuit, and nobody cared. The ship still steered, still steamed, still leaked at exactly the rate one pump could keep up with. That sentence was true, and it had to be enough.

---

The morning watch stood down, and the crew did what crews do after a night like that one, which was count one another. It took hours. There were faces with blood on them that had come through the night without a scratch, and scratches that had not bled until the work was over. One stoker wrapped in a blanket under the galley overhang had to be shaken gently, to establish that he was asleep and not past helping. Kaito's singed patch of hair was combed, pointlessly, flat, and he let it happen.

Jake carried the linen and the salve around the watch in the weak light, and tied off the last of it on the boy with the burned forearm. The boy watched him do the knot three times, and then said, out of nowhere, with the whole gray morning for cover:

"That night we left home. The night the ship cleared the capes. I was on the fouled side of the deck, and I saw somebody go over the rail into the ring, and I saw who came up the other—"

"Get that gasket line dried before it takes a set," Jee said, from two paces behind, in the voice of a man mentioning a rope.

"Aye, sir."

The boy stood, and rolled the line, and went. Jake did not look at either of them, and his hands finished the knot on nothing, because there was no hand left to tie, and tied it anyway, and put the tray in order. Nobody on the deck had stopped working. Nobody was going to start.

At the rail, Iroh poured tea out of a pot he had carried personally through a typhoon without spilling it, and handed cups by name down the line of men still pretending to check lashings. When he came to Jake he put the cup in the ruined glove instead of the sound hand, and held on to it a moment longer than tea requires, and said nothing at all, and went on down the rail.

"Tea's hot below," Kaito said from the ladder head, too loud, cheerful, wet-haired and whole.

"I'm coming," Jake said.

He went. Behind them, far astern, the storm still sat on the horizon with its black-green weight of cloud, doing its work on other ships. The little ship went north under a plain gray sky with her pump going, and her crew watched her every inch like men who have just found out what she is.
