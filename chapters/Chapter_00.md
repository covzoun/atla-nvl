# Prologue: The Agni Kai

Jake was thirteen years old and a servant in the Fire Lord’s palace. He had lived there as long as he could remember, raised by two kitchen workers named Haru and Emi who had taken him in when there was no one else. Most days he carried trays, filled basins, and stood where he was told. He was good at being unnoticed. That was how he survived.

On this particular afternoon he stood at the back of the war gallery with an empty teapot in his hands, trying to be furniture. The gallery was a long, high room that looked down over the palace courtyard through open arches. Generals and clerks filled the benches nearer the rail. Jake kept to the wall.

If he did not move and did not speak and kept his hands folded on the pot, the room stopped counting him. Men said things in front of him they would never have said to a boy who was listening. He had heard a great deal that way, and he had never once been asked what he heard. Servants got to be furniture. This one had chosen it.

Below the arches, the courtyard lay quiet in the late light. A sanded circle waited in the center. Braziers—shallow iron bowls of burning coals—stood along the ring, not yet lit. A shallow stone basin sat in the alcove by the west door. Jake had filled that basin himself at first light, because that was his hour and his basin. He had not known why it needed filling. He had only done the work.

The argument started over the maps.

“The new division goes in first,” a general said. “Untried men. The Earth Kingdom line will spend itself on them, and the veterans walk through the gap.”

“They are not a gap.”

The second voice belonged to Prince Zuko. Jake did not need to look to know it. He had known that voice for years—on the stairs when he was carrying a tray too hot to hold, and across a courtyard when the prince had once sat with him on the fountain steps and spoken to him as if Jake were a person instead of a servant. Zuko was thirteen as well, tall for his age, already carrying the weight of a future he had not chosen. To most of the palace he was the Fire Lord’s son. To Jake he was the one person who had ever looked at him as though he might answer back.

“They are boys from the colonies who have never held a spear,” Zuko said. “You would spend them like coals and call it a plan.”

There was laughter after it, brief and respectful, the kind that closed a door. Then the Fire Lord spoke, and the laughter went out of the room entirely.

A runner came to the doorway. He looked past Jake, as runners always did, and spoke to the steward.

“The prince has insulted the war council,” the runner said. “The Fire Lord has called an Agni Kai. The gallery is to attend.”

An Agni Kai was a formal fire duel. Every child in the palace knew the shape of it: two firebenders in a swept circle, no weapons, no armor, the court at the rail to witness. It settled an insult the way a debt was settled—in front of everyone—and it ended when one of the two could not get up. Jake had seen two, both between officers, both over before he had understood what he was watching. He had never seen one called on a prince.

Only then did the courtyard begin to move. Servants lit the braziers. Stewards cleared the lower doors. The gallery emptied toward the rail in a low, ordered rush. Jake filled the teapot from habit and followed with the rest.

---

They lined the rail above the courtyard. Generals, wives, clerks, servants with trays. Below them the sanded circle waited under torchlight.

Prince Zuko came out of the doorway into the light. He stopped at the edge of the ring, thirteen years old and trying to stand like someone older. For a moment he looked as if he expected to face the general he had argued with. Then the tall doors on the far side opened, and the Fire Lord walked out onto the sand.

Zuko’s face changed.

“Father—” he began.

“You will fight for your honor,” the Fire Lord said. His voice did not rise. It did not need to. “Rise and fight, Prince Zuko.”

“I won’t fight you.” Zuko’s voice had cracked once in the war room; he held it whole now. “I spoke out of turn. Punish me for the words if you mean to, but I will not raise my hand against you.”

“Then you will learn respect,” said the Fire Lord, “and suffering will be your teacher.”

There was no order given. There was no bell. Fire gathered down the long line of the Fire Lord’s arm and drove across the sand toward the boy in the center of the ring.

Jake was already moving before he had decided to move. There was a boy in the middle of that fire, and that boy had once sat with him on fountain steps and spoken to him as if he were a person. Jake put his feet into the ring in four strides and threw the morning’s water up off the basin stone in a single flat sheet between Zuko and the blast. The water rose green with basin-stain, like a lid being lifted.

For half a breath he held a roof of water over the prince’s body, arms up, hands open and flat beneath it, palms to the sky. It was the easiest thing he had ever done. Then the fire came down on it anyway.

The water took the force. The force went through the water, because there was too much fire and not enough water, and what survived boiled. Jake had put himself between the blast and the boy. The water took the worst of it off the prince’s side and gave it to him instead. Steam came down onto his uplifted palms in one scalding sheet—palm and thumb and out every fingertip—as if the fire had gripped him through the water and shaken. Jake screamed. The basin’s water broke apart and fell on him and the sand and the prince in a warm, dirty rain.

The blast still found Zuko, across the shoulder and the side of the face, and drove him down into the sand with his arms still raised. The gallery made a sound like a cloth being torn. Jake heard himself make no sound at all. His hands were cold. The cold ran up his arms into his chest and kept going. He was thinking, quite calmly, that Zuko’s hands were open in the sand, open and empty, and that he was not going to do anything else.

He was still standing when the guards came off the rail. He had gone stupid on his feet, hands curled against his chest, the skin of them sliding. A man beside the ring shouted it before the others took him down.

“Waterbender!”

It did not belong to him.

He was a boy who had carried water to a basin at first light, and somebody had mistaken the water for him. While he was still trying to find the error, the guards had his arms and were hauling him backwards out of the torchlight.

The last thing Jake saw before they took him around the wall was the white circle, the braziers, and Zuko on his side in the sand with his good arm working under him, turning his burned face away from the gallery. At the rail’s end the prince’s sister stood with her arms folded, watching the wet stone rather than the boy being dragged away. Jake could not tell what she had made of any of it.

---

They put him in a small room off the laundry that was used for temporary holding. It had a grate for a window and a thin cot. No one came back for two days.

Jake spent the time being useful about not understanding. He made up the cot though nobody had told him to. He counted the floor stones. He worked his hands over the wash bucket, palms up, because the linen had gone stiff with salt and the skin under it was hot. The water below them rose an inch and hung there, holding to the shape of his palms. He did not lower them into it. When the sting eased he set his arms at his sides, and the water lay back into the bucket, and that was the whole of it.

The burns were the plainest part. The skin had gone tight and shining across both palms and wept through the linen an orderly had wrapped without looking at him. Blistered and cooling, they looked exactly like what they were: he had held fire above his head with his hands open. He turned them over in the lamplight and memorized them. A man’s face came into his head instead—the Fire Lord’s arm going down the length of the ring. He made himself stop thinking it.

Through the grate on the second morning he watched guards cross the yard to the servants’ wing, then come back out with a woman and a man in unmarked kitchen gray, hands not bound but very still. The guards had gone in through the door Jake had left by every day of his life—Haru and Emi’s door. Haru and Emi had taken him in when there was no one else to, and had kept him when there was no one else who would. A steward came at noon with a folded list and stood in the corridor reading names and hours. Every name was asked in a tone that had already decided the answer. The household’s error now had a face, and the face was a boy. Jake turned that arithmetic over until it stopped meaning anything.

When the steward came back past the grate, Jake asked the only thing he still knew how to ask.

“Will the rooms need the winter blankets aired? September damp gets into wool, and no one will think of it now.”

The steward looked at him for the first time. The look lasted long enough to frighten him. Then the man wrote something on his list and went away.

On the third morning the lock came off.

Uncle Iroh stood in the doorway. He was the Fire Lord’s brother, broad and calm, with a face that had learned how to hide what it felt. He said Jake’s name as though it were a cup he was setting down carefully.

“The Fire Lord has settled the matter of the boy who interrupted his Agni Kai,” Iroh said. “You will consider it settled the same way. Say nothing of it to anyone. Not to me. Not to the prince.”

“Where am I to go?” Jake asked.

“You are assigned to the prince’s household,” Iroh said. “Going north. You will want your coat.”

---

“By the Fire Lord’s command,” the herald read from the terrace steps, “the prince’s topknot is cut, and the prince goes north, and does not come back.”

They cut Zuko’s topknot in the fountain court at noon, in front of the staff that had not already been dismissed. He knelt with his chin up while the fire-knife took it. His face was swathed to the hairline in linen. The hair fell on the stones between his knees. Nobody spoke. Somewhere behind Jake a woman was crying with her mouth shut, which is a sound you feel in your own teeth. He spent the ceremony hunting for her in the crowd instead of watching, because hunting was bearable and watching was not.

“The two from the kitchen were taken before dawn,” a woman on the dismissed line said without turning her head. “Nobody is to speak of it.”

He did not see Haru. He did not see Emi. He stood at the back with a bundle of his own in both bandaged hands and listened for their two voices. The ceremony ended. The servants were turned out through the side gate. They were not in it.

He had one thing in the bundle that was his: a braided cord charm Haru had pressed into his hand the week before, when everything had still been ordinary. Jake had been carrying it in his sleeve for eight days. It was the stupidest object he owned, and he never once considered leaving it behind.

At the docks the ship stood taller than anything in his life had ever been. Crew moved past him in pairs with crates and did not look at the burned-handed boy at the prince’s shoulder. The one man who did look had his wrist taken by Iroh without any shift of expression, and did not look again.

“I’m not on the ship’s list,” Jake said, halfway up the gangway, because it had only just occurred to him.

“You are on it now,” Iroh said, and did not slow down.

On the deck, out of the crowd’s road, Iroh sat him down on a coil of rope and worked the wraps off his hands with fingers quicker than Jake had ever seen them be. The palms shone and tightened in the sea wind. Jake’s whole body arched at the cold of it. He did not make a sound.

“These are plain, and they are ugly,” Iroh said. He drew a pair of plain gloves out of his sleeve and put them on Jake’s hands one at a time, over new linen.

“Leather keeps the linen where linen wants to go, and the sun does not touch what the leather is over. You will wear them. That is the end of the subject.”

From the rope coil Jake could see where the prince was: Zuko at the far rail with his bandaged head between his shoulders, alone in a lane the crew opened and closed around him like water around a stone. Nobody approached him. Nobody met his eyes. Jake understood with a lurch that Iroh had put a task into his hands at the exact moment the prince’s own had nothing left to hold.

The lines came off the dock. The ship’s bell rang twice and the shore began to move.

Jake stood at the far rail in his new gloves with his bundle against his chest and watched the harbor lights go out one at a time. The customs lamps, then the forge row, then the twin fires at the harbor mouth that had not gone out in his lifetime. Between him and the stones the wake closed over itself in a long slow swallow, black into black.

The island put its shoulder between them and the last of the fires went.

Ahead of him there was open water, and it lay flat as far as the dark ran.
