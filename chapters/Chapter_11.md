# Chapter 11: The Divide Below Decks

The gulls were on the water out in the shallows, gathered in one place in the dark, and they had been there all night, and gulls do not sit on the sea at night. That was the first thing that told Jake somebody was out there.

He was in the water to the knee, which was a stupid place to stand. The sea kept coming in over his boots and into the legs of his trousers, until the cold of it stopped being information and became a kind of noise he worked inside. Behind him the ship lay black against a blacker sky with her pump going, a slow heartbeat under the boards, and the line he had thrown out an hour ago was still made fast to the bow cleat with the tide working at it.

Three men came up out of the water. The line was for the fourth, who was still in the channel: it ran from the bow cleat through the three ahead and on to him, and he had the end of it in his fist.

"Take him up to the ship," Jake said, low. "Don't let him go under. Walk him."

He went out to the man. He had the man's arm over his own shoulders before the man could refuse it. The man tried to refuse it, and then did not, which was the better of the two outcomes. The channel was up to their chests and the sea came in behind them in slow green breaths that pushed them both up toward the ship.

"Your heel," Jake said.

"It's nothing."

"Your heel is on fire and you've been walking on it for four days, so it's something."

"You bound it."

"I bound it. Salt got into it. I should have put grease on it." Jake found his footing in the sand and hauled. "I did say I'd come back and check it."

"You never said when."

The sand came up under them. The ship grew over them, black and enormous and dripping. The men ahead of them hauled the line hand over hand, and the gulls went up off the water together without making a sound, which they had never done in a week of watching them.

Jake got the man up onto the beach and turned him and looked at the heel by the light from the galley door, which was the only light there was.

The linen had gone into him. A ring of skin had gone with it, and the raw place was the color of a boiled shrimp. The flesh had begun closing over the linen instead of the other way around, which is the thing that happens when nobody looks at a wound for four days. Jake did not say that.

"That's a bad heel," he said, and took the man's weight again.

---

The ship had not moved. She lay where Zuko had told her to lie, off the wild coast, in a bight of sand where the water ran out in a shallow shelf, with the boilers banked and the pump going. She had lain there four days and not once been dry.

Jee had stood every watch since the party went into the trees, and the arithmetic of four days of nothing had been eating him all afternoon.

There was no boat. There had been no boat since the launch went over the falls at the river. The crew had built a raft out of the ship's spare grating during the dry dock, lashing it to two lockers, and had spent a day of the dry dock getting it back into the hold, where it still was. A raft was a thing you built when you meant to need it, and after that it was a thing you carried around.

So the party had come down to the water on foot, in the dark, in single file, and had waited four hours in a tidal creek while a patrol lantern came up the beach and went away again. Jee had watched the lantern from the rail with his hands behind his back and had not said one word about it, then or afterward.

The dead camp had been on the far side of the ridge. The deer path had gone up into the trees and stopped.

---

Zuko had come up out of the water ahead of the others, which was not his habit. He usually came ashore as a man comes ashore who owns the far side of it.

He stood on the beach in wet clothes and looked at the ship the way a man looks at a horse that has thrown him. Jake waited for him to speak, because something was going to have to be said, and there was a whole crew on the ship to hear it.

"Get the anchor up," Zuko said. "We go north."

"Jee will want a full rotation on the pump—"

"Get the anchor up."

He went up the ladder at the bow and in through the forward hatch, and the door shut after him, down the aft passage, with a sound like a small hard thing being put down on a shelf. That was the whole of the report. Four days of nothing, and the report was a course.

Jake stood on the beach in wet trousers with the gulls over his head and found that he was not angry about it, which was worse. Anger was a thing you could meet.

---

The soldier was on the galley step with his heel in a bucket of cold water and his eyes shut, and the cook held the lamp and talked, because the cook always held the lamp and always talked. Jake had worked that out over three years. It was the cook's way of saying he was there.

"Dead camp," the soldier said. "Fire Nation camp, two days old, maybe three. Cold ring, black pot, a stack of arrowheads."

"Arrowheads."

"Fire Nation arrowheads. The kind with the notch." The soldier opened his eyes and looked at the lamp instead of at Jake. "And a deer path going up into the trees, and the path stopped, sir. It just stopped, like something came through and took it."

"The river?"

"High. Came up in the night and went down in the day and came up again." He said it like a man reporting the weather in a country he did not live in. "It doesn't leave a line. Water doesn't leave a line. You can't read it."

Jake unwrapped the heel. The salt had been in it for four days and the skin had gone as skin goes, and when he pulled the last of the linen free the man made a sound through his teeth and did not move his foot.

"Grease," Jake said. "That's what I should have put on it."

"Grease."

"Any fat will do. Whale oil if the cook has it." Jake looked up. The cook was already moving.

"They don't talk up there," the soldier said, quieter.

"Who."

"The party." He swallowed. "Four days in the trees and nobody said anything. The one in front, the man with the billhook, he talked the whole first day. After that nobody talked."

Jake put grease on the heel with a piece of linen and wrapped it, and tied it above the injury and not over it, because a wrap over it would take the skin with it when it came off.

"Two days," he said. "Two days and no talking. Then you come down the mountain in the dark and wade in the sea, and the first thing you tell me is about the river."

"I didn't say I was sorry."

"I know you didn't."

The cook set a bowl of hot rice in front of the man and stood over him while he ate it. Jake went up to the deck, because the pump was going and the anchor was still down and there was a night of work in front of him that would not do itself.

---

The coal was counted once a day, at the change of the watch, and the count was the chief's job and the arithmetic was Jake's. The two of them had got very good at not saying so in front of the men.

The chief was a small man from a place where they made things out of clay, and he had coal in his bones as some men have salt. He would put his hand into the bunker and feel his way down the seams of it, and he would say the number, and Jake would say the number back at him, and they would agree. Then the chief would make a face at the walls of the hold, and Jake would do the sum silently and not say it out loud.

That was the arrangement. It had not always been.

"Two hundred and six," the chief said, and took his hand out of the dark.

"Two hundred and six."

"That's seven days at hard steam."

"It's six."

The chief did not look up. He had a way of not looking up that was more eloquent than most men's arguing, and it meant they had been here before.

"Seven is if the weather's kind and we don't have to hold her up in it," the chief said.

"It's not kind."

"It might come round kind."

Jake put the shutter on the lamp and let the dark take them both, because a man's face arguing about coal was worth more than his face agreeing about it, and the men on the ladder heard words and not arithmetic. He went up the ladder with the number in him where it sat all the time now, and it sat there like a stone at the bottom of a boot.

Six days of hard steam. After that, the fire goes in the bunkers and burns what is left of the ship.

They were not at hard steam and were never going to be. The ship made half speed on a fire kept down to nothing, and the number they were arguing over was a number about a fire they would not light. It fell a day every two days, and it would keep on falling a day every two days until the arithmetic came down to the inside of the ship.

He did not say that to the chief. The chief did not say it to him. Both of them went up the ladder into a morning that had no business being as gray as it was.

The chief had been a coal man for twenty years and had never in that time been sick at sea, and he had a way of putting his hand into a bunker that made it look like a man reaching into a stove at home. He had told Jake once, in the second year, that a ship's coal was a kind of clock, and that a good officer knew what time it was without looking at it. He had said it as though it were a compliment, and Jake had understood it as a description of what he was going to be for the next three years.

---

Rationing was Jake's other job, and it was a worse one, because rationing was arithmetic you had to say out loud.

They had been on rice since Ketu Harbor, and now they were on rice and whatever the cook could make out of the flour and the salt pork that had gone the color of a candle. The cook complained about the flour every day with a fluency that should have cost him something, and Jake let him, because a man who complains at the galley table is a man who is not complaining anywhere else.

"You'll have them on the quarter again by Friday," the cook said.

"Then I'll have them on the quarter on Thursday."

"By Thursday the flour is soup."

"Then I'll have them on the quarter on Wednesday."

The cook looked at him for a moment with the ladle in his hand. "You do that every day."

"Yes."

"And it never gets me more flour."

"No," Jake said. "But it gets you an argument to have, and that's worth something."

The extra half-meal went to the stokers, because the stokers were on three-hour shifts, and the stokers knew they were on three-hour shifts because Jake had put them there. He had done it in the first week after the blockade, when the pressure in the aft housing had started to go. He had done it again every week since, because there was no arrangement that did not need one more turn of the same screw.

The pump had been put to a two-man relay. That was the newest thing. It meant two men off sleep at every watch. It meant the ship was always being held up by somebody who had not been asleep since yesterday. It meant that one day soon the arithmetic of the crew would stop working, because you cannot take sleep out of a man indefinitely and call the result a crew.

Jake had done those sums too, at night, by the pump, with his hands on the plunger.

The ration was set out on the galley table every morning, in a row of bowls, and a boy counted the shares out of a tin into them. Each man took his and no more, and the taking was the part that had started to get difficult.

A man could forgive a bad dinner. A man could not easily forgive standing in a line in front of eleven other men while his share was counted out of the tin and nobody looked up, and it showed in the faces of the ones who had been at sea longest. The big island man always took his bowl last, and always took it with two hands, and never looked at Jake while he did it.

The man could not swim. Nobody on the ship had ever said so out loud. Jake had known it since the second year. The man went down the ladder to the pump and came up it again with his hand on the rail every time. He had never once mentioned it to anyone, including Jake, who had decided a long time ago that it was not his to mention.

The men asked him about it every day, in the flat voice men use for a question they already know the answer to. Is there a plan. Where are we going. What happens when the coal is gone. Jake gave them the only answers he had, which were none, and they took them as they took the rice, and he was aware of the arithmetic of that while he stood there handing it out.

---

The sea got up out of the north on the second day and never really went down again after that.

No storm yet. The swell came up in sets of three and made the ship work, and the wind stayed in the north where it belonged and did nothing but hold the swell up. They took water over the bow twice a day, a green sheet that came aboard and went aft, and everybody on deck had a job the whole time it was happening, and nobody said anything funny.

There had been one on the first day out, low and long, off the port bow and gone before the light was any good. Jake had it in the log with nothing but the time against it, and since then nobody on the ship had asked him what it was.

Kaito was on the sheet with a line. He was nineteen, with a burn on his shoulder that had not finished doing what burns do, and singed hair above one ear that the whole ship had watched him comb flat and pointless. He hauled when he was told to haul, and made a joke when there was a second in hand to make one in.

"The bosun says this is a good sea," Kaito said.

"That's the bosun's job."

"Yesterday he said the same about the wind, and then the wind came round and took his hat."

"Does the bosun have a hat?"

"He has a hat now. He had it before. He has it again." Kaito grinned at the rail. "It's a hard hat for a hard sea."

Jake held the sheet line while the green water came over and went aft. The grip of it came up through the hemp and into his left hand, and the old seam in his palm gave a little, the way a door gives when you lean on it.

He did not stop. He held it until the sheet was gone, and then he went below and changed the linen, and the linen had not really needed changing. He changed it anyway, because it was the sort of thing that took four minutes and gave him four minutes of not looking at anybody.

---

On the third day out, at the middle watch, Jake saw a hull.

It was a lean shape on the top of the haze, a long way off, sitting still in a patch of light as a boat sits when it is stopped or going very slowly. It was there for the better part of an hour. Then the haze took it.

Nobody on the deck said a word about it. Jake looked at the men and found that all of them had looked at it, and that all of them had decided at the same moment not to be the one who mentioned it, and that this was now the second time in a week.

That night it came again, further out, and lower, and this time the boy with the burn on his forearm was the one who spotted it, because the boy never stopped watching the water.

"Sir." The boy's voice was flat. "There's a hull."

Jake looked where he was pointing, and the hull was not there, and he had a bad minute while he decided whether the boy had seen it at all.

"Which one," he said.

"Two points off the port bow, low. It's gone now."

"Boats don't vanish."

"No," the boy said. "So it's big."

The boy was maybe seventeen, and he had a burn the length of his forearm that had gone the shiny pink that burns go when they are new, and Jake had dressed it himself three times in the last week. Jake had not asked him where he had got it and the boy had not said, and Jake was aware of having decided not to ask, and aware of what that meant.

"Get some sleep," Jake said.

The boy stayed at the rail a while longer, and Jake stayed beside him, and neither of them said anything, and it was the boy who finally went below. Jake watched him go. The hull did not bother him. What bothered him was how the boy had said it, flat and certain, as if he had seen that hull before and knew exactly what it was.

---

The talk below decks changed in the fourth night. It did not become mutiny. It was worse than mutiny. Mutiny is a thing men agree on.

It became arithmetic.

"Eleven days to the strait," the chief said, somewhere in the dark, over a bill he had gone over before. "The chief says eleven."

"The chief said eleven on Tuesday."

"He says eleven every day. It's not a new number."

"It's not a new number because nobody's counted."

"Five days of coal."

"Five days of coal," somebody agreed, and did not say anything else, and the not-saying was the sentence.

Jake lay in his alcove off the prince's quarters with his eyes open and the water working under the deck. He listened to the sum being done on the other side of the bulkhead, and he could have gone out and said the number, and it would have stopped them for an hour.

He did not go out. He lay there and let them do it, because a crew that was arguing about the prince was a crew that had not yet done the other thing, which was arguing about the future.

---

Kaito found him at the pump on the third watch of that same night and sat down on the sea chest with his back against the frame, and waited, which Kaito did not often do.

"Ask," Jake said.

"I asked them." Kaito was looking at the plunger. "The stokers. I asked them what they wanted."

"What did they say?"

"One of them said he wanted to sleep."

Jake waited for the rest of it, and there was no rest of it, and he understood that there was no rest of it because that was the whole of the answer.

"That's the list?"

"That's the list." Kaito turned the cap on his water bottle over and over. "I asked the second one and he said he wanted to sleep. And the third one said he wanted to sleep, and then he said he wanted to be told it was going to be all right, and I said I'd tell him, and I didn't, because I didn't know."

Jake put his hand on the plunger and let it come down and go up.

"Give them the sleep," he said.

"That's the whole of it? Just give them the sleep?"

"Give them four hours in the middle watch. Not the watch." Jake thought about the housing aft, and about the standing water, and about the fact that he was about to take four hours of sleep out of the pump and put it into the stokers. It moved the problem from the men's bodies to the ship's. "I'll take the relay myself. Three nights."

"Jake—"

"I said I'd take it. Three nights."

Kaito sat with it. "That's not a fix."

"No," Jake said. "It's a place to put it for three nights."

Somewhere above them a lashing went slack and slapped the mast, and neither of them looked up.

The furnace room was two decks down and it was the hottest place on the ship in any weather. The boy with the burn had the middle of the night there, because he would take the middle of the night anywhere it was offered. Jake went down at two with a tin of clean linen and dressed the arm again without being asked. The boy held it out and let it be done and did not look at him. He said thank you in a voice with nothing in it, and Jake said it was nothing, because that was the arrangement, and neither of them said the other thing.

---

Jee found him on the afterdeck on the morning watch with the sun not properly up, the sky the color of an old coin, and the sea still working.

He had been standing there a while, Jake thought, before he spoke. He stood with his hands behind his back and his fingers hooked over his belt, and he shifted his weight off his left side and put it back, and Jake saw him do it and said nothing about it. The sea came over the rail and went away again. A few feet off, the big island man, who had never once said so, watched the water with the flat attention of somebody counting.

"Lieutenant."

"Jake." Jee did not turn. "There's a thing I want to say to the prince, and I want to say it to his face, once, and then I'll let it go."

"Alright."

"I'm not asking him for anything." Jee said it as a man says a thing he has already said to himself several times. "I want to tell him what the ship is doing. I want him to stand on the deck one time. Ten minutes. At the rail. Let them see him."

Jake waited for the rest.

"I have kept a deck twenty years. I can make a man work. I can't make a man serve a boy he hasn't seen in four days. They're not saying it. They wouldn't dare say it, and they don't have to, because I can hear it, and I can hear it walking around the deck like a man who isn't there." He turned his head, and the arithmetic Jake had been doing all week was on his face too. "Let them see him. That's all. Ten minutes at the rail."

"I'll tell him."

"Don't tell him." Jee's mouth moved. "Ask him. There's a difference and I know the difference and I'd like it kept."

---

Jake went down the ladder to the passage outside the prince's quarters. He stood there with his hand on the rail and the lamp burning low behind the door, and he made himself say it in his own head three times before he said it out loud.

"My lord. Jee asks to speak with you."

The door did not open.

"There is a man on this ship who has asked to speak with you. I have asked him what he wants, and he wants ten minutes at the rail."

The silence held until Jake began to think the prince had decided to pretend he was asleep.

"I am not going to hear it," Zuko said.

"My lord—"

"He can come up." The voice was level and flat and it was not loud, and it was the voice Zuko used when he was holding something down. "If he wants to stand at the rail he can come up and stand at the rail."

Jake stood in the passage with his hand on the rail.

The thing he had done, he understood, was to make the request sound like a request. He had softened it into something a man could say yes to, and now the answer had been given to the soft version, and the hard version was still standing in the passage in his wet clothes wanting ten minutes.

"Sir," he said. "He isn't asking for anything. He only wants you to be seen."

There was a pause.

"Tell the lieutenant I am not coming up," Zuko said. "And tell him to say it to me himself, if he wants to say it that badly."

The lamp behind the door went out.

---

Jake went up and found Jee at the rail and stood beside him a while without saying anything, and let the sea do what it was doing.

"He'll see you tomorrow," Jake said.

He had said it before. Once in the second year, about a doctor for a man with a lung, and once in the third, about a pair of boots. Both times it had gone out of him without any effort, as a man lets a rope go over a rail, and both times the weight of it had arrived afterward, on the deck, with nobody there to hand it to.

Jee did not look at him. "Will he."

"In the morning. I'll have it."

Jee turned his head slowly. He was a big man and Jake was not, and there was a calculation running behind the old man's eyes that Jake could see running and could not stop.

"Tomorrow," Jee said. "I've given you till the morning watch. After that I'll go and stand outside that door myself, and I won't knock, and I'll say it loud enough for the whole passage to hear, and I don't care what he does about it."

"Alright."

"That's not a threat, boy. That's a man telling you when his patience runs out." Jee put his hand on the rail. "You know what the worst of it is? He's right. About the Avatar. Any one of us would run for it too, if we were him. It's how he does it that's eating us, and he doesn't know that's what's eating us."

He said it as a man says a thing he has been carrying a long time.

"Jee."

"Don't."

Jake went below.

---

He could not sleep, and he could not think of a single thing to do with the hours. In the end he went up on deck and found Iroh in the lee of the deckhouse, with a folding cup in his hands and a pot of tea beside him that had been there long enough to stop steaming.

"Uncle."

"Sit." Iroh did not look up. "You look like a man who has made a promise."

Jake sat down on the hatch cover opposite him. The tea went into the cup, and the steam came up, and Jake put both hands around it and did not drink it.

"I asked him for ten minutes at the rail for Jee. He said no. He said if the lieutenant wants to stand at the rail he can come up."

"That was not what you asked."

"No," Jake said. "It wasn't."

Iroh drank his tea and said nothing for a while. The ship worked under them, and the engine note came up through the boards with a beat missing in it, every fourth turn.

"You want me to speak to him," Iroh said.

"Yes."

"I will not."

Jake turned the cup around in his hands.

"If I speak to my nephew about the ship, he will hear that the ship is the cost." Iroh set the cup down on the deck. "And if the ship is the cost, then the hunt is what is paying for it. And then he must choose, Jake, and he is sixteen, and a boy of sixteen told the true arithmetic of his own life will take one of two things. He will take the ship, or he will take the Avatar. Both of them leave this deck poorer and leave him poorer still, and he will not know which of the two he has chosen."

Jake did not have an answer for that, and he was grateful, because the not having it was what he had come up here for.

"You did not come up here for tea," Iroh said.

"No."

"You came up here to have the thing handed to you, and I am not going to hand it to you either." Iroh refilled the cup and pushed it across the deck. "What I will tell you is a fact, and then you will do with it what you can."

"Alright."

"The crew has not given their loyalty to the prince." Iroh said it without any weight on it at all, which made it worse. "They have given it to you. They ask you for the shift, and they take the ration from your hand, and they tell you their feet hurt. They do not do it to the boy in the cabin with the scar, because the boy in the cabin has never once asked them how their feet are."

Jake looked at the tea.

"Do not spend it on him," Iroh said. "Spend it on them. That is what it is for, and he will never know that you did it, and that is also part of the cost."

The engine missed its beat again.

"How long have you known the coal was six days?" Jake said.

"Since the day you counted it."

"And you said nothing."

"I said a great many things," Iroh said, "and none of them were to you."

A boy came up the ladder from below and stood at the edge of the lee, out of the wind, holding a tin cup. He was the one with the burn on his forearm, and the burn was dressed now with a strip of clean linen, and he held his arm as a man holds an arm that is still deciding about itself.

"Sir," he said. "The pump's singing different. Do you want me to run it on the slow stroke or the fast one?"

Jake looked at him. "What's the note?"

"Deeper. It was a hum, now it's a hum with a crack in it."

"Then slow. Always slow when there's a crack in it." Jake pulled the wrapping tighter on his own left hand, out of habit and without any reason to do it. "Run it slow and tell the chief I want the housing sounded at first light."

"Aye, sir."

The boy stayed at the edge of the lee a moment longer than the question required, and Jake felt him looking at his hands, at the left one especially, at the linen and the leather over it. He was taking note of something for later. That was what Jake did not like about it.

Then the boy went back down the ladder, and Iroh took a drink of his tea.

"That one watches you," Iroh said.

"Everybody watches me."

"That one is writing it down." Iroh stood up and picked up the pot and looked out at the sea with the wind in his beard. "I am going up. I will stand at the rail for an hour. It will not be the same as the prince, and every man on this deck will know the difference."

"Uncle—"

"Go and sleep, Jake."

He went up the ladder to the deck. Jake heard the boots cross the boards and stop at the rail, and after a while the bosun's voice, surprised, and then the low murmur of a man being told to hold his mouth, and then nothing.

Jake did not go up. He sat on the hatch cover with the tea gone cold in his hands and did the second sum, which was the one Iroh had not asked about.

Jee would come to the door in the morning.

He could stop it. He could stand in the passage and tell Jee that the prince was unwell, or asleep, or that it was not the hour, or any of the nine small lies a person can say in a corridor to keep a thing from happening. He had a whole night to think of them, and they would all have worked, and every one of them would have cost him the thing Iroh had just told him about.

He got up and went to his alcove and lay down with his boots on, and he did not sleep. At some point in the small hours he stopped trying to and simply lay there being awake, which is a different thing and not a better one.

---

The morning watch came in gray and cold, and Jee did not come to the door.

Jake went and found him instead, on the afterdeck, at the rail, with his hands behind his back.

"Lieutenant."

Jee did not turn. "I said I'd come at the morning watch."

"I know what you said."

"I said it out loud, in front of you, so you'd have the night to do something." The old man shifted his weight. "And you have come to tell me you didn't do anything."

"I came to tell you the truth," Jake said. "He will not see you. Not at the rail and not anywhere. I said what I said last night because I did not have the truth yet, and I should not have said it."

Jee turned around.

He did not shout. Jake had been half expecting him to shout, and had braced for it in some way, and the not-shouting was much worse than the shouting would have been. The old man looked at him for a long moment with his jaw working.

"Twenty years," Jee said. "I have given that boy the ship, the coal, the men, and my own two hands, and I have never once asked him for anything, and I have just asked him for ten minutes."

"I know."

"And he cannot give you ten minutes." Jee's voice was almost gentle. "That is the answer, then. He cannot."

"Yes."

"Then I have my answer, and I will hold it, and I will keep it below this deck with the rest of us." He put his hand on the rail. "But you will tell me one thing, Jake, and you will not soften it, because I am too tired to be handled."

"Alright."

"Does he know what he is spending?"

Jake thought about it honestly, as Iroh had told him to, and he thought about the lamp under the door and the man in the cabin who could not make himself come up and stand on a deck for ten minutes.

"No," he said. "I don't think he knows."

Jee nodded, and it was a nod that cost him something, and he turned back to the sea.

"Aye," he said. "That's the trouble with it. It's not that he's a bad lad. It's that he doesn't know the price, and we all do."

Jake stood beside him and let it be true. That was the thing about Jee: he did not need to be managed. He needed to be accurate, and he was, and there was nothing Jake could do with accuracy.

"Tell him one more thing for me," Jee said, without turning. "Not a message. Just tell him that the men know the price, whatever he does or doesn't know it, and that we have been paying it without complaint, and that the complaint is not about the money."

"Alright."

"He'll not understand it."

"He'll understand it," Jake said, and knew as he said it that he did not know that, and said it anyway, because it was the only thing he had to give the man and the man was standing there waiting for something.

---

The weather turned in the last two days, and it turned without any fuss about it.

The wind came around from the north and stayed. The swell that came with it was longer than the old one, and it did not break, and the ship took it on the quarter and rolled and came back and took it again. The motion got into everybody's hips, and their sleep, and their tempers.

The sky went from gray to a gray that had no business being called a color, and the light on the water went flat and stayed flat, so that the horizon was not a line anymore but a place the sea stopped being certain about.

The birds went wrong.

Jake noticed it on the second afternoon of the bad weather, at the rail, with the sheet coming over. Three gulls had been with the ship for a week, and there had been four, and there were three now. The three were flying low and hard, under the level of the rail, inland, all of them in the same direction at the same time, and they did not come back to the ship, though they stayed in sight of it.

He watched them until they were specks, and then they swung back across the bow, low and in line, and went on inland. After that he went and asked the bosun, who was a hundred years old and had been wrong about the weather before and would be again.

"They're going in ahead of it," the bosun said. "Birds know."

"What is it?"

"Bad. Look at the glass." The bosun tipped his chin at the barometer on the binnacle, which is a brass box with a needle in it that says what the air is about to do. The needle was not where it had been the day before. "That's what it's for. Nobody looks at it."

Jake looked at it. The needle had come down a finger's width, and he had not been watching it for two days, because he had been watching eleven other things.

He wrote it in the log. He wrote the coal and the weather and the swell and the birds in the small square hand he had taught himself in the palace, where the copying of ledgers was the one thing they had let him do for two years.

*Wind N, holding. Swell long, not breaking. Birds gone inland, low. Glass down.*

He wrote the coal under the weather and stopped there, because there were two numbers for the coal and one column, and he was not the man who would have to pick one. Then he put the pen down, because there was nothing to put after it that would have helped.

The ship went on working while he wrote. The sheet came over twice more in that hour. Both times the ship came up out of it with the rail awash and the water going aft in a green sheet, and nobody swore at it, because there was no energy left in a man for swearing. The boy with the burn came past the log table with a tin cup and looked at what Jake had written. Jake turned the page over. The boy went on without a word, and Jake sat with the pen in his hand and did not turn the page back.

---

His hands went on the second night of the bad weather.

He was on the pump with the relay, and the housing aft was singing its bad note. The water came up over the coaming, and he got his left hand flat on the grating to push it, and the old seam in his palm opened.

Not much. The seam was doing what a seam does when a man has been wet and cold and working for nine hours. He held the water back with his right hand and his shoulder and his teeth for a while, and then it stopped, and there was a red line across his palm that was not deep and had been opened before and would open again.

He wrapped it himself, with his teeth on the linen, one-handed, as he had learned to do it in the first year when there was nobody to do it for him.

Down the passage, the light under the prince's door was on. It had been on every night for a week. Jake saw it on his way to the pump and he saw it on his way back, and he did not knock, and he did not slow down, and it was the only square of warm light on the whole ship.

He thought about the promise he had broken, and about the coal, and about the fact that the morning watch was four hours away and had already come and gone.

The sum did not close. He had done it that morning, and it had not closed then either, and it would not close tomorrow. The number it wanted to close on was four days of hard steam and then the inside of the ship.

He did the third sum then, the one he did not write down, which was the sum of what he had left in him. It came to about the same as the coal.

There was a way to close the sum. He had done the arithmetic on it more than once and he had never once done it out loud. The ship had a name, and the name was written in a chest in the cabin. The chest was in the cabin with the lamp on, and the prince in that cabin was sixteen, and asleep or awake or sitting on the deck in the dark with the light off.

Jake went up the ladder and out into the weather and did not go below.

The gulls were still there at midnight. All of them, whatever the number was, low over the water and going inland, and the sea ran under them in long gray lifts, and the ship climbed one and came down and climbed another. Jake stood at the rail with his wrapped hand hanging loose and watched them go, and did not go below.
