# Chapter 3: Ketu Harbor

The ship stopped shuddering an hour before they reached the breakwater, and it was the stillness that finally let Jake sleep. Two hours, maybe less, curled in the alcove with his knees against the bulkhead and the deep cold worked so far into his bones that even sleep hadn't warmed him. When he woke the deck was different under him—no roll, no grinding creak of ice against the hull. Just the low slap of harbor water and the shouted numbers of men bringing them in to dock.

He came up onto the main deck already working. There was always something to do in the last hour before port: lines to coil, the crew's spare gear to gather, Zuko's second set of armor to check over so it would be ready the moment they set foot on land. Jake kept his hands moving because his hands moving meant his mind didn't have room to do anything else, and right now his mind wanted to do a great many other things.

Ketu Harbor came up slow through the morning haze, and each length of it made the ship beneath his feet feel smaller.

He'd known, in the abstract, that they were sailing into a real port—a working Fire Nation installation, not some coastal fishing town that would barely notice one more military vessel. But knowing a thing and standing in front of it were different animals. Warships lined the deep-water piers in neat, identical rows, their black hulls scrubbed and gleaming, banners snapping crisp and red against a sky the color of ash. Soldiers moved along the docks in disciplined columns, boots striking stone in unison. Everything about the place had been built to declare its own order, its own strength, its own certainty that the war was already won.

Their ship did not belong among it. Jake felt that before he could have said why. The bow still wore the scars of the avalanche—a long raw gouge where ice had scraped paint down to bare metal, a crumpled section of railing that the crew had lashed rather than repaired, water stains bleeding rust down the hull like something the ship had wept. Where the Fire Navy cruisers sat proud and identical, theirs limped, listing faintly to port even now, dragging itself the last hundred yards to a berth at the very end of the pier—as far from the polished ships as the harbor could put them.

Jake understood, without anyone explaining it to him, exactly what that distance meant.

He found Zuko at the rail above the gangway, already dressed, his jaw set in the particular way it set whenever he was bracing for something he couldn't burn his way through. Iroh stood a pace behind him, hands tucked into his sleeves, wearing the mild, faintly pleased expression he wore in front of strangers—the one that made him look like exactly what he claimed to be: a retired old man enjoying his tea.

"Uncle." Zuko didn't look at him. His eyes were on the harbor, cataloguing it the way he cataloged every unfamiliar space—exits, uniforms, rank insignia. "Don't say his name here. Not the boy, not the arrow, none of it. If word gets out that the Avatar is alive, every firebender on this coast will want a piece of the hunt."

"I am aware of the stakes, Prince Zuko." Iroh's voice stayed light, but something moved under it, quick and sharp, gone before Jake could be sure he'd heard it at all.

Jake fell into step just behind Zuko's shoulder as the crew ran out the gangway, close enough that his presence would read as attendance rather than distance. He kept his eyes down, his hands loosely folded, his whole posture arranged into the particular kind of stillness that made people's gazes slide past him without stopping. It was a skill he'd learned before he could walk properly—how to be furniture, how to occupy space without appearing to claim any of it. He had never needed it more than he needed it right now.

Because here, on soil the Fire Nation had already conquered and paved and fortified, everything about him was a lie waiting to be found. His birth. His hands beneath the gloves. The water in his blood that could, if it wanted to, undo every careful year of pretending he'd never had it. Three years of open sea had let him forget, in small ways, how heavy that lie could get. Ketu Harbor put the weight back on him step by step, the way the gangway put weight back on his legs after months at sea.

He glanced once at Zuko's back—the set of his shoulders, the particular rigidity that meant he was furious and trying very hard not to show it—and felt something in his own chest ease slightly. Whatever else happened here, Zuko wanted him close. Had said as much without saying it, the way Zuko said most things that mattered: *you're coming with me,* not a question, offered at the base of the gangway like it wasn't a small mercy.

Jake was grateful for it in a way he wouldn't have known how to explain. Being kept near meant being watched over. It also meant being useful, being seen to belong to Zuko's household rather than left to the mercy of whoever the exile crew's guards might be while their masters took tea. He'd take that arrangement over solitude in a heartbeat, and he suspected Zuko knew it, in whatever unspoken way Zuko knew most things about him.

They were perhaps twenty paces down the pier when the man appeared.

He came from the direction of the largest cruiser in port, walking with the unhurried confidence of someone who had never once in his life doubted that a room would arrange itself around him. His uniform was Commander's red, pressed to a precision that made Zuko's salt-stiffened coat look like something dragged out of a shipwreck, which, Jake supposed, it very nearly was. Twin points of dark hair framed a face built for smugness, brows arched in permanent, faint amusement, and his stride carried the particular swagger of a man walking through a harbor he considered entirely his own. Two guards fell in a half-step behind him, and even they seemed pressed and gleaming, as though his tailoring extended to everyone in his orbit.

Jake's stomach tightened before the man had said a single word. He didn't know this officer's name yet. He didn't need to. He recognized the shape of the danger immediately, the way he'd learned to recognize weather before it broke—something in how the man's gaze moved, unhurried, cataloguing the battered ship, Zuko's stiff posture, Iroh's folded hands, and finally, briefly, Jake himself. The look didn't linger. It didn't need to. It simply noted him, filed him away as unimportant, and moved on. Jake had spent a lifetime being grateful for exactly that kind of dismissal, and it had never once stopped feeling like a blade passing close enough to nick.

"Getting in the way of what, Prince Zuko?"

The voice was rich with satisfaction, pitched to carry. Zuko had gone rigid beside him—Jake felt it more than saw it, a change in the air between them, a held breath.

"It's Commander, now," the man went on before Zuko could answer whatever he'd been about to say. "And General Iroh." A small, theatrical bow toward Iroh, mockery dressed as respect. "The great hero of our nation, gracing my harbor."

"Retired general," Iroh said mildly.

"Of course." The Commander's smile didn't move. "The Fire Lord's brother and the Fire Lord's son. Welcome guests, whenever they choose to appear." His eyes swept the ship again, more slowly this time, lingering on the raw gouge in the hull, the crumpled rail, the visible list to port. "What brings you to Ketu Harbor?"

"Our ship requires repairs," Iroh said, before Zuko could answer.

"I can see that." The Commander tilted his head, studying the damage with an interest that had stopped being polite several seconds earlier. "That's quite a bit of it, for a routine patrol."

Jake kept his eyes on the boards of the pier, on the salt stains and the rust-colored water pooling between planks, and made himself breathe slowly through his nose. He didn't need to look up to know what this man's attention would do if it settled on the wrong detail for too long. He thought of the waterspout. The wave. The girl on the flying beast who had frozen two guards solid without a flicker of hesitation. If this smug, sharp-eyed Commander so much as suspected what had truly happened at that Water Tribe village, the hunt for the Avatar would be the least of Zuko's problems.

"You wouldn't believe what happened." Zuko's voice had gone flat and fast, the tone Jake recognized from a dozen small lies over three years of exile—not smooth enough to be convincing on its own, but delivered with enough conviction that most listeners let it pass rather than argue with a prince. "Uncle. Tell the Commander what happened."

Iroh brightened at once, as though he'd been waiting all morning for exactly this invitation. "Yes! I will tell him." He pressed a hand briefly to his chest, an old man startled by his own memory. "It was quite incredible."

"You crashed into something?" the Commander prompted, one eyebrow lifting, clearly enjoying himself.

"Yes," Zuko said. "An Earth Kingdom vessel."

There was a pause just a beat too long. Jake felt it stretch, felt his own pulse pick up in the silence, and did not let his face show a single flicker of it.

"Really." The Commander's smile widened, sharp at the edges. "You must tell me all the thrilling details. Over a drink, perhaps."

"We were just leaving," Zuko said, already turning.

"Prince Zuko." Iroh's tone shifted—still warm, but with an unmistakable edge of correction beneath it, the voice of a man who had spent decades managing exactly this kind of standoff. "Show the Commander proper respect. We would be honored to join him." He turned toward their host with an expression of pure, guileless pleasure. "Do you happen to have any ginseng tea? It is my particular favorite."

The Commander laughed, low and satisfied, already gesturing them toward the row of command tents beyond the pier. "I believe that can be arranged, General."

Jake fell into step a pace behind Zuko as they were drawn forward, close enough to catch the tension still coiled through his shoulders, the tight, controlled way he was holding his hands at his sides. He kept his own gaze low, his breathing even, cataloguing exits and guards out of habit rather than plan—a servant's habit, a survivor's habit, the same watchfulness that had kept him alive in the Fire Nation Royal Palace long before it had kept him alive on this ship. Somewhere behind them, sailors were already being herded off toward the harbor barracks for questioning, standard procedure for any ship making an unscheduled Fire Nation port call, and Jake felt a fresh thread of cold work down through his chest at the thought of what those interrogations might turn up.

He didn't know the Commander's name yet. He would, before the day was finished, come to know it very well.

For now he simply walked, silent and unremarkable, into the mouth of the trap.

***

The command tent swallowed sound the moment the flap fell shut behind them. Outside, gulls and dockworkers and the distant clang of ship repairs; inside, only the low hiss of oil lamps and the too-sweet smell of incense burning somewhere behind a folding screen. Heat pressed close under the canvas, thick enough that Jake felt sweat gather at his collar within moments of entering, though he kept his face as still as he'd kept it on the pier.

A map dominated the tent's center table, the Earth Kingdom laid out in painted greens and browns, red pins driven through cities like wounds. The Commander went to it at once, one hand resting against the table's edge with the easy possessiveness of a man surveying property he already considered won.

"By the year's end," he said, not turning, "the capital falls. And the Fire Lord finally claims the victory his father and his father's father could not."

"If my father thinks the rest of the world will simply fall in line behind him," Zuko said from his seat, arms crossed, "then he's a fool."

The words hung in the hot air a beat too long. Jake, standing just inside the tent flap where a servant belonged, felt his pulse tick up at the recklessness of it—criticizing the Fire Lord aloud, in a stranger's tent, in front of a man clearly gathering ammunition. But the Commander only smiled, as if Zuko had confirmed something he already suspected.

"Two years at sea have done little to soften that tongue of yours." He turned, hands clasped behind his back. "So. How goes the search for your Avatar?"

"We haven't found him," Zuko said.

"Did you truly expect to?" The Commander's smile sharpened at the corners. "The Avatar's been dead for a hundred years, Prince Zuko. Unless, of course, you've stumbled onto some evidence to the contrary."

"No. Nothing."

The lie came out flat and immediate, and Jake watched the Commander's eyes narrow just slightly, weighing it, before the tent flap stirred and a soldier stepped through and dropped to one knee.

"Commander Zhao." The name, finally spoken aloud, confirmed what Jake had already understood in his gut back on the pier. "We interrogated the crew as you instructed."

Jake's whole body went still in a way that had nothing to do with keeping quiet and everything to do with the sudden roar of blood in his ears. *The crew.* His crew. Men who had hauled him out of freezing water more times than he could count, who had watched him bend the wave off that deck three weeks ago and said nothing, not one word, not to Zhao's harbor patrol, not to anyone. He kept his eyes fixed on a point on the tent wall and made himself breathe, slow and even, while every part of him screamed to know what came next.

"They confirmed," the soldier went on, "that Prince Zuko had the Avatar in his custody. And let him escape."

The silence that followed felt endless. Jake waited for the second half of it—*and that his servant is a waterbender, that we saw him move the sea itself*—waited with his stomach in his throat and his gloved hands curling slowly at his sides. But the soldier said nothing more. He rose, bowed, and withdrew, and the tent flap fell shut behind him, and that was all.

They'd broken on the Avatar. Given up the one secret that would spare their own necks from a harbor prison and said nothing—nothing—about him. Jake felt something in his chest loosen so fast it was almost dizzying, relief crashing through him with a force that made him grateful for the stillness he'd trained into his own body over a lifetime of standing quietly in dangerous rooms. Whatever else happened here, they had held the line. Every one of them.

He didn't have time to feel it properly before Zhao's expression curdled into open contempt.

"Now, remind me," Zhao said, savoring each word, "exactly how your ship came to be so thoroughly wrecked. A twelve-year-old boy. He bested you and a crew of trained firebenders." He shook his head slowly, theatrically, like a man forced to witness something distasteful. "You're more pathetic than I gave you credit for. Capturing the Avatar is far too important a task to leave to a disgraced teenager and his beaten men." His eyes flicked, cold and final, to Zuko. "He's mine now."

Zuko was across the tent before Jake had fully registered him moving, one hand already crackling with the promise of flame, and it was only the guards flanking Zhao—two of them, fast and well-trained—who caught his arms and hauled him back before he closed the distance. Jake's own hands twitched at his sides, a useless, instinctive urge to step between them that he strangled before it could show on his face. He had no standing here. Interfering would only give Zhao more to use.

"Careful, Prince Zuko," Zhao said mildly, not even flinching. "Striking a superior officer is a court-martial offense. Even for you." He gestured, and the guards released Zuko, stepping back to flank the tent's entrance instead. "My search party departs within the hour. Once I'm out to sea, my men will escort you back to your ship. You'll be free to go." He said *free* like it was a kindness, and like he knew exactly how little it meant.

Then he was gone, the tent flap swinging shut behind him, leaving Zuko standing rigid in the sudden quiet with his fists still clenched at his sides.

Jake moved before he'd decided to, closing the distance to Zuko's shoulder, close enough to be felt without being obvious about it. Zuko didn't look at him. His jaw worked once, twice, and then his boot came up and slammed into the low table beside them, snapping one of its legs clean through and sending the whole thing crashing sideways in a spray of scattered maps and ink.

"Prince Zuko." Iroh's voice was gentle from the corner of the tent, entirely unbothered, as though nephews routinely destroyed furniture in his presence. "Perhaps we should ask for that tea now."

Zuko didn't answer. He stood over the wrecked table breathing hard, and Jake watched the fury drain slowly out of his shoulders into something colder and more contained, and said nothing at all, because there was nothing to say that would fix what had just happened in this tent.

Zhao returned perhaps a quarter hour later, unhurried, freshly amused, as though he'd only stepped out to check the tide.

"My search party is ready," he said. "As promised, once I'm out to sea, my guards will see you back to your ship."

"Don't underestimate me, Zhao." Zuko's voice had gone low and hard. "I'll capture the Avatar before you do."

Zhao laughed, a short, genuine sound of real amusement. "You can't compete with me, boy. I command hundreds of warships. And you?" His gaze traveled over Zuko slowly, deliberately, cataloguing the salt-stained coat, the battered ship visible through the tent's open flap, the whole diminished shape of him. "You're a banished prince. No home. No allies." A pause, precisely timed. "Your own father doesn't even want you."

"You're wrong." The words came out too fast, too raw. "Once I deliver the Avatar to him, he'll welcome me home. With honor."

"If your father truly wanted you home," Zhao said, almost gently, "he would have let you return by now. But in his eyes, Prince Zuko, you are a failure. A disgrace to the Fire Nation." His eyes flicked, deliberately, to the left side of Zuko's face. "You carry the proof of it right there."

The temperature in the tent seemed to drop despite the heat pressing against the canvas walls. Jake felt his own scarred hands curl tight inside his gloves, an old, helpless ache flaring up through skin that had healed wrong three years ago and never quite stopped remembering it.

"And really," Zhao went on, unhurried, savoring it now, plainly enjoying the reaction he was drawing, "is this what you've been reduced to? Dragging some disgraced servant boy around the world with you—the same one who got you burned in the first place?" His gaze slid, deliberate and dismissive, to Jake. "I'd have thought you'd want to forget his face. Instead you keep him at your heel like a pet. Tells a man quite a lot about how far you've fallen, Prince Zuko."

Jake didn't move. Didn't let his expression change, didn't let his eyes drop or his breath catch, though something in him went white-hot and small and furious in a way he had almost no practice feeling. He was used to being invisible. He was not used to being used—held up, deliberately, as a wound to press into someone else.

Zuko's reaction was instant, and it was not the reaction of a man defending his own pride.

"Maybe," Zuko said, and his voice had gone very quiet, very even, more dangerous than the lunge had been, "you'd like a scar to match."

Zhao's smile only widened. "Is that a challenge?"

"An Agni Kai." Zuko didn't hesitate. "At sunset."

The word dropped through Jake like a stone through ice, and for one long moment he heard nothing else in the tent at all—not Zhao's satisfied acceptance, not Iroh's careful stillness in the corner, not even his own pulse, which seemed to have simply stopped rather than continue into whatever came next.

"At sunset, then," Zhao said, already turning away, already dismissing them with the ease of a man who had gotten exactly what he wanted. "I do hope you remember your forms better than your manners, Prince Zuko."

He left them there, the tent flap settling shut behind him, and in the sudden quiet Jake became aware of Zuko breathing hard through his nose, staring at the space where Zhao had stood, his hands opening and closing slowly at his sides as the full weight of what he had just done began, visibly, to settle onto him.

***

The light in Zuko's cabin had gone the color of a low fire by the time they reached it, slanting long and red through the single porthole, crawling slow across the metal floor as the sun sank toward the harbor mouth. Jake had counted that light before, in another life, in another room—watched it move the same way across polished stone while he waited for something terrible to finish happening. He made himself stop counting now and reached instead for the clasps at Zuko's shoulder.

Neither of them had spoken since the tent.

Zuko stood with his arms slightly away from his sides, the posture of a man being fitted for something rather than undressed, and let Jake work without protest. The outer plates came first—breastpiece, shoulder guards, the segmented arm coverings crew called down whenever Zuko trained—each piece unclasped and set aside with a small metallic clink that seemed, in the quiet cabin, unnaturally loud. Jake had done this a hundred times. His hands knew the sequence without needing his eyes: the hidden catch beneath the left shoulder, the leather strap that always stuck slightly in salt air, the way the breastplate had to be lifted and turned rather than simply pulled to clear the collarbone.

His hands were not entirely steady today. He noticed it distantly, the faint tremor running through his fingers as he worked the last strap loose, and forced it still through sheer will before it could show. Zuko didn't need to see that. Zuko needed him certain.

The plain undershirt came next, and then that too, until Zuko stood in only his training wrap, bare-chested for the ritual that would strip him further still before the sun finished setting. He looked younger like this. Jake had noticed it before, on the rare occasions armor came fully off—something in the absence of all that engineered weight that let the boy underneath the prince show through, all lean muscle and old scar tissue and a spine held too straight for someone trying not to shake.

Jake reached for the fresh wrap folded on the narrow bunk and began winding it at Zuko's waist, movements economical and sure, the same motion he'd used a hundred times readying Zuko for drills that meant nothing next to what waited at sunset. He didn't ask if Zuko was frightened. He already knew, the same way he knew the tide by the color of the water, the same way he knew winter approaching by an ache that had nothing to do with the season. He could see it in the rigid line of Zuko's shoulders, held so carefully upright that they had to be costing him something to maintain.

Neither of them said Ozai's name. Neither of them needed to. It sat in the cabin with them as surely as the two of them did, folded into the red light crawling across the floor, into the particular stillness of Zuko's jaw, into the way Jake's own scarred palms had begun to itch and burn beneath his gloves the moment the word *Agni Kai* had left Zuko's mouth in that tent. His hands remembered fire even when the rest of him tried not to. They had remembered it for three years, and tonight, apparently, they intended to remember it more loudly than usual.

"You should eat something first," Jake said, because it was true, and because it was easier to say than anything else sitting in his chest.

"I'm not hungry."

"I know." Jake finished the wrap and smoothed it once with his palm, an old habit, checking for wrinkles that would chafe. "Eat anyway."

Zuko didn't move to obey, but something in his shoulders loosened by a fraction, the smallest possible give, and Jake let it stand as the closest thing to agreement he was likely to get. He crossed to the small shelf where the ship kept dried fruit and hard bread for exactly this kind of hour, before battle, before anything that might otherwise leave a man's stomach too empty to think straight, and pressed a strip of dried mango into Zuko's hand without comment. Zuko looked at it for a moment like he'd forgotten what food was for, and then ate it anyway, mechanically, because Jake had put it there and some part of him still trusted Jake's judgment more than his own right now.

The light kept crawling.

Jake busied his hands with things that didn't need doing—straightening the folded armor, checking the strap he'd already checked twice, anything that kept his fingers occupied and his mind a half-step behind the images trying to surface. The smell of ozone before a strike. The particular quality of silence in a room full of people who all knew what was coming and none of whom would stop it. He had been thirteen the last time he stood this close to an Agni Kai about to happen, and his hands had not known yet what they were about to do, and everything after that moment had cost him everything he'd ever had.

He was sixteen now. His hands knew exactly what they could do, and tonight, whatever else happened, he would not be allowed to use them. If he stepped into that arena—if the water in the harbor so much as answered him in front of two hundred Fire Nation soldiers—there would be no exile waiting on the other side of it. There would be nothing waiting at all, for either of them.

He didn't say any of that. He picked up the strip of cloth meant to bind back Zuko's hair for the duel instead, and stepped behind him, and began working it through with the same steady, unhurried care he gave to everything he did with his hands when his chest had nothing steady left to offer.

"Jake."

"Mm."

Zuko's reflection in the small polished disc mounted by the bunk—the closest thing the cabin had to a mirror—held very still. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't." Jake kept his hands moving, kept his voice even, kept every part of himself that wanted to shake very carefully out of his voice. "Remember your forms. That's all. The rest of it is just noise."

It was, he understood distantly, something close to a lie—or at least not the whole truth, because there was so much more he could have said and didn't, standing there with his scarred hands working through Zuko's hair in the fading light, the ghost of another burning room pressing close around both of them without either one giving it a name. But it was the only kind of comfort he knew how to give. Not reassurance. Not soft words. Just steadiness, offered with his hands, the same way he always offered it, because it was the only currency he trusted himself to spend honestly.

Zuko's throat worked once. He didn't answer.

The cabin door opened without a knock, and Iroh stepped through, his expression grave in a way Jake rarely saw on him—none of the mild theater he wore for strangers, only quiet, sober weight.

"It's time," Iroh said.

Zuko turned from the polished disc, and whatever had been sitting loose and unguarded in his face a moment before folded itself away, replaced by something harder, flatter, ready. He reached for the light outer robe laid across the bunk—the last piece he would wear until the ritual required him to shed it—and pulled it on with hands that, Jake noted, had finally stopped their own faint tremor.

"Uncle." Zuko's voice had gone very quiet. "Stay close to Jake."

"Of course," Iroh said, as though it needed no saying at all.

Zuko didn't look at Jake again before he turned for the door, but his hand found Jake's shoulder on the way past, one brief, firm press of contact, gone before Jake could do more than feel the weight of it, and then he was moving out into the corridor with his spine set straight and his face already arranging itself into the mask he would wear across the arena floor.

Jake followed him out into the reddening light, his scarred hands curled tight at his sides, carrying with him every year of dread that had built toward exactly this hour.

***

The arena sat open to the sky, a raised stone platform ringed by soldiers standing shoulder to shoulder in disciplined rows, their armor catching the last hard light of sunset until the whole ring seemed rimmed in dull red fire. Jake found a place at the barrier beside Iroh, close enough to the platform's edge that he could feel the residual heat radiating up off the stone from a full day under the sun, and gripped the low rail with both gloved hands because it gave him something to hold that wasn't Zuko's arm.

Across the ring, Zhao shrugged off his outer robe with theatrical ease, baring a broad chest already gleaming faintly with sweat, his expression settled into calm, contemptuous certainty. He had the look of a man who had done this before and won every time. Jake didn't doubt it.

Zuko shed his own robe a moment later, and the sight of him standing bare-chested on that stone, the fading sun throwing his scar into sharp relief along the left side of his face, sent something cold and immediate straight through Jake's chest. He had seen this before. Not this arena, not this opponent, but this—the specific geometry of two figures facing each other across an open circle of stone while the world went silent and waiting around them.

"Remember your firebending basics, Prince Zuko," Iroh said quietly, close enough that only Zuko and Jake could hear him. "They are your greatest weapon."

Zuko didn't answer aloud. His jaw was set, his stance already dropping into the low, coiled readiness Jake had watched him drill into his body a thousand times over three years of exile. Across the ring, Zhao rolled his shoulders once, unbothered, and said something to a soldier nearby that Jake didn't catch, and didn't want to.

"I refuse to let him win," Zuko said, low, more to himself than to either of them.

"This will be over quickly," Zhao called, loud enough to carry.

The gong sounded.

The sound cracked through Jake's chest like a physical blow, and for one terrible moment the arena dissolved and he was not standing at a harbor barrier in the Earth Kingdom at all, but thirteen years old again, in a throne room that smelled of incense and fear, watching a boy he loved kneel before a man made of fire and judgment. He gripped the rail hard enough that the metal bit into his palms through the leather of his gloves, hard enough that the old scars beneath ached in immediate, furious protest, and made himself stay upright through sheer, grinding will.

Zuko struck first, a fast, low sweep of flame aimed at Zhao's legs. Zhao blocked it without visible effort, stepped through the dissipating heat, and answered with a two-handed blast that caught Zuko square in the chest and threw him backward across the stone.

The impact of his body hitting the platform seemed to land somewhere behind Jake's own ribs. He didn't move. Couldn't move—every instinct screaming at him to vault the barrier, to put water between Zuko and the next blast the way he had put water between crew and cliffside three weeks ago, and every ounce of remaining sense telling him that if he so much as raised a hand here, in front of two hundred Fire Nation soldiers, there would be no exile left to return to for either of them. He stayed exactly where he was and hated himself for it with a clean, simple fury that had nowhere else to go.

Zuko rolled to his feet just as Zhao leapt into the air, both fists cocked back for a blow aimed squarely at his face—the same trajectory, the same intention Jake remembered from another arena, another fire, another boy on the ground—and Jake's breath caught somewhere in his throat and simply stayed there, refusing to move either way.

Zuko rolled clear at the last possible instant.

He came up already moving, already low, and swept one leg through Zhao's stance before Zhao's feet had fully returned to the stone. Zhao's balance broke. Jake watched the Commander's whole considerable confidence crumble in a single instant as his own momentum betrayed him, arms pinwheeling, and then Zuko was on him, pressing the advantage with a speed Jake had never quite seen from him in three years of watching him train—driving forward, breaking whatever remained of Zhao's footing, until the Commander went down flat on his back against the stone with Zuko standing over him, fist already drawn back and burning.

"Do it!" Zhao's voice cracked across the silent arena, furious and raw. "Do it!"

The world narrowed. Jake's hands had gone white-knuckled on the rail, every nerve in his body braced for the sound and light of a blast connecting with flesh, for the smell that would follow it, for the sound a person made when fire took something from them that would never grow back.

Zuko's fist came down.

The flame struck stone an inch from Zhao's ear, close enough to singe hair, close enough to leave no doubt about how easily it could have gone the other way, and did not touch him at all.

For one full second nothing in Jake moved—not his breath, not his pulse, not the endless spinning dread that had been building in his chest since the word *Agni Kai* had first left Zuko's mouth in that tent. And then it broke all at once, not into relief exactly, but into something larger and stranger that he didn't have a name for yet. He watched Zuko straighten, watched him look down at the man he had every right and every provocation to maim and did not, and felt something in his own chest that had been braced rigid for three years finally, quietly, come apart.

"That's it?" Zhao spat from the stone, incredulous, contemptuous even in defeat. "Your father raised a coward."

"Next time you get in my way," Zuko said, low and even, "I won't hold back."

He turned his back and walked away.

Jake saw it before he understood what he was seeing—Zhao's hand snapping up from the ground, fire already gathering, aimed squarely at the unguarded space between Zuko's shoulder blades. He opened his mouth, no plan behind it, only the raw animal need to shout a warning that would have meant nothing against the speed of it—

—and Iroh moved.

He crossed the space between the barrier and the center of the ring faster than Jake would have believed a man his age could move, caught Zhao's ankle mid-strike with one hand, and turned the gathering flame harmlessly aside with the other before hurling the Commander backward onto the stone with an ease that made the earlier duel look almost gentle by comparison.

"No, Prince Zuko," Iroh said, and his voice had lost every trace of the mild old man he played for strangers. It rang clean and hard across the silent arena. "Do not taint your victory." He looked down at Zhao sprawled on the stone, and his tone dropped colder still. "So this is how the great Commander Zhao behaves in defeat. Disgraceful." He turned, unhurried, toward the ranks of watching soldiers, then back to the man on the ground. "Even in exile, my nephew conducts himself with more honor than you have ever shown in command." A pause, deliberate. "Thank you again for the tea, Commander. It was delicious."

Zuko had stopped and turned back at the sound of it, and Jake watched his face in the fading light—not triumphant, not even relieved, just very still, watching his uncle stand over the man who had tried to kill him from behind. Something passed between them that Jake couldn't name from where he stood, and then Iroh turned and walked back toward the barrier without another word, and Zuko fell into step beside him.

Jake vaulted the low rail before he'd fully decided to, closing the distance to Zuko's side as the three of them turned together and walked out of the arena, leaving Zhao alone on the stone in the last dying light of the sun.

***

The harbor had gone quiet by the time they reached the docks again, the frantic daylight bustle of loading cranes and marching columns replaced by the ordinary evening sounds of a working port settling toward night—water lapping against hulls, gulls calling somewhere out over the dark water, the creak of mooring lines shifting with the tide. None of them spoke as they walked. Jake found he didn't need them to. The silence between the three of them had lost whatever had been sharp about it back in the tent, back in the arena, and had settled instead into something looser, something he could breathe inside of.

His own heartbeat was only now easing back toward something ordinary. He noticed it the way he noticed weather changing—not all at once, but by degrees, the tightness in his chest unwinding a little further with every step that put the arena behind them.

Zuko walked between them with his robe pulled back over his shoulders, his damp hair coming loose from its binding, carrying himself with a stillness Jake hadn't seen on him before. Not the rigid, braced stillness of a man holding himself together. Something quieter than that. Something closer to peace, if peace was a thing Zuko's body knew how to hold.

"Did you really mean that, Uncle?"

Zuko's voice, when it finally came, sounded almost young—smaller than the voice that had challenged Zhao to a duel a few hours earlier, smaller than the voice that had lunged across a tent at the mention of a captured Avatar. Jake glanced sideways in time to see him watching Iroh with something uncertain in his face, waiting.

"Of course," Iroh said, without hesitation, without any of his usual theater. "I told you. Ginseng tea is my favorite."

Zuko huffed out something that wasn't quite a laugh, and the corner of his mouth pulled up in a faint, unguarded curve that Jake had learned, over three years, was rarer than almost anything else Zuko's face knew how to do. It didn't last more than a moment. But Jake saw it, and something in his own chest eased another degree at the sight of it.

He fell into step at Zuko's shoulder as they walked, close enough to be felt, the way he always positioned himself, and reached without a word into the small bag he'd carried down from the ship, pulling free the canteen he'd filled before they left. He held it out. Zuko took it without comment, drank, and handed it back, and that was the whole of what passed between them—no grand words, no acknowledgment of what either of them had just survived, just the same quiet exchange of care they'd traded a thousand times over three years of exile, dressed up tonight in the ordinary shape of water and cloth.

The ache in Jake's scarred hands hadn't left him. It sat under his gloves the way it always did, old and familiar, flaring faintly with the cooling evening air. But for the first time since Zuko had said the words *Agni Kai* in that tent, the ache didn't drag the throne room up behind it. He kept waiting for the memory to close over him the way it always did—the smell of ozone, the particular quality of silence before pain—and it simply didn't come. Somewhere on that stone platform, watching Zuko choose to strike ground instead of flesh, something that had been coiled tight in Jake's chest for three years had finally, quietly, let go.

Zuko was not his father. Jake had known that in the abstract for years, the way a person knows a fact without ever having tested its weight. Tonight he had watched it proven in front of two hundred soldiers and a beaten, furious commander, watched Zuko hold the power to ruin a man exactly the way Jake's own life had once been ruined, and choose, instead, to walk away.

The cool evening air came off the water in slow, salt-heavy drafts as they climbed the ramp toward their battered ship, and Jake breathed it in without the familiar tightness that usually rode alongside it in a Fire Nation port. Behind them, Ketu Harbor's lights were coming up one by one against the darkening sky, indifferent to what had happened in its arena, already moving on to whatever the next tide would bring. Ahead of them, the ship waited, rust-streaked and dented and entirely, wonderfully theirs.

Jake climbed the ramp beside Zuko, matching his pace without needing to think about it, and let himself believe, for the length of that walk at least, that they might come through the rest of this hunt just as intact as they'd come through tonight.
