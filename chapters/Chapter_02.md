# Chapter 2: The Breach

The engines had been screaming for the better part of an hour, a deep metal groan that Jake felt more than heard, running up through the deck plates and into the soles of his boots. Ahead, through curtains of thrown spray and the ship's own steam, the ice wall of the village rose pale and jagged against the dark water. It didn't look like much. A rampart of packed snow and hardened ice, built to keep out wind and wolves, not warships.

Jake pulled his hood up.

He did it the way he did everything before a fight — methodically, without letting his hands hurry. Hood first, drawn low enough to shadow his eyes. Then the collar, worked up stiff and high until it bit at his jaw. Last, his gloves, tugged snug at the wrist, checked twice for any gap where skin might show. Standard-issue cold-weather gear, the same as every soldier crowding the deck around him, and that was the point. He wanted to be exactly that. Standard. Forgettable. A shape in a uniform that nobody would look at twice.

He told himself it didn't matter how carefully he covered his face. Nobody down there would know him. Nobody down there could know him. He had been a baby, small enough to be carried under one arm, when he'd left — if leaving was even the word for what had happened to him. There was no one left in that village who could look up at a hooded figure on a warship's deck and see anything except an invader.

He pulled the hood down another half inch anyway.

Zuko stood at the very edge of the prow, three strides ahead of him, armored in full battle plate with his helmet catching the gray light in dull flashes. He hadn't moved in almost a minute. Just stood there with his shoulders set and his weight forward, like a man leaning into a wind only he could feel. Jake didn't need to see his face to know what it looked like. He'd watched Zuko wear that same stillness before drills, before confrontations with harbor officials, before every moment that mattered enough to make him stop performing anger and start rationing it.

Somewhere behind them, second-in-command Yorin barked an order down the line, and the deck crew braced against the rails. Jake braced too, out of habit more than need, one gloved hand finding the cold metal at his side.

He kept his eyes on Zuko's back. Not on the wall ahead, not on the huddled shapes he could already make out along its base — small, wrapped in furs, backing away from the water's edge in a way that made his chest go tight and airless. He made himself watch Zuko's shoulders instead. Watched for the twitch that would mean a stumble, the shift of weight that would mean Zuko meant to jump the rail early and lead the charge on foot before the ramp was even down. That was his job right now. Not the wall. Not the people behind it. Zuko's flanks, and nothing else.

It didn't quite work. He could still feel it at the edges — that ice-water pull in the back of his skull, some old current under everything else, whispering that this was southern water, this was southern snow, this was a shape of village he had never consciously seen before and somehow already recognized in his bones like a word almost remembered.

He shoved it down the way he shoved down everything. There would be time to feel things later, in the dark, in the narrow alcove off Zuko's cabin where nobody could see his face do whatever it wanted to do. Not here. Not with his hands needed.

The prow hit.

It came up through the whole ship at once — a shudder that threw men sideways along the rail, that rattled Jake's teeth in his skull and knocked a breath out of him he hadn't planned on losing. Below, the sound was worse than the feeling: a grinding, splintering roar as the ship's steel bow tore through packed ice like it was nothing more than crusted snow over a puddle, throwing up a wall of white debris that hung in the air a moment before raining down across the deck in stinging chips. Somewhere in the wreckage of sound Jake thought he heard something else — a higher note, thin and human, a cry cut off almost as soon as it started — and he made himself not look for its source.

The engines cut. In the silence that followed, steam began venting from vents along the hull in long exhausted hisses, rolling forward across the deck in low banks that caught the last gray light and turned the whole bow into something out of a fever dream — a ship dressed in fog, crouched at the ruins of a wall that had stood, by the look of the ice, for longer than Jake had been alive.

He watched Zuko's hand drop to his side. Ready.

The ramp released with a metallic shriek and began its slow descent, steam pouring out around its edges as it came down, down, until it struck the packed snow beyond the wreckage with a thud that Jake felt travel up through his own boots. Beyond it, through the drifting fog, he could see them now — dark shapes against white, drawing back, drawing together, someone shouting something in a dialect close enough to Fire Nation speech that Jake understood the shape of the fear in it even before he understood the words.

Zuko didn't wait for the ramp to fully settle. He was already moving, stepping off the prow and down into the mist with the guards falling into step around him, his cape snapping once behind him before the steam swallowed the shape of it whole.

Jake followed.

He didn't run to catch up. He never did — that wasn't his place, and Zuko wouldn't want it, not here, not in front of the crew. He simply matched the pace, half a step behind and off to the side, close enough to close the distance in a single stride if something needed closing. His boots found the ramp's grooved metal, then the packed and broken snow beyond it, and the cold came up through the soles of his boots like it belonged to him.

Maybe it did. He didn't let himself finish the thought.

Ahead, the mist was already starting to thin, and shapes were resolving into people — a knot of villagers pressed close together at the mouth of a shattered doorway, furs and braided hair and wide frightened eyes turning, one by one, toward the ruin of their wall and the armored men walking through it.

Jake kept his head down, his hood low, his gaze fixed on the back of Zuko's neck, and followed his prince into the wreckage of a home he had never been allowed to remember.

***

The village opened up in pieces as the mist thinned — a scatter of hide tents and one larger domed structure of packed snow, cookfires stamped out in haste, drying racks of fish overturned and forgotten. Jake counted heads without meaning to. Old women. Children clutching at furs twice their size. No men among them past a boy's age, and something in him noted that and filed it away before he could stop it — the empty space where fathers and grown sons should have stood, a war's worth of years pressing down on a handful of people who had nothing left to fight it with but bone clubs and spears carved for seals.

He hated how easily he understood the shape of that emptiness.

A shout cut through the murmur of the crowd, sharp with a bravado that didn't match the size of the boy giving it. He came fast across the packed snow — dark-skinned, wrapped in Water Tribe blue, war paint smeared crude and hurried across his face like he'd only just thought to put it on. A club, bone-handled, raised over his head. He couldn't have been older than Jake.

Jake's whole body coiled to move.

Then didn't.

Zuko had already turned toward the sound, unhurried, almost bored, and Jake read the set of his shoulders in half a heartbeat — no alarm in it, no need. This was a boy with a club charging a firebending prince in full plate. Zuko didn't need help with this. He would resent it, sharp and immediate, if Jake stepped between them now like Zuko couldn't be trusted to handle one furious teenager. So Jake stayed rooted where he was, half a step behind and to the side, and made himself watch instead of act.

It was almost worse, watching.

Zuko caught the club on a forearm brace, twisted, and sent it spinning into the snow. The boy staggered from the momentum of his own swing, and Zuko's boot found his chest a half-second later, dropping him hard onto his back with a grunt that carried across the whole clearing. He didn't get up right away. Jake watched him roll, gasping, and felt something ugly twist in his stomach that had nothing to do with pride in Zuko's form.

Zuko didn't spare the boy a second look. He was already moving toward the villagers, toward an old woman standing at the front of the huddled group with her chin lifted and her hand braced protectively against the shoulder of the girl beside her — younger, maybe Jake's own age, in a heavier fur-trimmed parka the color of deep water, her dark hair pulled back in loops at her temples. She had stepped half in front of the old woman without seeming to notice she'd done it, the way a person moves when their body decides something before their mind catches up.

Jake made himself not look at her face. He'd learned, over the walk down the ramp, that if he let his eyes catch on any one face too long, something in his chest cracked open that he needed sealed shut for the next several minutes.

Zuko seized the old woman by the arm.

"Where are you hiding him?" His voice carried, flat and hard, pitched to reach every huddled shape in the clearing. "He'd be about this age. Master of all elements."

The girl in blue made a sound — not quite a word, something closer to a cry bitten off at the root — and lunged forward like she meant to physically pry Zuko's hand loose. She didn't get the chance. Zuko released the old woman himself, shoving her back into the crowd, and lifted his free hand, fire arcing out over the villagers' heads in a bright violent stroke that scattered them backward, screaming.

"I know you're hiding him."

Jake's jaw ached. He realized after a moment that he'd been clenching it since the ramp, and made himself stop, made his face go carefully, deliberately blank behind the shadow of his hood. Watch the perimeter. Watch for threats. Don't watch the fire reflected in a dozen terrified faces that could have been faces he was born among.

The boy with the club was back on his feet.

He came again, wilder this time, closing fast with nothing but fury behind his swing, and Zuko ducked under it without breaking stride, caught the boy's arm, and used his own momentum to hurl him face-first into a drift of snow. There was a dry snap of wood — a spear, somewhere in the wreckage of the fight, breaking under someone's boot — and then the boy was scrambling up again, already reaching for something else at his hip.

Jake's attention had already started to drift outward, sweeping the treeline, the ruined wall, the huddled shapes for any sign of a second attacker, any weapon Zuko wasn't watching for—

He didn't see the boomerang.

It came from an angle he'd already dismissed as clear, a curved sweep of dark wood that struck the back of Zuko's helmet with a crack loud enough to make Jake flinch before he understood what he was hearing. Zuko staggered forward a half step, more startled than hurt, and Jake was already moving before he'd finished deciding to, closing the gap between them in three strides — too late, the threat already spent, the boomerang clattering away into the snow.

Zuko didn't need him. Zuko was turning, already furious, fire kindling at his fingertips as he rounded on the boy in blue with an expression that promised the next strike wouldn't be a shove into snow.

Jake had just enough time to hate himself for the miss before the penguin hit.

It came out of nowhere — a small, round, white-bellied shape moving faster than anything that size had any right to move, and astride it, feet braced on its back like he'd been born there, a boy Jake's own age or younger, bald, marked with a pale blue arrow that ran down his forehead and vanished beneath a collar of animal fur. He shot between two tents in a spray of thrown snow and took Zuko's legs out from under him without slowing at all, prince and armor going down together in an undignified heap.

For one full second, nobody moved.

Jake stood frozen at the edge of it, hand still half-raised toward a threat that had already resolved itself into Zuko sprawled face-first in the snow, and something in his carefully maintained read of the battlefield simply broke. He had tracked club, spear, boomerang — every weapon a soldier might carry, every angle a soldier might use. He had not tracked penguin.

The boy slid to a stop, planted his feet, and looked down at Zuko with an expression too easy, too unbothered, to belong on a battlefield at all.

"Looking for me?"

Zuko pushed himself up out of the snow, and for a moment he didn't move to attack. He just stared.

"You're the airbender." It wasn't quite a question. His eyes dropped to the boy's forehead, to the arrow vanishing under his collar, then came back up, and Jake — close enough now to see it — watched something in Zuko's face come apart and rebuild itself in the space of a breath. "You're the Avatar?"

The boy planted his staff in the snow and offered something that might have been meant as a reassuring smile, if the moment had allowed for one. "That's me."

"I've spent three years preparing for this. Training. Meditating." Zuko's voice had gone low and strange, the fury still there but buried now under something closer to disbelief. "You're a child."

"Well, you're just a teenager."

Jake did not move. He stood at the edge of the scene with his heart going hard against his ribs, and underneath the dread and the cold and the ringing place at the back of his skull where the boomerang had nearly landed on him instead, something else surfaced that he had no name ready for. Three years. Three years of drills before dawn, of Zuko pacing the deck at night unable to sleep, of the whole shape of both their lives bent around the idea of a vanished master powerful enough to end a war. And here he was. A boy with a stick and a penguin, cracking jokes in the wreckage of a village he'd apparently come back to save.

Zuko's hands ignited.

He came at the boy in a flat, controlled arc of fire, and the boy — Jake still didn't have a name to put to him, only *the boy with the arrow* — spun the staff in a wide sweep that opened into something like a fan, catching the flame and turning it aside in a gust that threw snow in every direction. He didn't press the advantage. He didn't come back at Zuko with anything at all. Instead his gaze went past Zuko's shoulder, out to where the villagers had drawn together into a tight, trembling knot at the mouth of the ruined wall, the old woman still upright at their center with the girl in blue at her side, and something in his stance changed. The staff came down. The fight went out of him all at once, visibly, like a held breath finally let go.

"If I go with you," the boy said, "will you promise to leave everyone alone?"

Jake watched Zuko go still.

He knew that stillness. It was the same one from the prow, the one that meant Zuko was doing math he didn't want to be doing — victory laid out in front of him, cheap and sudden and nothing like what he'd built himself up for. Zuko's jaw worked once. Then he gave a single short nod.

That was all it took. The boy lowered the staff the rest of the way and let two of the guards close in around him without resistance, holding his wrists out ahead of him like he'd rehearsed it. Behind him, the girl in blue made a sound of protest and started forward, only for the old woman's hand to close hard around her arm, holding her back. Jake made himself look away from both of them before whatever was rising in his chest could reach his face.

He fell into step at Zuko's shoulder as the guards marched their prisoner back toward the ramp, close enough to catch the faint tremor still running through Zuko's sword hand, close enough that if the villagers surged now — and for one taut moment, watching the fury and grief flare across a dozen faces at once, he thought they might — he would be the first thing between them and their prince. Nobody moved. The girl in blue was crying without trying to hide it, her arms wrapped around the old woman instead of reaching for the boy being led away, and Jake kept his eyes fixed on the middle distance until the ramp's steam swallowed the whole scene behind him.

His hands, inside their gloves, would not quite stop shaking. He balled them into fists at his sides and didn't let anyone see.

***

The quarters were quiet in a way that felt almost obscene after the noise of the village.

Jake pulled the door shut behind them and heard the latch catch, and for a moment neither of them said anything. The engines had picked up their steady deep-water hum again, carrying them out past the broken wall, past the huddled shapes he refused to picture, back into open water. Zuko stood in the middle of the room still in half his armor, breathing like he'd run the whole way here instead of simply walked, staring at nothing.

A knock came at the door — brisk, two raps — and Jake opened it to find one of the deck guards holding out the Avatar's staff, its pale wood strange and light-looking against the man's gauntleted hands. "General Iroh said to bring this here, sir. For the prince."

Jake took it without a word and shut the door on him.

He turned to hand it over and found Zuko already reaching, some rough eagerness in the motion that didn't quite match the tightness around his eyes. Zuko took the staff in both hands and simply held it a moment, turning it slowly, the way a person turns over a coin trying to decide if it's real. It was carved in delicate spiraling patterns along its length, the wood pale and worn soft with handling, and it looked absurd in that room — a fragile, ancient, living thing surrounded by riveted steel and the dull red of Fire Nation plating.

"This will make an excellent gift for my father." Zuko turned the staff over in his hands, watching lamplight catch the worn grooves. It sounded like something he'd already said once today, out loud, when it still had an audience. He'd told the boy as much on deck, hands bound, right before the guards led him below. I suppose you wouldn't know much about fathers, being raised by monks. Somehow it sounded less like triumph the second time, alone in a quiet room with no one left to hear it but Jake.

Jake said nothing. There was nothing to say to that, and Zuko didn't seem to want an answer so much as a place to put the sentence down.

He watched Zuko set the staff carefully against the wall near his cot — carefully, like it might break, like it was worth something beyond its use as a trophy — and then stand there a moment longer with his back to the room, shoulders still rigid under the plate.

Jake stepped closer and reached up without asking, the way he always did, fingers finding the clasp at the side of Zuko's helmet and easing it free. Zuko let him. He tipped his head slightly to make it easier, an old unconscious habit built over three years of exactly this, and Jake set the helmet aside and turned Zuko's face gently toward the lamp to check the spot where the boomerang had struck. A shallow red mark along the ridge of bone, already purpling at the edge. Nothing split. Nothing that needed binding.

"You'll bruise," Jake said, quiet, the first words either of them had spoken to each other since the ramp. "Doesn't look worse than that."

Zuko exhaled through his nose, something that wasn't quite a laugh. "A twelve-year-old hit me harder than most of the men in this crew."

Jake's hands moved down without being asked, checking the give of the shoulder plate where the penguin's charge had knocked him flat, testing for anything that pulled wrong. Zuko didn't flinch, didn't complain, just stood there and let himself be checked over the way he only ever did with Jake, the way he'd never once allowed a physician aboard to do without an argument first.

"He's a child," Zuko said, and this time it came out lower, almost bewildered. "Three years, Jake. Three years, and it's a child riding a penguin."

Jake didn't answer that either. He didn't trust what would come out if he opened his mouth — something too close to *he's younger than I am and we're going to lock him in a metal room in the belly of your father's warship* — so instead he finished his check in silence, stepped back, and let the quiet stretch out until it started to feel less like tension and more like something almost bearable. Almost peace. The ship rocked gently under them, ice water sliding past the hull, and for one breath Jake allowed himself to feel something that might have been relief. Nobody down there had burned. Nobody down there had died. The old woman, the girl in blue, all of them — spared, because a boy with an arrow on his head had cared more about a village of strangers than about his own freedom.

The alarm ripped through the ship a heartbeat later, a shrieking klaxon that seemed to come from every wall at once, and beneath it, faint and distant, the sound of running feet and a guard's voice shouting something Jake couldn't make out.

Zuko's whole body went rigid.

"He's loose." It wasn't a question. Zuko was already moving, snatching his helmet back up off the table, eyes flicking once toward the staff propped against the wall. "He'll come for that. He has to." He looked at Jake, and whatever softness had been in the room a moment ago was gone, burned away in an instant. "Sweep the outer corridors. Cut off anything that leads to the deck. If he gets past you—"

"He won't."

Zuko held his eyes for one more second, something unspoken passing between them that didn't need words — *be careful, don't die, come back* — and then he was already turning away, already reaching for the lamp to douse it, settling into the dark of the room to wait like a hunter in a blind.

Jake didn't wait to watch him finish. He was already moving, out the door and into the corridor at a dead run, the memory of a boy who could take Zuko's legs out with a penguin sitting cold and heavy in his chest the whole way.

***

The corridor lights swung in their brackets from the alarm's vibration, throwing long stuttering shadows across the metal walls. Jake moved fast and low, checking each junction before committing to it, the way three years of drills had taught him.

He found the first guard slumped against a bulkhead twenty feet down, breathing but out cold, no mark on him that Jake could see — no burn, no blood, nothing but a man who looked like he'd been hit by something that left no bruise at all. A second guard sat wedged against a doorframe further on, blinking dazed at the ceiling, muttering something about wind that didn't make any sense until Jake remembered the arrow on the boy's forehead and understood exactly what kind of bending had put him there.

He was three corridors further when the crash came — a deep, structural boom from somewhere behind him, close to Zuko's quarters, loud enough to rattle a support beam in its housing.

Jake turned and ran.

He came around the last corner just as the door to the quarters burst outward off one hinge, and a small blurred shape shot past him close enough to stir the hair at his temple — a rush of displaced air, gone before Jake's hand had even finished reaching for it, already vanishing down the corridor toward the stairs to the deck. He didn't chase it. Zuko first. The thought arrived before he'd finished deciding anything, already carrying his feet through the ruined doorway.

He found Zuko on the floor amid the wreckage of his own cot, half-buried under a mattress that had been driven with enough force to leave a dent in the ceiling plating above him. For one horrible second Jake's chest went hollow — and then Zuko shoved the mattress off with a snarl, already scrambling upright, favoring nothing, cursing under his breath in a way that told Jake more than any wound check could have. Winded. Furious. Whole.

"Where—"

"Deck," Jake said, already turning. "He went for the stairs."

Zuko didn't waste breath answering. He was already moving before Jake finished the word, and they went up the corridor together at a dead run, boots ringing on the metal grating.

The cold hit Jake like a wall when they burst out onto the deck — the enclosed heat of the ship's interior gone in an instant, replaced by wind sharp enough to sting his eyes and the open gray sweep of the southern sky. He caught sight of the boy immediately, sprinting along the rail toward the stern with a strange loping grace, and then watched him vault the railing entirely, snapping the staff open into something with wide fabric wings that caught the wind and lifted him clean off the ship.

Zuko didn't slow down.

Jake's stomach dropped as Zuko hit the rail without breaking stride and simply threw himself off after him.

He didn't think. He was at the rail himself a half-second later, hands white-knuckled on the cold metal, watching Zuko's fingers close around the boy's ankle in midair and both of them plummet, tangled together, the glider spinning uselessly away. They hit the lower deck hard, a tangle of limbs and armor skidding across wet metal, and for one endless breath neither of them moved.

Then, distantly, a deep low sound rolled across the water — not thunder, not engine, something animal and enormous — and Jake looked up to see a shape descending out of the clouds that made his mind simply refuse it for a second: a creature the size of a small ship itself, six-legged, white-furred, dropping toward the water on nothing but the air moving beneath it. Two riders clung to a saddle on its back. Even from this distance Jake could make out the blue of Water Tribe clothing, and something in him went cold and still, recognizing without wanting to that this was no rescue mounted by soldiers or spirits. This was a family coming for their own.

Below, Zuko was already rolling to his feet, blood at his lip, eyes locked on the boy scrambling backward across the deck toward the rail. Jake gripped the upper railing hard enough to feel the cold bite through his gloves, and did not look away.

***

The boy — pinned against the rail with nowhere left to run — went still for the space of one breath, and then Zuko's fire caught him across the shoulder and sent him tumbling backward over the edge into the sea.

He didn't come back up.

Jake's hands tightened on the railing hard enough to hurt. The water below churned pale and black in the ship's shadow, closing over the spot where the boy had gone under, and for three, four, five long seconds nothing broke the surface at all. Zuko stood at the rail above it, chest heaving, staring down like he expected the boy to simply resurface and swim for shore.

Then the light came.

It rose out of the water in a column that dwarfed the ship's own hull — a spinning, roaring pillar of white-blue sea, climbing higher than the mast, throwing spray in every direction hard enough to sting Jake's face from twenty feet away. At its base, dimly visible through the churn, the boy hung suspended, eyes and the arrow on his skin all burning the same searing white, his whole small body wreathed in something that no longer looked like a child at all.

Jake had never seen anything like it. He had heard stories — every soldier on this ship had, campfire tales about the Avatar being something more than a bender, something the spirits themselves moved through — and he had filed them away the same place he filed folktales about White Herons and lucky charms. Looking at it now, feeling the water's pull in his own blood answer that light like a held note finally struck, he understood that none of it had been exaggeration.

The waterspout collapsed inward and slammed down onto the deck as a single towering wave.

It hit the crew first — a wall of black water sweeping men off their feet, dragging them screaming toward the rail — and Jake didn't think, didn't plan, simply moved. He dropped low, one hand catching the deck grating for an anchor point, and let the wave's own momentum answer to something underneath his skin instead of fighting it. He couldn't stop that much water. He didn't try. He only asked it, the way Iroh had taught him to ask fire's opposite of everything — *where do you want to go, and can I make it somewhere safer* — and felt the current under the surface bend, just slightly, just enough. A nearby soldier who should have gone straight over the rail instead spun sideways into the base of the mast and grabbed hold, coughing, alive. Another current he steered wide of Zuko's legs entirely, buying him a half-second more purchase against the tide dragging at his boots.

No one would ever see it as anything but the sea being cruel in one direction and merciful in another. That was the only reason he dared to touch it at all.

Zuko went over the rail anyway, the wave simply too much even for what little Jake could redirect, and Jake's stomach dropped as he watched him vanish over the side — only to reappear a half-second later clinging white-knuckled to the ship's massive anchor chain, boots scrabbling for purchase against the hull.

On deck, the boy had collapsed where the wave left him, spent and small again, no trace of the terrible light left in him. A dark-skinned boy in war paint — the one from the club, from the boomerang — was already scrambling across the drenched deck toward him, snatching up the fallen staff, and found Zuko instead, hauling himself back up over the rail hand over hand. The boy didn't hesitate. He jabbed one finger square into Zuko's forehead and shoved, and Zuko went right back over the side with an undignified shout, catching the chain again on the way down.

The girl in blue had reached the deck too now, dropping from the great white creature's saddle with water already curling and freezing at her fingertips, and Jake watched — genuinely watched, some part of him going quiet and attentive despite everything else happening — as she froze two advancing guards solid from the ankles up with two clean, sure motions. No hesitation in it. No wasted movement. Untrained, maybe, rough at the edges compared to what he imagined proper Water Tribe forms should look like, but real, and strong, and unmistakably his own element moving through someone else's hands for the first time in his life where he could actually watch it happen instead of feeling it happen inside himself alone.

He didn't have time to feel whatever that stirred in him. Iroh appeared at the rail, hauling Zuko up and over by the collar with startling strength for a man his age, both of them soaked to the bone and streaming water onto the deck. Zuko didn't even pause to catch his breath. He turned toward the great white creature now rising off the deck with its passengers aboard, fire already gathering in both fists, and Iroh's hands came up beside his a heartbeat later, their combined blast roaring out across the widening gap of water and sky.

The boy on the creature's back — barely upright, barely conscious — twisted at the last moment and threw one arm out. The fireball bent, curved, and struck the ice cliff towering behind the ruined village instead of its intended target.

The cliff face groaned.

Jake heard it before he understood what it meant — a deep, splitting crack that ran the length of the ice wall — and then the whole cliff came down at once, an avalanche of white thunder that swallowed the sky and buried the front third of the ship under tons of collapsing ice in a single deafening roar.

The impact threw Jake flat against the deck grating, ice and freezing spray raining down over him, and for a long moment there was nothing in the world but noise and cold and the ship groaning in protest beneath him.

When it finally settled, the great white creature was already a shrinking shape against the gray sky, carrying its four passengers away toward the horizon, and the ship — Zuko's ship, his home for three years — sat half-swallowed in ice, listing hard to one side, silent except for the wind and the last settling creak of snow finding its footing.

***

Jake pushed himself up off the grating with his ears still ringing, ice sliding off his shoulders in a small avalanche of its own. His eyes found Zuko before anything else did — older instinct than the last three minutes of chaos — and held there just long enough to take him in: upright, soaked through, breathing hard but standing on his own power at the rail, no blood, no wrong angle to his limbs. Whole.

Only then did the rest of the deck come into focus. It had become something unrecognizable — buried under drifts, listing at an angle that made every step feel deliberate, littered with men groaning their way upright or lying still enough that he made a note to check them first.

He crossed to the nearest crewman, a hand under the man's arm, hauling him up out of a snowbank and checking his eyes before moving to the next. Someone was bleeding from a cut along his hairline. Someone else had a wrist bent wrong and was staring at it like he hadn't decided yet whether it hurt. Jake worked down the line of them the way he always did after a fight, quiet and fast, more useful than words, and somewhere in the middle of it he registered, distantly, that Zuko still hadn't moved from the rail, staring up at the empty gray sky where the great white creature had disappeared.

He didn't move. Didn't shiver, though Jake could see from here that he should have been. Just stood there with water running off his armor and his jaw set hard enough to ache.

Jake finished with the last of the nearby crewmen and crossed the tilted deck toward him, boots finding uncertain footing on the ice-slicked metal.

"That kid." Zuko's voice was low, rough, more to the frozen air than to Jake specifically. "That kid, Uncle." Iroh had come to stand a few feet off, hands folded into his soaked sleeves, saying nothing. Zuko didn't look at either of them. His eyes stayed fixed on the horizon. "Just did this."

Jake didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that would help.

"I won't underestimate him again." Zuko turned at last, and whatever had cracked open in his face back in the quarters — the disbelief, the flicker of something almost like doubt — was gone now, sealed back under something harder and colder than before. He raised his voice to carry across the ruined deck. "Dig the ship out. We follow them the moment we're free."

The crew, battered and dripping, began to move. Someone found a shovel wedged half-buried near the fallen ice. Someone else started barking orders down toward the lower decks, checking for flooding, for damage below the waterline. Jake watched it happen the way he watched everything — cataloguing what needed doing, what could wait, who needed a hand and who just needed to be told to keep moving.

He crossed the last few feet to Zuko without being asked and settled into place beside him, close enough that his shoulder nearly brushed the sodden edge of Zuko's cape. He didn't offer words. He didn't ask if Zuko was hurt, because he could see he wasn't, not really, not in any way that mattered right now. He simply stood there, present, soaked through the same as everyone else, and let his stillness answer whatever Zuko hadn't asked.

Zuko didn't look at him. But his shoulders eased, fractionally, the way they only ever did with Jake standing this close.

Jake looked past him instead, out at the ice burying half their ship, at the long gray line of open water where the Avatar had simply flown away into the sky like the whole raid, the whole capture, the whole three years of exile and two of searching, had been nothing more than a prologue before the real hunt began. Two years of empty water and false leads had felt endless while he was living through them. Looking at the wreckage around him now, he understood with a kind of settling cold that had nothing to do with the wind that those years had been the easy part.

He picked up a length of splintered decking half-buried in snow, testing its weight as a makeshift lever, and moved to help the nearest knot of crewmen digging at the ice choking the bow.
