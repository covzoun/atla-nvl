# Chapter 1: The Reality of Exile

The freshwater line had frozen to the rail sometime before dawn, and now it fought Jake for every inch.

He worked it free with a mallet, one careful rap at a time. A line chipped in a hurry snapped at the ice's mercy, and the ship needed every strand of rope it had. Frost had crept over the deck in a fine grey fur. It lay on the rigging, on the belaying pins, on the shoulders of the two men at the bow who stood hunched in their coats like birds. Every exhale went up white and stayed a while.

Three years this had been his life. Cold water, colder work, and a ship that complained about both.

His hands complained first, before his back and before his knees. It was a deep ache that ran under the leather of his gloves and settled in the scars, as if the old burns kept a record of every degree of cold the sea sent down. Iroh had given him the gloves in his first week aboard and spoken of them once, about sun and linen, and never mentioned them again. Jake wore them without discussion. They hid what they were meant to hide and protected what needed protecting, and out here that was the whole philosophy of a thing done well.

He was sixteen. He had come aboard at thirteen with his hands wrapped in linen and his name struck off every list in the palace but this one, and the crew had spent three years deciding what to make of him. Most of them had settled on *useful*. A few had settled on *the prince's shadow*. One, a deckhand with a bad ear, had settled on *the water one*, said it once, and never said it again, because Jake had put a bucket of seawater over his boots and walked off, and the man had laughed and let it stand.

He heeled the loosened ice overboard in a grey slide, coiled the freed line, and moved to the next stretch.

Further along the deck, the prince was not working.

Zuko had been pacing since the second dog watch, a tight worn track between the mast and the rail, his boots striking the frost with more force than footing required. He had come up in his under-robe with the sleeves pushed back. Steam lifted off his bare forearms where the heat inside him leaked out without his noticing. His topknot had come half loose in the wind. Every few strides he stopped at the rail and looked out at the rolling dark water, and every few strides he began again. Jake kept count of it. He had long since learned not to trust the count.

"Anything?" Zuko asked, not looking at him. He never looked at him when he asked it. He asked the horizon, mostly, and Jake happened to be standing nearby.

"No smoke. No sails."

Jake glanced out at the ice all the same, because Zuko would want to know he had actually checked. "Same as an hour ago."

Zuko made a low sound in his throat that was not quite a word and went back to the rail, knuckles whitening where he gripped the cold metal. Jake let him have the silence. There was nothing useful to say to three years of the same horizon. Zuko had never wanted comfort so much as company — someone else awake and watching, so the waiting did not feel like something he was doing alone.

Jake picked the ice free of the next stretch of line and let his eyes drift out past the rail, to where the floes broke apart into open black water and then closed together again into endless white.

It caught him before he expected it to. It always did.

There was nothing about the ice that should have meant anything to him. It was only water, frozen and refrozen a thousand different ways, stretching out flat and blinding under a colorless sky, broken here and there by pressure ridges that threw long blue shadows across the snow.

He had never set foot on southern ice before this voyage. He had no memory of a village, a home, a mother's face, nothing that this landscape should have been able to reach.

And still something in him kept turning toward it. It was like standing in a room where somebody had been kind to you once and being unable to remember who. Some old current under his ribs recognized the cold, the light, the particular grey-white color of the sky, however flatly his mind refused to.

He had stopped trying to name it a long time ago. Naming it only made it worse.

His hand had gone still on the rope. He noticed that before he noticed why, and made himself start moving again, working the frost out of the fibers with more attention than the task required. Cold got into a man's head out here, that was all. Iroh said so often enough, usually right before pouring tea. Long stretches of white could make anyone imagine things. Faces in the ice. Voices in the wind. The phantom sense of somewhere they had never been. It was the kind of thing that happened to sailors on long postings. It did not mean anything.

He told himself that, mostly believed it, and went back to his knots.

The ladder well banged open behind him and three men came up the companionway in single file, their boots ringing on the grating. The watch change. The oldest of them was already talking, complaining about the cold in the boiler room and the cold in the galley and the special cold of a man who has to leave his bunk at the hour when the bunk has finally got warm. The other two laughed. Then all three saw Zuko at the rail and the laughing stopped.

None of the three looked at him for longer than a moment. They walked past him the careful way men walk past a dog they know.

"Lieutenant," Jake said. "The galley line is clear."

Jee came up last, his arms crossed over an oil-stained coat, his face carrying four days of the same complaint. He looked at the frozen rail, at the coil Jake had cleared, at the two men at the bow who had not moved all morning, and then at the prince.

"He hasn't slept," Jee said. It was not quite a question.

"Passed his night on the deck," Jake said.

"Noticed." Jee took a breath and let it go. "Three years of ice and he paces it like it owes him money."

"He'll want the ship trimmed before the light comes. Two knots is two knots."

Jee looked at him a moment, then grunted, which was as close as the man came to agreement before coffee. "Tell him if he wants the foresail retrimmed, we do it in daylight. I am not sending men up the mast in the dark for a knot we will lose to the current anyway."

"I'll tell him."

"You'll tell him in your own way," Jee said, and said it without malice, which was how Jake knew it was nearly a compliment. He turned and went aft, bellowing at the watch, and the three men scattered to their places.

Jake finished the last length of line, stood, and felt his knees complain about the standing. He gathered the coil and carried it to the pin rail.

Down the deck Zuko had started pacing again, faster now. His bare arms were goosefleshed despite the heat still coming off him in faint visible waves. Jake watched him from the corner of his eye. The tight shoulders. The restless hands that kept curling and releasing at his sides. The steady fall of his gaze across the same stretch of horizon. Three years had worn most of the softness out of Zuko. What was left underneath was harder, thinner, strung tight enough that Jake had learned to feel the tension in the air before Zuko ever said a word about it.

He moved himself along the deck toward the rail. It was a distance he had learned by feel over three years of shared decks and shared silences, and he kept it now without thinking. Near enough to be spoken to. Not near enough to crowd him.

"It's only the cold," he said, mostly to himself.

He turned his attention back to the horizon Zuko was so certain would eventually give something up, and let the ship's slow grinding progress through the floes fill the silence between them.

---

The light came that night.

Jake was below when it happened, in the galley, thawing the last of the freshwater line over the cook's stove and drinking the cook's tea because the cook owed him for the line. The ship had taken on a smell by nightfall that he was used to and did not like: wet wool, iron, and the sourness that came off men who had been cold for a week. Somebody was losing at tiles in the mess, and somebody else was telling him exactly how badly, and Jake had one shoulder against the bulkhead and his eyes half shut and was wondering whether it was worth the climb to his alcove.

Then the ship changed.

It came up through the deck, a low shudder, and then voices, and then boots, and then somebody shouting up the ladder well. Jake set the mug down and was moving before he had decided to.

On deck the cold hit his face like a hand. The men were all at the port rail. They were not talking.

A column stood on the horizon.

It came up out of the ice itself, so far off that he could not see the break, and it was the color of the inside of a lamp. It went up and up, thin and clean, and where it met the sky it lit the cloud from underneath in a slow bloom that spread along the whole southern rim of the world. The ice threw it back. For a moment every crack and pressure ridge stood out blue-white and hard as engraving, and the deck planks were lit like noon, and the men's faces were lit, and Jake could see that every one of them had forgotten the cold.

It stayed for the length of a held breath. Then it thinned. Then it was gone, and the dark came down over the ice again, and the wind was the only sound on deck.

Jake's hands were cold. Not the ache. A flat, ringing cold, as if he had closed them on a chain.

"Get my uncle."

Zuko had not raised his voice. He had the rail in both hands and his face turned south, and the torchlight from the aft housing caught the edge of his scar and left the rest of him in shadow.

"Get my uncle now," he said again, and this time a soldier moved.

Iroh came up with his nightrobe belted and his hair down and his feet in slippers. He took one look at his nephew. Then he looked at the place where the light had been, and he took his time about the last three steps.

"That was not a comet," Zuko said.

"No," Iroh said.

"That was not weather."

"No."

"It came out of the ice. There is nothing in the ice."

"Nothing with people in it," Iroh agreed, mildly, and Jake heard the shape of the argument before it was made. "Prince Zuko. It is late, the sea is dark, and there will be a great deal of light in the morning to look at it by. Come down. I will have tea brought."

"I don't want tea."

"The jasmine is very good."

"I don't want your tea, Uncle, I want —" Zuko turned, and the light from the aft housing caught his whole face, and the words stopped. He breathed in once through his nose, hard, as Iroh had taught him for three years. "That was a bending light. Nothing natural makes a light like that. You know it."

"I know it was bright," Iroh said. "I know that we have been down this road forty times, and I know that the last time we chased a light in the sky it was the reflection off a whale's back, and it cost us nine days of coal."

The crew were watching. Jake was watching the crew. Two of them had found reasons to look at their boots, and one, the signals man, was watching the prince with the fixed, hopeful attention of a man who would very much like to see something happen and would not be blamed for it.

"Nine days," Zuko said. "You think I care about nine days of coal."

"I think you have two hundred men aboard this ship who care about nine days of coal," Iroh said.

Zuko came away from the rail. For a moment Jake thought he would shout, and the men thought so too, and the signals man actually took half a step backwards. But Zuko had learned that much in three years, at least: how to keep a thing inside in front of the crew.

Then he did not keep it inside.

"Helmsman," he said, and the man was there, of course he was there, at the top of the wheel housing with his hands already on the spokes. "Bring her about. South-southeast. A course for the light."

"Prince Zuko," Iroh said.

"I will have the ship at that ice by morning," Zuko said. "If there is nothing there, I will look at your jasmine tea and tell you that you were right, and you may tell me so all the way home." His voice cracked on *home* and he pushed it flat again. "But it is not nothing. It is not a whale. Something made that light, and it came from the south, and for three years the one thing that has been true about the south is that it is empty. Now it isn't. Helmsman, plain to it, and tell the engine room I want all the speed the boilers will give me."

The helmsman looked at Iroh. Iroh looked at the ice. Then Iroh said, "Plain to it," in a voice that had been a general's voice once, and went to the rail.

The deck broke out into movement. Men to the lines, men to the ladder well, a boy running aft with the order for the boiler room. Above them the sail cracked and filled. Under them the deck took on a deep, grinding note. The ship leaned into her turn and began to break heavier ice than she had broken all winter. The floes went by the hull with a noise like the world's largest door being forced.

Jake stayed where he was until the deck had cleared around him. Then he went to Iroh.

The old man was looking south with his hands in his sleeves and his face was doing nothing at all, which meant it was doing something.

"He'll not sleep," Jake said.

"No."

"I'll keep the all-night watch with him. He'll want to be on the deck when we're close enough to see."

"That is not what I am worried about," Iroh said.

Jake waited. Iroh took a breath and did not spend it on the thing he was worried about, which was his way.

"Bring him a cup anyway," Iroh said. "The jasmine. He will not drink it, but the cup will be warm, and he will keep his hands round it, and that is a kind of sleep for a man like my nephew."

Jake nodded and went below.

The boiler room was the only warm place on the ship. It was also the loudest. The chief engineer had his shirt off in the middle of the ice. The men who fed the furnace had their sleeves rolled to the red line of the flame. When Jake came down the iron stair the chief looked up and did not need to ask who had sent him.

"All the speed we can make," Jake said, because there was no use softening a thing that could not be softened. "He wants us at the ice by morning."

"Morning," the chief said. "By morning, at the speed he wants, we will have put eleven days of coal through the furnaces in one. You tell him that."

"Tell him yourself."

The chief had been three years on this ship and had been the only man aboard who could say such things to the prince and survive, and he took his time about answering. "I'll tell him when the numbers are bad enough that he has to hear them," he said at last. "They are not bad enough yet. Take the tea and get out of my boiler room."

Jake took the tea and got out of his boiler room.

On deck the wind had picked up with the turn. The light was gone from the south and there was nothing to see, no glow, no column, only the dark and the grinding and the whole ship leaning into it. Zuko was at the bow with his hands on the rail and the sea coming up between the floes and breaking there in white sheets that were nearly black in the dark.

Jake put the cup into his hand. Zuko took it because it was there. He did not drink it.

"Well?" Zuko said, after a while.

"Jee says the foresail will be retrimmed at first light and not before. He won't send men up the mast in the dark to chase a knot."

Zuko's mouth moved. It was not a smile, but it came from the same building. "He said that to you."

"He said it near me."

"They think I don't hear it," Zuko said. "They think I hear the ship and not the men." He turned the cup in his hands. "Let him keep the foresail. We'll want the mastmen rested." He looked back south. "This coward's hundred years in hiding are over."

Jake did not answer. It had been a long time since he had answered that sentence, and there was nothing in the world he could put next to it that would not be either a lie or a knife.

He stood half a pace behind the prince's left shoulder and watched the darkness go by, and somewhere out past it, in country he had never seen and could not remember, a light had come up out of the ice and gone out again.

---

Zuko drilled the deck at first light.

The cold had come back hard in the night and the planks were glassy with it. Two of the off-watch men stood at the mast in padded coats, their knees already bent for the fire. Iroh sat in a chair that had been carried up from his cabin for the purpose, a blanket over his legs and a cup of something in one hand.

"Again," Iroh said.

Zuko came across the deck. He was fast; he had always been fast, faster than the men he drilled. He threw fire in a wide low sweep and the two soldiers blocked it high and low, and the flames broke on their forearms and bloomed off into the cold. Zuko landed, pivoted, and threw again, one arm and then the other, the fire coming off him in plates. The deck hissed where it struck. The men gave ground, took it back, gave it again.

"Enough," Iroh said.

Zuko stopped. His chest was working.

"Power in firebending comes from the breath, not the muscles." Iroh set the cup down and got up, and the blanket slid off his knees and he did not bother with it. "The breath becomes energy in the body. The energy extends past the limbs and becomes —" he put a small, lazy line of fire out across the deck that turned to nothing a foot short of his nephew's chest "— fire. You are throwing your shoulders around like a man rowing a boat. Get it right this time."

Jake, at the pin rail with a dozen small jobs in his hands, watched Zuko's jaw set. It was the face of a boy who had heard the same sentence four hundred times and had decided, every time, that this time it was an accusation.

"I have drilled this sequence all day," Zuko said. "Teach me the next set. I'm more than ready."

"You are impatient," Iroh said.

"I am not a child."

"No." Iroh sat down again. "You are a prince with a great deal of fire and no patience, and those are worse things to take into a fight. You have yet to master your basics." He raised his voice, because that was what he did instead of shouting. "Drill it again."

Zuko's teeth came together.

Then he turned and kicked.

The fire came off his foot in a single tight hammer of flame, and it was not aimed at the man, not really, no more than a slap is aimed at a face. The soldier got his forearm up, and the block did not hold. The blast took him across the chest and put him on his back on the icy deck, and it caught the front of his coat, and the wool went up all at once, with a smell like burning hair.

The deck stopped.

Jake was already moving. He did not run. Running made men remember the fire. He crossed the deck with the water from the channel gutter following him, a rope of it the width of his wrist, and he put it on the man's chest and rolled it and the flame went out under it in a gout of steam. Then he took the second ribbon of water, the one still in the air, and turned it over the man's arm and up to the shoulder, checking with the flat of it where the cloth had gone in.

The soldier was swearing steadily and without any particular words. The smell of burned wool came off him in the wind.

"Easy," Jake said. He was on one knee in the wet. "Let me see the arm."

"It's nothing, sir."

"Let me see it anyway."

The man let him. Jake peeled the coat back at the cuff and there was a red line running up the inside of the forearm, hot and shining, and a blister already coming up on the back of the wrist where a fold of cloth had held the heat. Not deep. It would hurt for a week and stiffen him for three days.

Jake pulled the glove off his right hand to feel it.

The men had seen his hands before. They had seen them a hundred times, in the galley, on the lines, in the wet; the ship had stopped making anything of them sometime in the first year. But the new men had not. There was a boy of maybe fifteen at the mast with a bucket in his hands, and Jake saw him see it. The whole underside of the palm was gone smooth and shining where the skin had burned and healed wrong. The boy looked, and then made himself look away. And then, which was worse, he made himself look back and keep looking, being brave about it.

"Go to the galley," Jake said to the burned man, "and tell the cook I said salve and a clean wrap, and then tell him I said you're to sit down for an hour. That's an order from me. If he argues, tell him I'll come and argue."

"Sir."

"And then come back up and take the fender off the starboard quarter and mend the stitching on it. It's had a hole in it since the last blow. Nobody's had the hands for it and you'll have one good hand and an hour to spare, so you'll do it, and it'll be the best fender on the ship, and that will be that."

The man looked at him, and the anger in his face gave up and went somewhere else. "Yes, sir."

Jake helped him up. The man went below with his good hand wrapped round his bad arm, swearing quietly, and the boy at the mast found something urgent to do with the bucket.

Zuko had not moved.

He stood where the kick had left him, with his fists closed and his breath going in and out through his nose, and he was looking at the deck where the man had fallen. He was not looking at Iroh, and he was not looking at Jake. His jaw was working.

"The sages say the Avatar is the last airbender," he said, at last, and his voice was very even, which was how Jake knew how much it cost him. "He must be over a hundred years old. He has had a century to master the four elements. I will need more than my basics to fight him."

"You have been drilling this sequence all day," Iroh said.

"I will need —"

"You will need to be alive at the end of the first minute," Iroh said. "Which you will be, if your breath is steady. Very well." He picked something up off the deck, and his whole face changed. "But first I must finish my roast duck."

Zuko stared at him. "The duck."

"It is a very good duck," Iroh said, and sat down with it. "And the Avatar has waited a hundred years, so he can wait while I eat it."

Somebody in the off-watch laughed, and cut it off.

Zuko turned and walked. He went the length of the deck with his shoulders up and his hands still closed, and the men opened for him, and he did not look at anyone, and he went down the ladder well and was gone.

Jake put his glove back on. The water was already draining off the deck, back into the channel it had come from, and the steam was thinning in the wind, and the two men at the mast were looking at the burn mark on the planks and at each other.

"He's not angry at the man," Iroh said, around his duck.

"I know," Jake said.

Iroh chewed. "You do."

"He's angry that it happened in front of you."

"That is not what I said." Iroh looked up at him, and the mildness in his face had gone somewhere. "Sit down, Jake."

Jake sat, because the old man had asked, and because his hands were cold and the chair was out of the wind. Around them the deck went back to work. The fender, the lines, the sea. The ship was still going south at the best speed her boilers would give her, and she had been going south all night, and the note of the engines had changed.

"He will be a great firebender one day," Iroh said. "He has the rage for it. But the rage is not the fire, and if he does not learn that, the fire will use him the way a bad rider uses a good horse." He offered Jake the duck, which was Iroh's way of asking whether Jake was all right. Jake took a piece. "You were quick with the man."

"It was only his coat."

"It was his coat," Iroh agreed, "and now he has a burned arm and a fender to mend, and he will not spend the week deciding whether his prince tried to kill him, because he will spend it being the man who mended the fender."

Jake chewed. In three years he had learned that Iroh almost never said the thing he actually meant, and that if you waited long enough he would say it in a story about a duck.

"The men are frightened of him," Jake said.

"The men are frightened of what he will do," Iroh said. "There is a difference, and it is the difference between a prince and a weather system. They have not been frightened of you in two years."

Jake looked at his gloves.

"In the first year they would not stand near the rail when you took the ice off it," Iroh said. "Now a boy of fifteen will look at your hands because he thinks looking is braver than not looking. That is a thing that happened slowly, on this ship, over a great many mornings. It is also the reason I do not want you on the deck when we reach that village."

"I'll be on the deck."

"I know," Iroh said. "I am telling you the shape of the thing, not asking." He gave the duck another minute. "The prince will want you at his left shoulder tomorrow. Stay there. Whatever the men say about lights and foreigners, stay at his shoulder and keep your hands where they are. There is going to be a great deal of steam and shouting and very little weather in which a man might notice a thing he was not looking for."

"I know how to be careful."

"You know how to be useful," Iroh said. "They are not always the same skill."

---

The prince's quarters were the only room on the ship that was too hot.

It was pumped through with heat from the engines, and the red banners on the walls held it, and the small brazier in the corner made it worse. Jake had stopped noticing it years ago, except when he came in from a night watch and the heat closed over his face like a wet cloth.

He knelt at the foot of the bed and laid the armor out in its order. Breastplate. Shoulders. Gauntlets. Straps. He checked each strap for fraying before he checked it for fit. It was a task with a beginning and an end in it, and he was grateful for that in a way he did not examine.

Zuko paced the narrow floor between bed and wall, breathing in deliberate counts that kept breaking halfway.

"You think it's him," Jake said.

Zuko stopped. "What?"

"It's what you've been asking the rail since the light." Jake ran the strap through his fingers. "You've not said it to me. So say it."

Zuko looked at him for a moment, and Jake could see him decide, twice, to say something proud instead. Then he sat down on the edge of the bed, and the whole shape of him changed, and he was a sixteen-year-old boy in a hot little room a long way from home.

"I think it's him," he said. "I think it has to be. What else puts a light like that in the sky?"

"A volcano," Jake said.

"Don't."

"A comet."

"Jake."

"A very large lamp."

Zuko's mouth twitched and he looked away, and the twitch was the best thing Jake had seen all day. "I have two hundred men and one ship and a hundred years of nothing, and the one night something answers, my uncle tells me it is a whale." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "If it isn't him —"

"It's him," Jake said.

Zuko looked up.

"I don't know it. I think it." Jake set the breastplate down. "Something that big doesn't happen by accident. And if it isn't him, then it's something, and something is better than nothing, and we'll go and look at it and come away with an answer either way. An answer is worth nine days of coal."

Zuko was quiet for a while.

"That's what he says," he said. "My uncle."

"Your uncle says it better. He's had longer to practise."

A knock, and Iroh let himself in already halfway into a yawn, his hair loose, his day robes traded for something softer. He looked, for the first time all day, like a man intending to rest.

"I am going to bed now." He paused, and it became clear that the pause was the point. "A man needs his rest."

Zuko did not answer. Jake kept his eyes on the gauntlets.

"Prince Zuko," Iroh said, more gently. "You need sleep. Even if you are right, and the Avatar is alive, you will not find him tonight. Your father tried. His father before him. For a hundred years, patience has defeated every one of us."

"Because their honor did not hinge on his capture." Zuko stood up. "Mine does."

Iroh looked at him for a moment longer. Jake, on the floor with a gauntlet in his hands, saw the thing pass behind the old man's eyes. Not disappointment. Not quite worry. The tired steadiness of a man who had made this argument before and lost it every time.

"Good night, Prince Zuko," Iroh said, and went out.

The door swung to.

Jake rose and crossed to the waiting breastplate.

"Arms up."

Zuko obeyed, as he always did for this. He raised his arms while Jake settled the plate and worked the side straps home. Three years of the ritual sat in their two bodies — the hands finding each buckle without looking, the prince standing rigid and being handled in a way he would have broken any other man for attempting.

Jake tightened the shoulder strap and felt, under his gloves, the old scars pulling with the pull of the work.

"You'll take the gauntlets as well," he said. "The cold turns the steel into a blade's edge before you feel the cut."

Zuko held out his hands without comment. Jake fitted them in place, one and then the other, and did not look further ahead than his own hands.

"Jake."

"Mm."

"If we take him," Zuko said, and then stopped, and the next part came out with an effort. "If we take him, I go home."

Jake worked the last clasp. "I know."

"Not back to the ship. Home. I would be —" He stopped again. His hands, in the gauntlets, closed. "It would be finished."

"I know," Jake said, and stood back.

The armor was on and every strap was true. Zuko stood in the middle of the hot little room with his scarred face and his borrowed sword and his father's colors. He looked exactly like what he was: a boy dressed up as the thing that had hurt him.

"Get some sleep," Jake said. "There's nothing to see in the dark, and there'll be a great deal to see at first light."

"And you?"

"I'll be back before the watch changes."

Jake went out through the alcove. It was a space not much wider than a corridor, with a mat on the floor and a sea chest at the foot of it. The chest held a spare shirt, a needle, a stone for the knife, and a small braided cord of leather. Haru had put the cord into his hand the week before everything ended, as a fairing for nothing at all. He did not take it out. He had stopped taking it out in the first year, when he had still been in the habit of looking at it. It was enough that it was in there.

He sat on the mat with his back to the chest and pulled the gloves off, one and then the other, and looked at his hands as a man looks at a tool at the end of a long day. The scars were pale in the lamplight and the cold had got into them, and the old seam down the left palm had gone tight and shiny. He worked his thumb across it and then stopped himself, because that was a road with a bad end, and put the gloves back on.

Above him the ship's note had gone from a grind to a rumble. The ice was thinner here. They would be at the village before dark tomorrow.

He had never seen a Southern Water Tribe village. He had been carried out of one so young that he had no street in him, no names, no faces, nothing he could honestly call a memory. Everything he had of the place was in the ice outside the hull and in the pull he felt when he looked at it, and one was not a memory either. One was just weather in his chest.

Tomorrow he was going to put a prince in armor and stand at his shoulder while that prince went into a village of people who looked, some of them, like him.

He lay down on the mat with his gloves on and his hands folded on his chest. After a while he heard Zuko's breathing change on the other side of the door, and then stop, and then start again, startled out of sleep. Then footsteps. Then the creak of the bed. Then nothing.

It took Jake a long time to sleep. When he did, he dreamed about a basin of water and did not know, in the dream, why he was afraid of it.

---

The flare went up in the afternoon.

Jake was on the foredeck with the boarding gear when the light came off the ice, red and low and blooming, and the sound of it reached them a half heartbeat later. Fire Navy signal. Everybody on the deck knew the color of that light before they knew what they were looking at.

Zuko had the spyglass up before the last of it had fallen.

Jake watched the whole of him gather into that narrow circle of glass. Shoulders forward, breath going shallow and even, the particular stillness that meant the rest of the world had been put down somewhere. Beside that, his own chest had tightened without permission. He had never seen what Zuko was seeing, and could barely guess at its shape. Only the borrowed second-hand of the prince's certainty, a private view shared out of habit.

"The last airbender," Zuko said, low.

Through the glass: two small figures, at distance, moving fast over open ice near a darker shape Jake took some seconds to read as a hull half-buried in the pack. Not a wreck anyone had come to claim. An old ship left to the floes, like the Fire Navy left everything down here that it had finished with.

"Quite agile," Zuko said, "for his old age."

The figures changed direction. They had been walking; now they ran, cutting across the ice toward a fixed point, and Zuko tracked their line and steadied the glass and went suddenly, coldly still.

"Wake my uncle," he told the sentry, without lowering the glass. "Tell him I have found the Avatar." A pause, and the glass moved. "As well as his hiding place."

Jake followed the angle of the spyglass out into the grey with nothing to see it with. And found it anyway: a low cluster of shapes on the horizon, softened by distance into something that could have been ice if he had not known to look. Small. Huddled against the wind. Anything built here had to be built that way. Lights in the windows, or ice catching the light wrong; at this range the eye took what it was given.

Once, just once, he let himself look at it properly.

Three years of grey water, and he had never seen a southern settlement. Not Wolf Cove, not any cove. And still the thing that came up in him was older than memory and lower than fear, a pull in his chest toward the small huddled lights on the rim of the world. He understood, standing there, that nothing out of his past had ever once felt like this looking at him. The feeling was entirely one-sided. The village was not his and never had been.

He had eighty years of somebody else's war between himself and any of it.

And by morning, by his own hands as much as the prince's, he was going to help tear it apart.

"Jake."

He made his face. It took a moment's work, the same work it had always taken, and then it was done. "Sir."

"Boarding parties. Two boats, six men each, and the men who can run on ice. Not the ones who can't." Zuko turned from the glass at last, and his one good eye was bright and terrible. "I want the gates covered, the walls covered, and every man who can hold a spear facing outward. We are not here for prisoners. We are here for the boy."

"Yes, sir."

"Tell Jee to have the boats in the water an hour before first light. Tell him I want the men fed before, not after. Tell him the engines stay hot." Zuko looked back at the village, and his voice was almost gentle. "We have three years behind us, and it comes down to one morning. I won't have it lost to a cold man with an empty stomach."

"No, sir."

Jake went below.

The crew were already moving when he got to the mess. Somebody had heard the flare. Somebody had heard the order. The men were checking blades and boots and the straps on the boarding hooks. Kaito, nineteen and still convinced that a rope wanted to be talked to, was telling two new hands that they were on the second boat, and that the second boat was the lucky one. The two new hands believed him because they were new.

"Second boat," Jake said. "Both of you. You'll want your hoods up and your hands wrapped. It's a sight colder on the ice than it is on the deck, and a man who can't hold his hook is no use to anybody."

"Sir," they said, and went to wrap their hands.

Jake found Jee at the boat davits, and gave him the orders, and watched the lieutenant's face do the arithmetic on an hour before first light and the engines staying hot and the men fed first.

"He wants it done properly," Jee said.

"He wants it done."

"That's not the same thing." Jee looked at him. "And you? Where are you standing?"

"At his left shoulder."

Jee grunted. "Of course you are."

The davits creaked as the first boat went out over the black water and down. The ice was close now, and the village was a smear of lights on it, and somewhere out there a boy who had not existed for a hundred years was running toward those lights with two Water Tribe children and no idea what was coming.

Jake went to the galley and made sure the bread was out of the oven and the tea was hot, because a cold man with an empty stomach loses mornings. Then he went up to the foredeck and stood at the prince's left shoulder, and the two of them looked south at the lights and said nothing at all.

*Wolf Cove, the same night.*

Katara had never seen the village this quiet with everyone awake in it.

The flare had burned out over the ice an hour ago, and the women were still standing in the open with their arms wrapped around themselves, looking at the place where it had been. The men had gone south two winters ago with the last of the boats. What was left of Wolf Cove was thirty people, a wall of packed snow, and a row of drying racks nobody had thought to bring in.

"Say it again," Sokka said. "Slowly. From the part where you went into a wrecked ship that was full of firebending traps."

"We didn't know it was full of traps," Katara said.

"You didn't know it was full of traps."

"I didn't know it was full of traps," Aang said, cheerfully, from where he was sitting on the wall with his legs swinging. "But it was a really old ship. You could tell by the smell."

Sokka put both hands over his face.

The wind came off the water and went through the village, and the nearest cookfire guttered and bent. Katara watched it and felt, for the first time all evening, how tired she was. Her arms ached from the sledding and the running and, before that, from something else. From the moment on the ice when the water had come when she called it, and the iceberg had split, and a boy had stepped out of the light.

She had not told Sokka about that part.

She had told him about the ship, and the traps, and the flare. She had not told him that when she had stood over the frozen block and screamed at her brother, the sea had answered her. It had come up out of the crack in the ice in a long grey arm and gone where she had thrown it, and it had not been Sokka's throwing or the wind or luck. It had been her.

"A flare like that carries for miles," Sokka said. "Miles, Katara. Any ship on this coast would have seen it."

"Then maybe we should have thought of that before we went aboard a Fire Navy ship."

"We. We." Sokka pointed at her. "You said *we*."

"I said we because I was there."

Gran-Gran came across the packed snow with her stick and her chin up, and the circle of women opened for her without being asked. She looked at the wall, and the racks, and the three of them, and she did not look pleased.

"Inside," she said. "All of you. The wall will be there in the morning, and so will whatever saw that light."

"Sokka says a ship could have seen it," Aang said helpfully.

Gran-Gran looked at Sokka. Sokka, to his credit, did not look away.

"Then we will be ready for a ship," she said. "Katara. Bring the boy something to eat that is not snow."

They went in. The tent was warm and smelled of seal oil and old wool. Aang ate four bowls of stew and told Gran-Gran about the air temple he could not remember and the monks he had left behind. She listened to all of it with her hands folded, the way she listened to weather reports.

Katara sat by the flap with her knees drawn up and watched the dark outside.

She was not afraid of the flare. She knew she should be. She had grown up on stories of what the Fire Nation did to villages too small to matter. She knew what it meant that a light had gone up over the ice, and that somewhere out there a ship might have seen it. But she could not hold on to the fear, because something else kept pushing it aside.

There was another bender in the world.

All her life she had been the only one. The only waterbender in the village, the only one Gran-Gran's stories had ever fit. She had taught herself in secret, badly, in the shallows off the point where no one could see her fail at it. She had carried it alone for years, and she had begun to think that the loneliness was part of the bending, that it came with the water, that this was simply what it was to be the last of a thing.

And then a boy had fallen out of the sky with a blue arrow on his head and told her he was the Avatar, and the world had turned over.

She looked at him now. He was demonstrating something with a spoon and a bowl of stew, and Sokka was trying not to laugh, and Gran-Gran's mouth had done the thing it did when it was pretending not to be a smile.

Outside the wind went along the wall, and the sled dogs answered it.

Katara lay down at last with her mother's necklace against her throat and her hands curled under her chin. In the morning the wall would be there, and the sea would be there, and whatever had seen the light would be closer. She would be afraid then.

Tonight she lay in the dark and thought: *there is another one of me.*

And she slept better than she had in years.
