# Chapter 9: What the River Keeps

The practice staff cracked overhead, hard enough to sound like a snapped mast. In the alcove below, the salve smelled of crushed mint and something older underneath, a bitter root Iroh bought by the jar and never named. Jake kept his eyes on the lamp, his hands open on the edge of the crate, and listened to the sound that had been going on above him since before first light. One set of feet driving hard, another falling back, a flat crack, then another. Neither of them said so. They had both been listening to that sound for two days.

"Open," Iroh said.

Jake spread his fingers over the old man's knee. The linen came away from the left palm in a slow peel, and the cloth stuck at the base of the thumb, where the split ran longest. The alcove was the ship's one corner without a vent, barely wide enough for a cot and a crate, and the lamp swung with every shudder of the hull.

The wound underneath had stopped bleeding sometime in the night. It was not better. Iroh turned the hand to the light and said nothing for a while.

"Close it for me," he said at last.

Jake curled his fingers. They went about halfway. The tendons pulled, and a thin, high heat ran up into his wrist. He held it there and kept his face still.

"Mm," said Iroh. "And the right?"

"The right is better."

"That is not what I asked."

"It closes more," Jake said.

Iroh smoothed salve into the split with his thumb, so lightly that the touch registered mostly as cold. The ruined gloves lay on the crate beside the lamp where he had left them to dry, stiff and dark to the knuckles, the left one gaping along its burst seam.

"You will not be hauling anything today," Iroh said.

"There's the pump line. The crew—"

"The crew has hands." Iroh looked up at him, mild and unmoved. "Yours are on loan to me until sundown."

Jake let that stand. He had learned long ago which of Iroh's sentences were suggestions.

Overhead the fighting got heavier. A sharp, flat crack, then another, and Jake's hands went still in Iroh's.

Iroh's thumb paused on the linen. Then he finished the wrap, tied it off with a neat flat knot, and moved to the right hand. A second crack came, then a grunt, then the scrape of someone going down hard on one knee.

"Perhaps," Iroh said, tying the second knot, "we should see how the morning is going."

The deck was gray and wet and smelled of soot. Two days of scrubbing had not lifted the black from the aft rails, and the stern still listed, a slow lean to port. A thin brown smoke leaked from the stack, thinner than it had been, but wrong in color. The crew kept to the middle of the ship with their heads down, coiling line that did not need coiling, giving the center of the deck a wide berth.

Zuko was in the middle of it.

He had stripped to his undershirt despite the cold. Strips of linen circled both his wrists, Iroh's work, the knots small and tight. He was driving Lieutenant Jee across the planks with a practice staff, and there was no form in it. Jee was a competent fighter, but he was backing up now, staff held in front of his chest like a door, and every time he found his footing Zuko took it from him. Zuko did not pause or reset. He just came again, breath ragged, hair stuck to his forehead, the scar gone dark with sweat on the ruined side of his face.

He was not training. Jake had seen Zuko train for three years, and training had a shape. It stopped. It corrected. Zuko would say *again* in his flat angry voice and mean *better*. This had no *again* in it. This was a man running from something on foot, on a deck forty paces long.

*If he stops, he has to think about the sky over the island,* Jake thought. *The ground-fire. The line firing with no signal.* He put it away where he put most things, and looked at the practical problem: Jee's left foot was dragging, and the older man was breathing through his mouth.

Zuko drove in again. The staff came across low, and Jee blocked it, but the force of it rang up his arms and the staff slid sideways out of his grip. It skittered over the planks. Jee went to one knee, a hand flat on the deck.

"Get up," Zuko said.

Jee got a hand to his ribs and breathed. The crew had stopped pretending to coil rope.

Jake crossed the deck, close enough to be seen, and went down on one knee beside the lieutenant. He kept his wrapped hands low and open, and took Jee's elbow with his forearm instead of his fingers, because his fingers would not have held.

"Rib?" he said.

"Bruised." Jee's voice was dry. He kept his eyes on the planks. "I've had worse from a sandbag."

"Can you stand?"

"I can stand."

"Then stand slowly," Jake said, and Jee almost smiled.

Above them Zuko went still, and the stillness carried down through the planks. Jake got Jee's weight onto his good side and rose with him. When he finally looked at Zuko, he made himself look at the staff and not the eyes.

"He's winded," Jake said. "Give him a minute."

"He should not be winded."

"He's been on his feet since the fires." Jake kept his tone flat. "So have you."

Zuko's mouth went thin. He looked like he wanted to say something, and Jake watched the words reach the back of his teeth and stop there. Then the deck lurched.

It was not the engine. Jake knew every note of the engine by now. This was something else, the whole hull rolling against its own list as the bow swung. A coil of rope slid across the planks and fetched up against the rail. Overhead the rigging sang one long note as the wind shifted.

They were changing course.

Zuko's head came around fast. He looked at the bow, at the sun low and pale on the starboard quarter where it had no business being, and the practice staff cracked down against the deck.

"Who gave that order?" His voice carried to the stern. "Who touched the helm?"

Nobody answered. A deckhand near the rail found something fascinating in a knot.

"We were heading north. I set the heading myself." Zuko wheeled on the bridge, and the helmsman, Jake saw, had gone very still behind his wheel and was not looking down. "Who is changing our course? That is mutiny. I want the name of every man on that bridge—"

"Actually," Iroh said, "it was me."

He had come up the stair behind Jake at his own pace. He stood at the edge of the cleared space with his hands folded over his stomach, wearing an expression of such gentle, regretful dignity that Jake felt the crew's collective breath go out.

Zuko stared at him. "You."

"I assure you, Prince Zuko, it is a matter of the utmost importance."

"What matter?" Zuko said. "We have a cracked engine housing. We have no coal. Zhao's whole fleet is looking for us, and there is nothing in the world more important than—"

"I have lost my lotus tile."

The silence went on long enough for a gull to cross it. Jake looked at the deck. He knew he should not, and he did it anyway, and found Jee studying the same piece of plank with great care.

"Your," Zuko said. "Your *tile.*"

"The white lotus. From my Pai Sho set. It is a rare piece, and a set without it is very nearly no set at all." Iroh sighed. "I have turned my quarters upside down. It is gone. The nearest Earth Kingdom port is half a day's sail from here, and the markets there are reputed to be good."

"You changed our course." Zuko's voice had dropped, which was worse. "For a *game piece.*"

"Ten minutes," Iroh said. "I ask only ten minutes in the port. Then we will resume your search with my full and undivided attention."

Jake watched Zuko's shoulders come up. They rose slowly. He tipped his head back, and the prince stood a moment with his face to the underside of the bridge, jaw working, and Jake had time to think *oh.*

Zuko opened his mouth and breathed out.

Not a bolt — a roar of fire and black smoke that struck the planks of the overhead and flattened out against them. Heat washed over the deck, and a deckhand ducked. The smell of scorched pitch came down thick and bitter. The cloud rolled under the bridge and thinned out over the rail, and when it was gone Zuko stood with his chest heaving, scorch marks climbing the boards above him in a black fan.

Iroh patted his belly.

"I am so lucky," he said warmly, "to have such an understanding nephew."

Zuko looked at him. Then he walked away, down the length of the ship toward the bow, with his staff still in his hand and his boots loud on the planks, and nobody moved to stop him.

Jake let out a breath. His hands kept time with his pulse, and the heat of the fire lingered on his face. He looked at Iroh.

Iroh was not looking at him. He was watching the prince's back go forward along the rail, and his face, now that Zuko could not see it, was the face of a man who had lost a game piece and did not mind it — tired, and very patient. Jake understood then that there was no tile. There were ten minutes on land, and an old man who thought Zuko would die of the running if somebody did not make him stop.

It was dangerous. Any port was dangerous, with Zhao hunting them and a bounty that might be posted by now. He thought it as he went to the rail to look at the coast coming up gray and green through the haze.

The tile was a lie. Jake did not intend to say so.

The ship limped toward the harbor on its stuttering engines, and by midday the first masts were showing above the water. Jake went back down to the alcove for the gloves. They were still stiff, and it took him a long time to work his hands into them.

---
The port was called something Jake never caught. The name was painted on a board at the head of the quay in Earth Kingdom characters.

The smell reached them before the gangplank did. Fish and tar, crushed ginger, the sweet rot of fruit left too long in the sun. Overhead the gulls screamed at the fishwives, and under that rose the market itself, a dozen sellers at once, each pitching a voice over the others.

He kept his hands in his coat pockets. The gloves were back on over fresh linen, and the left one gaped at the seam where Iroh had pinned it shut with a bone needle and a strip of leather. In his pockets the wrapped fingers lay curled and useless against the lining. If anyone bumped him, he would have to decide whether to flinch. He had decided in advance that he would not.

The coat was buttoned to the throat. Under it he wore a plain brown tunic borrowed from the stores, and nobody had asked him why his own red crew jacket was rolled at the bottom of a sea chest. A good port was a port where nobody looked twice at a boy of his height. He walked a half-step behind Zuko's left shoulder, where he could see most of the street and most of the prince's face.

Two men in dull gray stood by a fruit stall with spears on their shoulders. Earth Kingdom, local garrison, bored. Jake marked them and let his eyes pass on. A third man leaned against a post eating roasted nuts, with a sword on his hip and the wrong kind of stillness, and Jake held on him longer.

Behind Jake came three of the ship's soldiers, plain cloaks pulled over their armor. They stayed ten paces back and walked like men who had been told not to look like soldiers, which was to say they walked like soldiers.

"Hmm," said Iroh.

He had stopped at a stall hung with brass and bamboo and string. Iroh leaned over a tray of carved game pieces, picked up one after another, held each to the light, and set them down again with great courtesy.

"Not that one," he said. "Not that. A nice piece, though. No."

"Uncle, we are not here to look at pieces."

"We are here to find one particular piece, nephew."

"We are here for ten minutes."

"And I am using them wisely."

The woman selling the pieces showed Iroh a white blossom carved from bone. He turned it over twice and shook his head. It was a lotus, but a poor one, with the petals too thick and flattened. Zuko stood with his arms folded and watched the minutes go by, and Jake watched him do it.

The prince had put a hood up. It shadowed the scar without hiding it. His wrists were still bound in Iroh's linen, and when he held his arms folded the knots showed at his cuffs. Jake thought *keep them covered*, and did not say it, because saying it would only have turned Zuko's attention on the hands beneath them. Not today.

By the third stall Iroh had gathered a tsungi horn, which he tucked under one arm like a prize. By the fifth he had added a small stone monkey with its paws clasped over its ears. By the seventh he had stopped asking about the tile at all and was haggling over tin bells.

"Not a lotus tile in the entire marketplace," Iroh said at last, looking pleased.

Zuko's eyes closed. "Good."

"The monkey, though."

"It's good to know," Zuko said, "that this trip was a complete waste of time for everyone."

"Not everyone." Iroh held up the monkey. "Look at his expression."

Jake looked at the carved monkey, at its stone paws and its small, smug face. Zuko did not look at the monkey. He looked at Iroh, and then at the street, and Jake saw the exact moment the prince decided to stop arguing, because his shoulders lowered a fraction and went hard.

They had come out at the water end of the market, where the stalls gave way to barrels and the smell of fish thickened. Rough tables had been dragged up against a warehouse wall under a patched canvas awning. Three or four men sat around one with tankards. They were not Earth Kingdom garrison and not fishermen, though they had the red hands and sea-bleached eyes of both. One was thin and loud, a long-faced man in a striped vest. Across from him sat a man twice his width in a captain's coat gone green at the seams.

Jake looked at their belts first. Short swords, a boarding axe, a coil of chain on the bench by the big man's boot. A pirate crew, or near enough to one. Six that he could see, and the awning might be hiding more. He counted the distance between the table and Zuko's back. Twelve paces. Then he closed his fingers inside his pockets and saw how much grip was left.

Not much.

"I'm telling you, Captain," the thin man said. "It was right there. Both of 'em. I had my hand on the girl's collar."

"You had your hand on nothing," said the big man.

"We lost the Water Tribe girl, and the little bald monk she was traveling with."

Jake felt it before he understood it. It was a cold, small, exact spike under the breastbone. *Water Tribe.* He had heard those words in a dozen voices over three years. Always in the past tense. Always about somebody else. A girl. The torn ribbon lay in Zuko's pocket, the pale stone against the dark blue, and Jake did not look at the prince's pocket. He looked at the pirates' hands.

*Cutthroats,* he told himself. *Six of them, armed, and the prince is about to talk to them.*

Zuko had already turned.

He did not walk to the table so much as arrive at it. One moment he stood beside his uncle, hooded and silent, and the next he was three paces from the pirates with his shoulders squared and his weight forward. In the same movement he had stepped a half-pace to the right, so that his body was between the table and the place where Jake stood. He did not think the prince knew he had done it.

"This monk," Zuko said.

The thin man blinked. Half the table turned. The big man in the captain's coat ran his eyes over Zuko from the hood to the boots.

"Who's asking?"

"The monk," Zuko said again. "Did he have an arrow on his head?"

For a moment nobody answered. Jake's gaze moved across the table: the thin man's right hand drifting toward his belt, the captain's left sliding down by the chain. He took a half-step forward to stand level with Zuko's shoulder, not behind it, and kept his hands where the pirates could see them. They were in his pockets. It would have to do.

"A blue one," the thin man said slowly. "On the forehead. And the hands."

"Where?"

"Now hang on—"

"*Where.*"

The big man rose. He was larger standing than he had been sitting, and he carried a dozen years of salt in the lines of his face. He looked at Zuko's bound wrists, at the soldiers lingering at the end of the stall, at Jake. Jake held his gaze. The captain's mouth moved in a way that might have been a smile.

"Might be," the pirate said, "we could use a hand finding him."

"You want something," Zuko said.

"Everybody wants something, boy."

Zuko's jaw shifted. Jake saw him weigh it. The hunger in the set of his face was too sharp, and the thing the pirates were offering was exactly the shape of the hole the Avatar had left in the sky.

"Fine," Zuko said. "We work together."

"Then we start now. They went up the river. Girl was practicing her water tricks in the woods, the last we saw."

Jake kept his face still.

*Water tricks.*

Zuko turned his head. For a moment he looked straight at Jake, and the hood fell back from his eyes, and in them there was nothing Jake could read except that the prince was not going to ask. He simply expected that Jake would be there, and Jake was, a half-step behind and to the left, where he had always stood.

"You," Zuko said. "Bring three men. We're going into the woods."

"Yes," Jake said.

He took his hands out of his pockets, and the linen under the gloves pulled tight, and the pain came up through his wrists in a long clean line. He let it. He went after the prince and the pirates and the sound of the river, and he did not look back at the ship.

---
The woods began where the warehouses ended, and the path ran uphill alongside the river for a long time before anyone spoke.

The big pirate in the captain's coat led. The thin one trotted at his elbow, and a third came behind with a coil of rope over his shoulder. Zuko walked in the middle of them like a man who had not decided whether he was among allies. Jake took the rear of Zuko's flank, with the three soldiers fanned out behind him in the brush, his hands loose at his sides.

The captain's price had come out in pieces. The girl had a scroll. A real one, the captain said, waterbending, old and worth a fortune. The pirates had held it for a night before she took it back. They wanted it, and Zuko had said they could have it. Jake had watched him say it, with no change in his face at all.

*He doesn't want the scroll,* Jake thought. *He's never even looked at it.* That was the whole of the prince's bargain: let them have their parchment, and he would take the boy.

The trees thickened. The river noise swelled and then broke into pieces. Splashing. Then a pause, then a thin sound like a sheet of cloth snapped out and let go.

The captain raised a fist. They all stopped.

"There," he whispered.

Jake went down on one knee behind a root-tangled bank. Between two stalks of fern the ground fell in a small hollow where the river had spilled its bank and left a pool. A girl stood at its edge.

She was about his own age, perhaps a little younger. She wore a heavy parka of blue, dyed deep, with white fur at the collar and cuffs and a pale pattern stitched down the front in thick knotwork. Her hair was dark and fell past her shoulders in a long braid. Her skin was a warm brown, and her boots were fur-lined too, absurdly heavy for this weather. She had pushed her sleeves back to the elbow.

She was bending.

Jake had seen waterbending on Kyoshi Island and in the cold dark moment when a girl in blue had stopped a dozen soldiers in place with a gesture. He had never seen it done simply, for its own sake, by someone who thought she was alone. The girl raised her arms, and water climbed out of the pool in a long clear rope.

She turned her hands over, and the rope turned with her. It was careful and not yet certain. When it faltered she scowled and began again. The water swung out and around and back, and the movements came from her hips and her shoulders, wide and rolling, her whole body leaning into the pull and then giving it back.

It looked like the tide. It looked like something he knew how to do, performed by a stranger in a different tongue.

*Southern.* He had her clothes, the stitching, the cut of the fur. She had come from the same ice he had come from. The thought did not warm him, and he waited for it to. He looked at her brown face and her bent shoulders and thought, with a flat, hollow wonder, *this is my blood*, and waited for the part that was supposed to follow.

Nothing came. He looked at the pattern down the front of her parka and had no idea what it meant. Was it a family mark, or a tribe's? She wore it like skin. He knelt in the ferns in a coat that smelled of ship's coal, with Fire Nation drill in every muscle of his back, and understood that he was the one in costume.

Beside him, Zuko lifted two fingers. The signal was small, and everyone saw it.

The pirates moved first. The thin one broke from the ferns with a shout, and the girl spun toward it, and the water in the air lashed out in a blue crack that caught him across the chest and flung him backward into a bush. The captain laughed, and two more were on her before she could turn again.

Zuko came down the bank in three strides, and a soldier cut around the other side of the pool. Jake slid out from the roots and took the high ground at the lip of the hollow. The girl got a second whip of water out, and then someone caught her wrists from behind, and the water dropped from the air in a heavy splash.

It took four of them to move her. She fought the whole way, kicking, twisting, shouting in a high, furious voice that had no fear in it at all. Zuko took a rope from a soldier and went around the nearest tree with her back against it. He wound the line three times about her arms and waist. When he drew the knot tight she hissed at him, and he did not look away.

Jake stayed where he was and watched the tree line. That was his work. There was nobody in the woods but them, and he made sure of it anyway.

Zuko stepped back. The girl sagged against the rope, panting. Her braid had come half loose. She lifted her chin and glared at him, and then past him, and her eyes landed on Jake for the barest instant and slid on. She had seen a Fire Nation soldier. A boy in a coat. Nothing more.

"Where is he?" Zuko said.

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"The Avatar." His voice was quiet. "Tell me where he is, and I won't hurt you or your brother."

*Brother.* Jake had not known there was one.

"Never," the girl said. She spat the word. "You can tie me up all day. You can—" Her voice caught, and went on harder. "You're a coward and you're going to lose. He'll beat you, and I'll be there to watch."

Zuko did not move. He looked at her the way he looked at a map, then reached into his coat.

"Perhaps," he said, "in exchange, I can restore something you've lost."

He drew out the necklace. The blue ribbon hung from his fingers with the pale stone swinging at the end. Jake had last seen it in a village by the ice, in the first week, on the neck of this girl or someone just like her. It looked small. It looked very much like what it was.

The girl went still.

Her eyes were on the stone. Her mouth opened. When they came they were thin.

"Where did you get that?"

"I didn't steal it." Zuko let the ribbon turn in the light. "I found it. I can give it back." He held it a hand's breadth from her face. "Tell me where he is."

Jake looked at the ground.

He did not decide to. His eyes simply went down to the damp earth, and to the toes of his boots, where a crushed fern lay in the mud. He had no right to the grief that stood in the hollow, and he knew exactly how it was being used. The prince was doing this the way he did everything, head-on and with all of himself, as if the world had left him no other road. Jake had watched him do worse.

He lifted his head, and kept his face empty.

"I'm not telling you anything," the girl said, and her voice shook, and she did not take her eyes off the stone.

"Then you've made your choice."

"The scroll," said a voice behind them.

The pirate captain had come up with his thumbs hooked in his belt. The captain nodded at a flat rock near the pool, where a long case of old yellow parchment lay half unrolled. Painted figures ran down it in a faded brush hand. Jake saw a woman with her arms raised and a ribbon of water running between her palms.

"We had a deal, boy," the captain said. "The scroll. That was the price."

"You'll have it," Zuko said.

"Now."

Zuko pocketed the necklace and crossed to the rock. He took the scroll up in his left hand, closed the case. With his right hand he opened his palm. A flame sprang up from it, small and steady and orange, and he held the parchment over it so that the yellow edge nearly touched the fire.

The girl cried out, "No—"

The crackle was loud in the quiet clearing. A curl of brown ran along the rim of the case, a hair's width from catching.

"Here is how it will go," Zuko said, looking at the pirate captain and not at the girl. "You want this. I want the boy. Go into those woods and find him, and bring him back to me. When I have him, you will have the scroll." His flame did not waver.

The captain studied the flame, and the scroll, and Zuko's face. Then he grinned, with most of his teeth, and turned.

"You heard the prince, lads."

They went crashing out of the clearing in a body. The girl strained against her ropes, jaw set, her eyes burning on the scroll in Zuko's hand. She did not look at Jake, and she did not look at the necklace, and when the sound of the pirates had faded she said nothing at all.

Jake counted heartbeats and did not like the number he reached. After a while he found that his own fingers had curled against his palms inside the gloves, and that the pain had come up again, steady and clean.

"They're coming back," one of the soldiers murmured.

Branches cracked. Voices came up through the trees. Zuko closed his hand, and the flame went out. Jake turned his head toward the sound and saw the first pirates break the tree line, dragging something between them.

---
The shore was a gray crescent of mud and shingle where the river widened above the falls, and the pirates had made themselves at home on it.

Their ship lay against a rotten jetty at the water's edge, a low, ugly vessel with patched sails furled and a rail the color of old teeth. Zuko's launch had been dragged up the bank beside it. Jake had studied the bank on the climb and judged it by habit: two exits, one slope, a single stand of reeds. The water ran a body-length from his boots.

He had not been asked to walk the girl down. A soldier had cut her loose from the tree, and another had taken her arm, and the two of them marched her out of the trees at the center of the party with her wrists bound in front of her. Her eyes found the scroll in Zuko's hand and stayed on it.

Iroh was waiting at the top of the slope. Jake had not seen him follow them. The old man stood with the tsungi horn tucked under one arm, and when Zuko passed him he only inclined his head.

The pirates brought the captives out of the reeds a moment later.

There were two. The first was a boy of about Jake's own age, lean and long-limbed, in blue Water Tribe furs cut very much like the girl's. His head was shaved at the sides, the rest drawn back in a short tail. Another pirate carried a bent boomerang of dark wood at his belt like a trophy. Jake knew the boy. He had seen him on the back of the bison at Kyoshi, a white-furred shape growing smaller against the sunset, and the memory arrived whole and uninvited.

The second captive was a child.

He came last, bound at the wrists with a rope that was too thick for him. Bare feet, loose orange and yellow cloth, a bruise darkening on one cheek. His head was shaved clean, and on it, running to a point between his eyebrows and in two lines along his hands, was the blue arrow. Jake looked at the small, solemn face and thought *him* at once. The boy who had stood in a doorway and been, for an instant, the whole sky.

"The Avatar," said the captain, with some satisfaction. "Fresh delivered."

Zuko did not so much as glance at the boy with the shaved sides. He stared at the smaller one. The hunt had been two years of closed doors, and now the child stood ten paces off with a rope around his wrists.

"Give me the boy," Zuko said.

"You give us the scroll."

"Release him, and it's yours."

"Hold on," said the boy with the shaved sides.

Everyone looked at him. His voice was dry and a little hoarse, and it was pitched precisely in the register of someone who thought he was being very reasonable.

"Let me get this straight. You two are about to trade the Avatar." He tilted his chin at the small captive. "The *Avatar*. For a piece of parchment."

The captain's mouth opened.

"Because I'm no expert," the boy went on, "but I'd say he'd fetch a lot more on the black market. Or from the Fire Lord himself."

There was a pause that Jake would have liked to measure. The captain's eyes slid from the scroll in Zuko's fist to the child in orange. Behind the captain, the thin pirate was grinning like a man who had been shown a bigger purse.

"Keep your scroll, boy," the captain said. "We'll take the reward for the kid."

Zuko's face changed.

It was only a small movement, but Jake felt it in the back of his own neck. The air from the blockade bridge came over the shore, smoke and held stillness, the half-second before a volley. He took his hands out of his coat.

"You'll regret," Zuko said very softly, "breaking a deal with me."

He moved before the last word fell. A sweep of his arm, and a roar of orange light split the damp air. The pirates flung themselves apart. Flame struck the mud where the thin one had been standing, and a column of steam leapt up from the wet shingle with a long hiss, and then the soldiers were running and the whole shore broke open at once.

Jake went with it. He had trained for exactly this: three soldiers in cloaks over their armor, a prince in the center, a dozen angles of attack. He shouted a single word over the noise, *left*, and the soldier nearest him swung wide to take the reeds. A man in a striped vest came down the slope with a boarding axe and met the soldier's spear.

Somewhere in the confusion a pirate flung something small and round against a stone. It cracked, and gray smoke billowed outward. A second went off, then a third. Within a few breaths the shingle had vanished into a stinging fog that tasted of sulfur. Jake lost the ship. He lost the jetty. He found Zuko by the sound of him, the dull crackle of fire flung into smoke, and stepped into the gap at the prince's back. Their shoulders met, and held.

Neither said anything. They had not needed to for a long time.

"Right," Zuko said, short and low. A pirate lunged out of the haze on that side, and a ribbon of flame took the man's blade at the hilt. Jake turned his shoulder a hand's breadth and was already facing the next, a stocky pirate with a hooked cutlass. Jake did not draw the knife. He let the man come in, side-stepped the first cut, and caught the second on his forearm. He kept his feet.

Behind his back, Zuko pivoted to his left, to the side where the scar had taken most of the light from his eye. A shadow was moving there. Jake saw it a half-second before he saw the blade: a big man, a heavy, curved sword raised high in both hands, coming out of the smoke on the prince's blind side with the whole weight of his body behind it.

There was no time to shout. The river lay six paces off, and Jake did not decide, because there was nothing to decide.

He dropped his weight. His feet snapped in, narrow and forward, left hand low and right hand high, a stance that had come to him through ten thousand hours on a palace floor and Iroh's patient corrections on a ship's deck. He pulled.

The river answered.

Water rose off the shallows in a single dense strand and came to him through the smoke like a thrown cord. He did not swing it in a wide arc. He cut it short and tight along a straight line, and the end of it cracked across the big man's wrists as the sword came down. The blade jerked and slid sideways, off Zuko's shoulder by a hand's width, and buried itself in the mud. Jake's right hand snapped back, and the water folded and shot forward again, and the man went off his feet into the reeds.

Pain came up his arms in a white sheet, and he kept his hands open. The left one would not close completely, so he did not ask it to. When a second pirate rushed him, he turned his forearm into the man's guard and stepped inside, whipped a thin lash of water across his knees, and watched the legs go out from under him. Another burst. Another half-step. Pressure, release, reposition.

The river spun about him in quick tight coils, never longer than he needed. Zuko's fire flared and died on his other side. A pirate yelled. A shape fell. For a handful of heartbeats the shore was only the two of them, back to back, a wall with no gap.

Then the smoke tore.

A gust off the river pulled it apart, and the jetty swung into view. The pirate ship had slipped its moorings. Its stern was already drifting out into the current, and at the rail stood the girl in blue.

She had cut herself free. There was still a length of rope hanging off her left wrist. The water had come up off the deck in a thin hard line and gone through it while every man on the shore was looking somewhere else. The boy with the shaved sides was beside her, the boomerang back in his fist.

She stood with her feet planted wide and her arms thrown out in front of her. Under the keel the river gathered itself and heaved, a great smooth swell of water pushing the heavy hull out into the channel. It was the same long rolling motion he had watched in the woods, only vast now, her whole body leaning into the push and giving it back.

She turned her head.

It was not a choice. She looked back as someone looks back at a shout, and her eyes crossed the smoky shore and landed on him. On a boy in a plain coat, with a flash of Fire Nation red at the lining where the wool swung open, standing in a tight narrow stance with a rope of river water coiled around his bandaged hands.

Her arms faltered.

He saw her mouth open. He saw her see the water, and then his hands, and then the water again, and the thought crossing her face one piece at a time. It was not fear. It was something stranger and more naked — the look of someone who has been walking in the dark and put a foot down on a step that was not there. Her eyes went to the shape his arms were making, tight and straight and wrong, and for one second they filled with something that had no name in any language he knew.

He did not drop the water. He did not know how. It hung off his hands in the sunlight, shining, and she stared at it.

They looked at each other across the churning river, and the whole noise of the brawl went away.

*She knows,* Jake thought, very clearly. He did not know her name. He could not have said what she was thinking. But he knew that there was no unseeing it, and that whatever he had been, anonymous and safe and uninteresting, he would not be that again. Cold went through him from the inside out.

Then the smoke rolled back over the water, and she was gone.

"The ship!" Zuko shouted. "They've got the ship!"

"Indeed they have," said Iroh from the slope above them. "Rather a nice one, too."

Zuko turned and saw it. For a moment his face was blank, and then, to Jake's astonishment, he laughed. It was a short, harsh bark with no humor in it, the laugh of a man who could not believe the size of the joke. The pirates had stopped fighting. Half of them were already scrambling up the bank toward the little dark-timber launch. A pair of them shoved the boat into the shallows, threw themselves aboard, and began bailing the water with their hands to put it afloat.

"Hey!" Zuko said. His voice cracked. "That's my boat!"

Nobody answered. The little launch swung out from the bank, its stolen crew hauling at the oars, and set off down the river after the pirate ship. They drew out into the middle of the stream and diminished together into the gray, two shapes in a line. Far below, a long, low sound had begun to rise above the water, a low continuous roar that Jake had been hearing for some time without knowing it.

Zuko was already running. Jake watched the last of the two sails slide from sight around the bend, and heard the falls swallow it.

---
The river he had been holding fell out of the air and ran back to the shallows.

Jake had not noticed he was still bending until the last of it slid off his hands. It came away from the gloves in a long dull trickle, and the strain went with it. His knees told him first. Then the arms, then the hands, hot and swollen and beating like second hearts.

He stood on the shingle with his boots in the mud and let himself be still.

The shore had been scraped raw. A long burnt scar ran across the shingle where Zuko's fire had struck the wet stones. Only a heavy sword remained, buried to the hilt where it had missed the prince. Jake looked at it. He did not touch it.

His coat was wet through along the left arm.

Downriver, the roar had grown. It had been a murmur when they came up the path and a rumble when the pirate ship cleared the jetty, and now it was everything, a single deep unbroken note that Jake felt in the soles of his feet. Beyond the bend the river simply stopped. Where it stopped there was only air, and white spray hanging in the air.

Zuko was at the edge of it.

Jake could see him where the bank rose into a spur of bare gray rock above the lip, a dark shape against the shine, with one arm stretched toward the water as though he could close his fist around the current. The soldiers had stopped well short of him. Nobody was going to stand next to the prince just now.

Jake started walking. It was slower than he liked.

The stones shifted under his boots. By the time he reached the spur his breath had come back and the pain had gone from a shout to a long hard hum. The river poured over the lip in a broad bright sheet. Down at the foot of it the water broke into white and boiled in a pool ringed with black rocks. Fragments of timber turned in the foam. Among them lay the little dark launch, bottom up, one oar still twisting in the eddies.

Zuko made a noise. It was not a word.

"That's—" He had both hands out at his sides, fingers spread, and nothing in them. "That was *my boat.*"

The pirate ship was gone. Jake looked for it a long time, and found only a splintered stretch of rail floating near the far bank. And then, past the mist, over the tops of the trees at the far side of the gorge, something rose.

He would have missed it if he had been looking anywhere else. It was white against the pale sky, vast and slow. It lifted out of the haze, higher than the treetops and then higher than the spray. As it banked away along the cliff he saw small shapes huddled on the broad back. One of them, very small, was standing up to wave.

Then the bison turned north, and the trees took it.

Jake did not say anything. Zuko had not turned his head. He stood with his boots on the very lip of the rock and watched the place in the sky where the creature had been, and the shoulders under the hood were shaking in a way that was not quite anger.

"Prince Zuko."

Iroh had come up the slope. He walked with his hands tucked into the sleeves of his robe. He stopped beside Jake, looked down at the broken launch, and nodded a little sadly, as though acknowledging a friend.

"Prince Zuko," he said again. "You are really going to get a kick out of this."

Zuko wheeled on him. "Not now."

"Oh, but I think you will." Iroh drew one hand from his sleeve and then the other. He reached into the left sleeve and brought out a small white tile. "That lotus tile," he said, "was in my sleeve the whole time!"

He held it up between two fingers, and smiled the smile of a man who had done something clever.

Nobody spoke. The falls thundered on. Jake looked at the tile because there was nothing else to look at that would not cost him something.

Zuko crossed the rock in three strides. He snatched the tile from Iroh's fingers, his hand shaking so badly that the little white flower trembled. Then he turned, drew back his arm, and hurled it out over the edge with everything he had.

It flashed once in the spray and was gone. Jake did not hear it land.

"Nephew," Iroh said mildly, "that was my best tile."

"It's *your* tile." Zuko's voice came raw out of his chest. "We've lost the boat and the pirates and the—" He stopped. Then he turned from the edge and walked off the rock, down the slope toward the trees.

He passed Jake without a glance. Jake had known he would. Zuko had looked straight through him at the trees. He had not seen. Whatever had happened in the smoke between a boy on the shore and a girl at a rail, it had passed Zuko by entirely, and Jake could not tell yet whether that was luck or something worse.

Iroh stayed where he was. He was looking at Jake.

It was a short look. It took in the wet coat and the torn left glove. Iroh's face did not change. But Jake saw the eyes go to the river, then to the bend, then to the empty sky over the gorge, and then come back to him, and he understood that Iroh had been standing on the slope for the whole of the fight.

Iroh said nothing at all. He gave a small nod and turned to follow the prince.

Jake stayed.

The falls roared on. He watched the white water pour into the gorge. The sound was too large to think under, and he stood with it filling his head and tried to think anyway.

*She saw.*

He worked it through slowly. The girl had seen him, and the girl would tell. She would tell the boy with the shaved sides, and they would put it together as people did. A waterbender, in a Fire Nation coat, standing at the prince's back. That was the whole of what they knew, and it was enough.

They would not be afraid of him. That was almost the strangest part. It was not the Avatar he was afraid of.

It was Zhao.

Zhao, who had stood on the dock at Ketu Harbor and sneered at a *disgraced servant boy* and thought him nothing. Zhao, with his fleet across the border waters, was out there now searching the same woods and the same coast. When a man like that was taken he talked, or his friends did, or someone was made to. *The prince has a waterbender.* Zhao would hear it. He would stop and think. Then he would remember the thin boy on the dock who had said nothing at all.

And everything Iroh had built since the Agni Kai, *servant* and not *bender*, *burned boy* and not *threat*, would come down at once.

He would not be exiled again. They did not exile you twice.

Jake lifted his hands and looked at them. The right glove was dark with river water. The left was dark to the wrist as well, but the wetness was river water and the stale seep of the wound from the night before, not new blood. His fingers had begun to shake very slightly. He pressed them together and the shaking stopped, or he made it look as though it had.

He had survived. A boy had come at the prince's back with a sword, and the boy had gone into the reeds, and Zuko had not been touched. Jake had done what he was for.

He felt like a ghost who had been seen.

On the slope below, the trees closed around two figures walking away. Neither had looked back. Jake turned from the water and went after them, down through the mud and the broken reeds, and he fell into his place a half-step behind the prince's left shoulder without being told.

It was the same place. It felt as though it had moved.

*Above the river valley, late afternoon.*

The wind off the gorge had a bite in it, and Katara pulled her parka's fur collar up around her ears.

Appa flew steadily. Sky and cloud and the tops of far hills slid by on either side, and behind them the roar of the falls had dwindled to a murmur and then to nothing. On Appa's neck Aang sat with the reins loose in his hands, glancing back at her over his shoulder every few moments.

He did it again now. She caught his eye.

"Aang," she said. "Yeah?"

She had been rehearsing it since the pirate ship, and none of it came out in the order she had planned. She moved forward until she was close enough to touch his shoulder.

"I'm sorry. About the scroll. About the whole thing." She made herself keep looking at him. "I was jealous. You were picking it up so fast, and I was the one who wanted it so badly, so I said other things instead. Things I shouldn't have. You didn't deserve it."

Aang's face went through several expressions and settled on the open one he wore when he was most himself.

"I wasn't even mad."

"You should have been."

"Maybe a little." He grinned, his bruised cheek making it lopsided. "But you came back for me. That counts for more."

She laughed, a little shakily, and put her arm around his shoulders. He leaned into it as if it were the most natural thing in the world, and for a moment the sky was only sky.

"Ahem," said Sokka. He had been quiet at the back of the saddle for so long she should have noticed. Now he sat up like a man who had waited for the right dramatic moment.

"If you two are done," he said, "I have something."

He reached inside his coat and drew out a roll of yellowed parchment, bound with a faded red cord.

Katara stared at it. For a second her mind refused to put the pieces together. Then she was across the saddle, both hands out.

"The scroll!"

"Don't tear it," Sokka said, and let her take it. "I grabbed it off the pirate captain's belt while he was busy being betrayed. Skill. Stealth. Unbelievable grace."

The parchment was dry and fragile, with a faint scorch along one edge where the fire had come close. The painted figures down its length had not been touched. She turned it toward the light and saw a woman with her hands raised, a ribbon of water between them.

"Thank you," she said. "Really."

Aang laughed, and Appa let out a long rumbling groan that might have been agreement, and for a few breaths the saddle was warm and theirs.

Then Katara looked down at the scroll in her hands, and her mind slid away.

She could see the river as plainly as if she were still standing on that ship's deck. Brown water, black mud, the white-hot flash of Zuko's fire on the shore. And in a gap in the smoke, a boy.

She had not known she was going to say it.

"Did either of you see him?"

Sokka's brows went up. "See who?"

"Him." Her voice came out smaller than she meant. "On the shore. At the end. Not Zuko. The other one."

Neither of them said anything. Aang had turned around on Appa's neck, the reins forgotten in his lap.

"There was a boy," Katara said, slowly, trying to find the right shape for it. "A little taller than me. Dark hair. A dark coat with red lining. He was standing right behind Zuko, and he had..." She stopped. "He was bending, Sokka."

"Bending what?"

"The river."

The wind went past them in a long thin note. Sokka let the silence sit a second before he sat up straighter.

"No," he said. "A waterbender. Working for the Fire Nation. We left the South Pole because there weren't any waterbenders left. You're the last one. That's why we're going north, to find a master. That's the entire plan."

"I know what the plan is."

"So there's no way there's some other waterbender standing next to Zuko."

"He wasn't throwing water around," Katara said. "He was fighting."

Sokka's mouth shut.

"I watched him," she said. "The whole time I was pushing the ship out. I looked back, and there he was. He pulled a whole stream off the shallows with his hands and he didn't swing it. He didn't roll it. It went in straight lines. Like a whip. Like..." She could not think of the word. "Like a fire strike. He had his feet together, almost, and his arms were so tight. Everything was sharp. It didn't *flow.*"

"So he's a bad waterbender," Sokka offered.

"No. He's good." She shook her head. "That's what's wrong. He was really good. And it didn't look like anything I've ever seen."

Aang had been very still. "Maybe someone taught him," he said carefully. "Somewhere else."

"Who?" said Katara. "Who would teach him? I'm the only waterbender I know, Sokka. That's why we're going north." Her voice went down. "And then I look up and there's another one, close to my age, standing next to the one person who..."

"He might not be a bad guy," Aang said.

"He's with Zuko."

"I know. But he looked..." Aang's forehead creased. "Did he look scared to you?"

"I don't know what he looked like," Katara said. She did. She had seen his face for three heartbeats through a rip in the smoke, and it was fixed in her head like a carving. A pale, tired face, soft in the jaw. He had not looked triumphant. He had looked, of all the things she could have imagined, caught.

His hands had been wrapped. Under the gloves, ragged ones, something had been wrong with them, and the water had circled in and out of his fingers without his flinching once.

She touched her own hands and tried to make them move the way his had. Straight, short, hard. The water in her mind went the wrong way.

Her fingers drifted to her throat and found nothing. The place where her mother's necklace had hung was bare skin.

"We should tell no one," Sokka said, in a low voice. "Not until we figure out what we're dealing with."

"There's no one to tell," said Aang.

"Right. Good."

Katara looked down at the scroll in her lap. The woman on it stared up at her with her hands lifted and the water curling between them, patient and graceful and very old. She knew what that kind of bending looked like. It looked like her grandmother's stories. It did not look like a boy in a Fire Nation coat with every line of him drawn tight as a bowstring.

*Who taught you?* she thought, over the water and the miles.

Appa banked gently, and the sun swung across the saddle. The river dropped away behind them, narrow and bright. The wind was cold on her face. She held the scroll against her chest.

Nobody spoke for a long time, and the bison carried them north.
